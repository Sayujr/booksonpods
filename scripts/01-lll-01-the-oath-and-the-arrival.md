# Love's Labour's Lost 1. The Oath and the Arrival

*The recorded audio uses an earlier version of the opening lines.*

**Host A:** Welcome to Books on Pods. Today we begin Love's Labour's Lost, with two scenes. In the first, four young men swear a ridiculous oath. In the second, four women arrive and start taking that oath apart.

**Host B:** Which already sounds like an oath with a short life expectancy.

**Host A:** Very short. So let's set the stage. We are in Navarre, a small kingdom between France and Spain. The young king, Ferdinand, has three friends at court. Longaville, Dumaine, and Berowne. Some editions spell that last name Biron, but it is the same man.

**Host B:** And the king has a plan.

**Host A:** He does. He wants to turn his court into a kind of academy, a place devoted to study and philosophy. And he opens the play with a big, grand speech about fame and eternity. Here is the heart of it.

**King:** Navarre shall be the wonder of the world; Our court shall be a little Academe, Still and contemplative in living art.

**Host B:** A little Academe. That is a nod to Plato's Academy in Athens. He is imagining himself as a philosopher king.

**Host A:** Right, and he is very young, and very serious about it. He tells his friends that they have already sworn to stay with him for three years as fellow scholars, and now they have to sign their names to the rules.

**Host B:** So what are the rules?

**Host A:** We find out through Berowne, because Berowne is the one who actually reads the fine print, and he does not like it. Listen to how he lists them.

**Berowne:** But there are other strict observances; As, not to see a woman in that term, Which I hope well is not enrolled there; And one day in a week to touch no food And but one meal on every day beside, The which I hope is not enrolled there; And then, to sleep but three hours in the night, And not be seen to wink of all the day.

**Berowne:** O, these are barren tasks, too hard to keep, Not to see ladies, study, fast, not sleep!

**Host B:** I love the repeated line. Which I hope well is not enrolled there. He keeps saying it, and you can hear that he knows perfectly well it is enrolled there.

**Host A:** That repetition is the joke. And the final couplet is almost a little song of misery. Not to see ladies, study, fast, not sleep. It rhymes, it bounces, and it makes the whole project sound absurd.

**Host B:** So no women, one meal a day, fasting once a week, three hours of sleep. That is the oath.

**Host A:** That is the oath. And then Berowne goes further. He starts arguing that this kind of study does not even work. He has a famous line about reading so much that you go blind.

**Berowne:** Light seeking light doth light of light beguile: So, ere you find where light in darkness lies, Your light grows dark by losing of your eyes.

**Host B:** That line is almost a tongue twister. Light seeking light doth light of light beguile.

**Host A:** It is, and it is doing something clever. The first light is your eyes. The second light is truth, the thing you are looking for. And what he is saying is that if you stare at books hunting for truth, you wear out your eyesight before you find it. The wordplay is the argument.

**Host B:** And the king notices the irony.

**King:** How well he's read, to reason against reading!

**Host A:** That is the perfect response. Berowne has just made a very learned speech about why learning is overrated. He is the most bookish person in the room, arguing against books.

**Host B:** So does he refuse to sign?

**Host A:** No. And this is one of the most human moments in the scene. After all that resistance, he gives in.

**Berowne:** Give me the paper; let me read the same; And to the strict'st decrees I'll write my name.

**Host B:** So he complains, he mocks it, he argues brilliantly against it, and then he signs anyway.

**Host A:** Because he does not want to be left out. And then comes the twist. He reads one of the rules out loud, the rule that no woman may come within a mile of the court. And he points out a problem that the king has completely forgotten.

**Berowne:** This article, my liege, yourself must break; For well you know here comes in embassy The French king's daughter with yourself to speak.

**King:** What say you, lords? Why, this was quite forgot.

**Host B:** Quite forgot. The Princess of France is coming on official business, and the king just forgot.

**Host A:** And Berowne gets the last word on that. He says that all this study made them forget the one practical thing that mattered. And then he makes a prediction.

**Berowne:** Necessity will make us all forsworn Three thousand times within this three years' space.

**Host A:** Forsworn means having broken your oath. So within minutes of signing, Berowne is already saying they will break it three thousand times.

**Host B:** So that is the first scene. A grand ideal, a list of impossible rules, one sceptic who signs anyway, and a visitor already on the road.

**Host A:** Now let's jump to the second act, where she arrives. The Princess comes with three ladies, Rosaline, Maria, and Katharine, and an older lord called Boyet, who serves as her adviser and messenger.

**Host B:** And Boyet starts by flattering her.

**Host A:** He does. He tells her she is the most precious woman in the world, and nature gave all its graces to her. And her response tells you immediately what kind of person she is.

**Princess:** Good Lord Boyet, my beauty, though but mean, Needs not the painted flourish of your praise: Beauty is bought by judgement of the eye, Not utter'd by base sale of chapmen's tongues.

**Host B:** Chapmen. That is an old word for traders, people selling goods at a market.

**Host A:** Yes. So she is saying, my beauty is judged by people who look at me, not by someone advertising it like a market seller. She has no patience for decorated language.

**Host B:** Which is interesting, because the men in the first scene are drowning in decorated language.

**Host A:** That is exactly the contrast the play is setting up. The men love grand speeches. The women see through them. And Folger's introduction says the play pushes back on the men's idea that women are either temptations or goddesses. Here, the women are simply sharper than the men.

**Host B:** So what happens next?

**Host A:** The Princess sends Boyet ahead to ask for a meeting, and while she waits, she asks her ladies about the men. And each lady turns out to have met one of them before. Maria knows Longaville. She says he is admired, but he has one flaw.

**Maria:** Is a sharp wit matched with too blunt a will; Whose edge hath power to cut, whose will still wills It should none spare that come within his power.

**Host B:** So he is clever, but he does not hold back. He will cut anyone with that wit.

**Host A:** Right. Katharine praises Dumaine. And then Rosaline describes Berowne, and her description is so warm that it gives her away.

**Rosaline:** His eye begets occasion for his wit; For every object that the one doth catch The other turns to a mirth-moving jest, Which his fair tongue, conceit's expositor, Delivers in such apt and gracious words That aged ears play truant at his tales And younger hearings are quite ravished; So sweet and voluble is his discourse.

**Host B:** Old people skip their duties to listen to him, and young people are completely carried away. That is not a neutral description.

**Host A:** Not at all. And the Princess notices.

**Princess:** God bless my ladies! are they all in love, That every one her own hath garnished With such bedecking ornaments of praise?

**Host B:** So the pairs are already lined up. Four men, four women, and the women have each picked one.

**Host A:** Then Boyet comes back with news. The king will not let them into the court. He is going to lodge them outside, in the fields, rather than break his oath.

**Host B:** That is quite rude. She is a princess on a diplomatic mission.

**Host A:** It is very rude, and she lets him know it. When the king arrives and says, Fair princess, welcome to the court of Navarre, she throws both words back at him.

**Princess:** 'Fair' I give you back again; and 'welcome' I have not yet: the roof of this court is too high to be yours; and welcome to the wide fields too base to be mine.

**Host B:** So, you are calling this a welcome, but you have left me standing in a field.

**Host A:** Exactly. And then the two of them talk about the oath directly. Listen to how quickly she corners him.

**King:** Hear me, dear lady; I have sworn an oath.

**Princess:** Our Lady help my lord! he'll be forsworn.

**King:** Not for the world, fair madam, by my will.

**Princess:** Why, will shall break it; will and nothing else.

**Host B:** Will in two senses. His determination, and his desire.

**Host A:** Yes, and that double meaning is the whole play in one word. He says, I will not break my oath by my will. She says, your will, your desire, is exactly what will break it. And then she gives him a real dilemma.

**Princess:** 'Tis deadly sin to keep that oath, my lord, And sin to break it.

**Host B:** Why is it a sin to keep it?

**Host A:** Because the oath forces him to treat a guest badly, to refuse hospitality, which in this world is a serious failure of honour. But breaking an oath is also a sin. So he is trapped either way, and she has pointed it out within a minute of meeting him.

**Host B:** There is also some business about money and land, isn't there?

**Host A:** There is. Her father owes or is owed a large sum, a hundred thousand crowns, connected to a region called Aquitaine. The king says the money was never paid, the Princess says it was, and the receipts will arrive tomorrow. It is the official reason for her visit, and the play mostly uses it to keep everyone together.

**Host B:** Meanwhile, Berowne and Rosaline meet again.

**Host A:** And this is one of the best exchanges in the act. It is very fast, with short lines that bounce back and forth. Scholars call this stichomythia, a duel in single lines. Listen to how Rosaline keeps turning his own words against him.

**Berowne:** Did not I dance with you in Brabant once?

**Rosaline:** Did not I dance with you in Brabant once?

**Berowne:** I know you did.

**Rosaline:** How needless was it then to ask the question!

**Berowne:** You must not be so quick.

**Rosaline:** 'Tis 'long of you that spur me with such questions.

**Berowne:** Your wit's too hot, it speeds too fast, 'twill tire.

**Rosaline:** Not till it leave the rider in the mire.

**Host B:** She just repeated his opening line back to him. Word for word.

**Host A:** That is how she wins the first point. And then the horse image. He says her wit is going too fast and will tire out. She says it will not tire until it throws the rider in the mud. And the rider is him.

**Host B:** So the man who made the most brilliant speeches in the first scene is losing.

**Host A:** He is losing badly. And he keeps trying.

**Berowne:** Now fair befall your mask!

**Rosaline:** Fair fall the face it covers!

**Berowne:** And send you many lovers!

**Rosaline:** Amen, so you be none.

**Berowne:** Nay, then will I be gone.

**Host B:** Amen, so you be none. That is brutal.

**Host A:** It is, and it is also flirting. They are both enjoying it. That is the screwball comedy energy the Folger introduction talks about.

**Host B:** And what about the other two men?

**Host A:** This is my favourite little sequence. After the meeting ends, the men leave, but then each of them sneaks back, one by one, to ask Boyet the name of a lady. Dumaine comes back first and asks about Katharine. Then Longaville.

**Longaville:** I beseech you a word: what is she in the white?

**Boyet:** A woman sometimes, an you saw her in the light.

**Host B:** Boyet is teasing him.

**Host A:** Boyet teases all of them. And then Berowne comes back too.

**Berowne:** What's her name in the cap?

**Boyet:** Rosaline, by good hap.

**Berowne:** Is she wedded or no?

**Boyet:** To her will, sir, or so.

**Host B:** Three men who swore an oath against women, all sneaking back separately to ask for names. In the same scene.

**Host A:** And none of them knows the others did it. That is a seed the play will water later. And then Boyet delivers his verdict on the king to the Princess.

**Boyet:** Navarre is infected.

**Princess:** With what?

**Boyet:** With that which we lovers entitle affected.

**Host A:** Affected meaning in love. Boyet says he watched the king's eyes the whole time, and the king could not stop staring at her. He even jokes that she could have Aquitaine for a single kiss.

**Host B:** So, to pull this together. What is the big idea of these two scenes?

**Host A:** I would put it like this. The men build a wall out of words. A grand speech, a signed oath, rules about fasting and sleep. And the moment real people arrive, the wall starts cracking. The women do not even need to try very hard. They just answer the men's words with sharper words.

**Host B:** And a director at the University of Washington described it that way too, didn't he? That these men build sanctuaries out of language.

**Host A:** He did. He said the play is about broken vows, false declarations, and wordplay, and that we build protective sanctuaries out of words that can both guard us and destroy us. That is exactly what we have heard in these two scenes.

**Host B:** So here is something to try. Say the Princess's line out loud, slowly. 'Tis deadly sin to keep that oath, my lord, and sin to break it.

**Host A:** And decide for yourself whether she is teasing him or being completely serious. There is no single right answer. Actors play it both ways.

**Host B:** Next time, we meet the comic characters below stairs. A Spanish braggart, a clever page, and a man named Costard who gets caught breaking the rules first.

**Host A:** See you then.
