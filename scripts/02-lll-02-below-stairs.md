# Love's Labour's Lost 2. Below Stairs

**Host A:** Welcome back to Books on Pods. This is episode two of Love's Labour's Lost. Last time, four young lords swore to study for three years and stay away from women, and then the Princess of France arrived with three ladies and started dismantling that oath one witty line at a time.

**Host B:** And today we go downstairs. We meet the people below the lords. A Spanish knight who is in love with his own vocabulary, his tiny and very sharp page, a country clown, and a dairy maid. We cover Act One, Scene Two, which we skipped last time, and all of Act Three.

**Host A:** And Act Three ends with one of the great comic speeches in Shakespeare, so we are going to build up to that.

**Host B:** Let's start with the Spaniard. His full name is Don Adriano de Armado.

**Host A:** And for an English audience in the 1590s, that name would have landed with a thud. Armado sounds like Armada, the Spanish fleet that tried to invade England in 1588 and was defeated. So a few years later, here is a Spaniard on stage named after a famous failure, strutting around a foreign court and talking very grandly.

**Host B:** So he is a joke before he even opens his mouth.

**Host A:** He is. He also comes from an old stage type, the braggart soldier. You find him in Roman comedy and in Italian commedia dell'arte. A soldier who boasts about his courage, talks in an inflated style, and turns out to be all show.

**Host B:** And his page is called Moth.

**Host A:** Yes, and here is a lovely detail. In Shakespeare's pronunciation, Moth probably sounded like mote, as in a mote of dust, a tiny speck. The scholar Marjorie Garber notes that the name points to his small size. He was played by a boy, and he is the smallest person on stage and easily the cleverest.

**Host B:** So let's hear them. This is the first thing Armado says in the play.

**Armado:** Boy, what sign is it when a man of great spirit grows melancholy?

**Moth:** A great sign, sir, that he will look sad.

**Host B:** That is such a deflating answer.

**Host A:** It is the whole relationship in two lines. Armado asks a grand, philosophical question about men of great spirit, and Moth answers like a child stating the obvious. If you are sad, you look sad. And it keeps going like that. Listen to them try to do some simple arithmetic. Moth has asked his master how much one plus two is.

**Armado:** It doth amount to one more than two.

**Moth:** Which the base vulgar do call three.

**Armado:** True.

**Host B:** He will not say the word three.

**Host A:** He cannot bring himself to say it. Three is a word for ordinary people. So he says one more than two, which is longer and grander and means exactly the same thing. And then Moth makes a joke about the whole oath. He says you can study three years in two words, just say the words three years and you are done. And Armado, who does not quite follow, says this.

**Armado:** A most fine figure!

**Moth:** To prove you a cipher.

**Host B:** A cipher is a zero.

**Host A:** Exactly. A figure is a figure of speech, and also a number. And Moth says the figure proves you are a zero. A nobody. He insults his master right to his face, and his master takes it as a compliment.

**Host B:** So where is this going? Why does Armado need comforting?

**Host A:** Because he has a secret. And it connects directly to the oath in the first scene. Listen.

**Armado:** I will hereupon confess I am in love: and as it is base for a soldier to love, so am I in love with a base wench.

**Host B:** Base wench meaning a lower class woman.

**Host A:** Yes. The girl is Jaquenetta, a dairy maid. And Armado is horrified at himself, so he asks Moth to name great men who have been in love, to make himself feel better. Moth says Hercules. Armado loves that. Then Moth says Samson, and Armado is thrilled again.

**Host B:** Hercules and Samson. Two men famous for strength, both brought low by love.

**Host A:** Which is not exactly comforting, if you think about it. But Armado does not think about it. Now here is the part I find really interesting, because it is where the critics come in. Why put this scene right after the oath scene? William Carroll, who wrote the modern introduction for the Folger edition, argues that Jaquenetta's story mirrors the lords' story. The king has banned desire. And what happens the moment desire is banned? Everyone wants it more. The servants and the clown are already breaking the rule in the very first act, before the lords even have a chance.

**Host B:** So the subplot is a kind of preview.

**Host A:** It is a preview, and a mirror. The lords are going to go through exactly what Armado is going through, just in better clothes and better verse.

**Host B:** Let's meet Jaquenetta then.

**Host A:** She has very few lines, but they are perfect. Armado tries to woo her in his grand style, and she gives him nothing back.

**Armado:** I will tell thee wonders.

**Jaquenetta:** With that face?

**Armado:** I love thee.

**Jaquenetta:** So I heard you say.

**Host B:** With that face. Oh, that is cruel.

**Host A:** And notice the pattern. Last episode, Rosaline cut Berowne down with short, sharp answers. Here, a dairy maid does the same thing to a Spanish knight. In this play, the women, high and low, do not fall for big words.

**Host B:** What happens to Costard? He was arrested at the end of the first scene, wasn't he?

**Host A:** He was. Costard is the clown who got caught with Jaquenetta, breaking the king's new rule on day one. Costard is also the name of a kind of apple, a big round country apple, which tells you something about him. The king's punishment is that he has to fast, and he is put in Armado's custody. And when Armado threatens him, Costard turns every threat into a joke.

**Armado:** Thou shalt be heavily punished.

**Costard:** I am more bound to you than your fellows, for they are but lightly rewarded.

**Host B:** So he takes heavily punished and twists it into heavily rewarded.

**Host A:** Right. Costard is not educated, but he plays with words as much as anyone. Then everyone leaves, and Armado is alone on stage. And he gives this wonderful, ridiculous speech about being in love. I want to hear a good chunk of it.

**Armado:** I do affect the very ground, which is base, where her shoe, which is baser, guided by her foot, which is basest, doth tread.

**Armado:** Love is a familiar; Love is a devil: there is no evil angel but Love. Yet was Samson so tempted, and he had an excellent strength; yet was Solomon so seduced, and he had a very good wit.

**Armado:** Adieu, valour! rust rapier! be still, drum! for your manager is in love; yea, he loveth. Assist me, some extemporal god of rhyme, for I am sure I shall turn sonnet. Devise, wit; write, pen; for I am for whole volumes in folio.

**Host B:** Whole volumes in folio. He is going to write a library.

**Host A:** A folio was the biggest, grandest kind of book. So he is not going to write a love note. He is going to write encyclopedias. And listen to that first line again. Base, baser, basest. The ground is base, her shoe is baser, her foot is basest. He is trying to praise her, and his own grammar keeps calling her lower and lower.

**Host B:** So what do critics make of Armado? Is he just a clown?

**Host A:** There are a few views. Meredith Skura argues that Shakespeare uses Armado and Costard to parody the whole idea of heroic honour and fame. Remember, the king opened the play with a big speech about fame living on after death. Armado is that idea in a funny costume. He wants glory, he talks about Hercules and Samson, and he cannot even say the word three.

**Host B:** So he is a cartoon of the king.

**Host A:** In a way. And the Victorian critic Walter Pater had a lovely phrase for him. Pater called him, quote, the extravagant but polished caricature of Armado. Polished is the important word there. He is a caricature, but he is a well made one. Pater thought Armado was a kind of exaggerated version of the same fancy style that Berowne uses, just pushed too far.

**Host B:** And actors?

**Host A:** On film, in Kenneth Branagh's 2000 film, which turns the play into a 1930s musical, Armado is played by Timothy Spall. One reviewer said Spall was actually quite good in the part, though the accent, quote, strains comprehension. Costard in that film is Nathan Lane, playing him like an old vaudeville comic.

**Host B:** Let's go to Act Three.

**Host A:** Act Three opens with Armado asking Moth to sing for him, to make him feel his love even more. And then Moth gives his master a lesson in how to seduce someone. It is basically a parody of how fashionable young men behaved. Here is part of it.

**Moth:** To jig off a tune at the tongue's end, canary to it with your feet, humour it with turning up your eyelids, sigh a note and sing a note, sometime through the throat, as if you swallowed love with singing love, sometime through the nose, as if you snuffed up love by smelling love.

**Moth:** With your hat penthouse-like o'er the shop of your eyes; with your arms crossed on your thin-belly doublet like a rabbit on a spit.

**Host B:** A rabbit on a spit. That is the lovesick pose. Arms folded, looking miserable.

**Host A:** And Moth says these are the tricks that catch women. A small boy, mocking adult romance, and he is completely right. Armado asks him where he learned all this, and Moth says, by my penny of observation. Pater called him, quote, Moth or Mote the elfin page. Elfin is right. He is a little spirit of mockery.

**Host B:** Then there is the bit about lead.

**Host A:** Yes, my favourite exchange. Armado tells Moth to go fetch Costard. Moth says he will go as swift as lead. Armado thinks he has caught Moth out.

**Armado:** Is not lead a metal heavy, dull, and slow?

**Moth:** Minime, honest master; or rather, master, no. You are too swift, sir, to say so: Is that lead slow which is fired from a gun?

**Armado:** Sweet smoke of rhetoric!

**Host B:** A bullet is lead, and a bullet is very fast.

**Host A:** Exactly. And Armado's response, sweet smoke of rhetoric, is such a great phrase that reviewers keep borrowing it. A theatre review of the Royal Shakespeare Company's production used it as its title. Because that is what the whole play is. Sweet smoke of rhetoric. Beautiful, and you cannot hold it.

**Host B:** So Moth brings back Costard.

**Host A:** He does, and Armado decides to use Costard as a messenger. He sets Costard free, and in return Costard has to carry a love letter to Jaquenetta. And Armado tips him. He hands him a coin and says, there is remuneration. And Costard has never heard that word before. Listen to what he does with it.

**Costard:** Now will I look to his remuneration. Remuneration! O, that's the Latin word for three farthings: three farthings, remuneration. 'What's the price of this inkle?' 'One penny.' 'No, I'll give you a remuneration:' why, it carries it. Remuneration! why, it is a fairer name than French crown. I will never buy and sell out of this word.

**Host B:** He thinks remuneration is the name of a coin.

**Host A:** He thinks it is the name of a coin worth three farthings, which is less than a penny. And he loves the word. He imagines going into a shop and offering a remuneration, and he thinks it sounds so much better than money. And then, the timing is perfect, Berowne walks in.

**Host B:** And Berowne also needs a messenger.

**Host A:** He does. Berowne has written a letter to Rosaline. Yes, the lord who said they would all be forsworn three thousand times is now sending secret love letters. He hands Costard the letter and a tip, and Berowne calls his tip a guerdon, another fancy word for a reward.

**Costard:** Gardon, O sweet gardon! better than remuneration, a'leven-pence farthing better: most sweet gardon! I will do it sir, in print. Gardon! Remuneration!

**Host B:** So guerdon turned out to be worth a lot more.

**Host A:** Eleven pence farthing more, he says. Berowne gave him a shilling. And the joke is that Costard now judges words by how much money comes with them. A word is worth whatever someone pays you when they say it. That is a very funny, very sharp idea in a play where everyone is obsessed with fancy words. William Carroll wrote a whole book on this play called The Great Feast of Language, and this little scene shows why the title fits. Words in this play are food, fashion, and here, quite literally, money.

**Host B:** And notice what has just happened in the plot. Two letters. One from Armado to Jaquenetta. One from Berowne to Rosaline. Both handed to Costard, who cannot read.

**Host A:** Hold that thought. We will come back to it next episode. Now, after Costard leaves, Berowne is alone, and he gives the speech we have been building to. He has spent the whole play so far mocking love. And now he has to admit it.

**Berowne:** And I, forsooth, in love! I, that have been love's whip; A very beadle to a humorous sigh; A critic, nay, a night-watch constable; A domineering pedant o'er the boy; Than whom no mortal so magnificent!

**Host B:** A beadle was a kind of officer who punished people.

**Host A:** Yes. He says, I used to be love's police officer. I whipped lovers. I was a critic of every sigh. And the boy he means is Cupid. He says he used to boss Cupid around like a schoolteacher. Now listen to how he describes Cupid.

**Berowne:** This whimpled, whining, purblind, wayward boy; This senior-junior, giant-dwarf, Dan Cupid; Regent of love-rhymes, lord of folded arms, The anointed sovereign of sighs and groans, Liege of all loiterers and malcontents.

**Host B:** Lord of folded arms. That is Moth's rabbit on a spit.

**Host A:** It is exactly that pose. Folded arms, sighing. And senior junior, giant dwarf. Cupid is a baby who is also the oldest god. A tiny thing with huge power. Berowne cannot decide whether to mock him or bow to him, so he does both at once. Then he turns to the woman herself, and it gets more complicated.

**Berowne:** What, I! I love! I sue! I seek a wife! A woman, that is like a German clock, Still a-repairing, ever out of frame, And never going aright, being a watch, But being watch'd that it may still go right!

**Host B:** A German clock. That is a strange compliment.

**Host A:** German clocks in this period were complicated, elaborate machines that constantly broke down. So he says a woman is like a clock that always needs repairs and never keeps proper time unless you watch it all the time. It is a joke, and it is not a kind one. But notice he is still saying, I seek a wife. He is joking his way into a confession.

**Host B:** And then he describes Rosaline.

**Berowne:** A wightly wanton with a velvet brow, With two pitch-balls stuck in her face for eyes; Ay, and by heaven, one that will do the deed Though Argus were her eunuch and her guard.

**Host A:** Pitch is the black tar used to seal ships. So he says she has eyes like two balls of tar. Rosaline is dark, and the play comes back to her darkness again and again. This is where critics have gone in some interesting directions. The scholar Robin Williams, writing on his blog I Read Shakespeare, argues that we should read Rosaline as a Black woman, and that calling her darkness only a metaphor or a convention is an evasion of what the text says. Others have wondered whether there is a link to the dark lady of Shakespeare's sonnets, the dark eyed woman the poet loves against his own judgement. The Washington State scholar Michael Delahoyde raises that as an open question rather than a conclusion.

**Host B:** And Argus?

**Host A:** Argus is the giant from Greek myth with a hundred eyes, who was set to guard a woman. Berowne is saying she would get up to mischief even with Argus watching. He is insulting her, and he is completely besotted. Both at the same time. That mix is what makes the speech so alive. And here is the ending.

**Berowne:** And I to sigh for her! to watch for her! To pray for her! Go to; it is a plague That Cupid will impose for my neglect Of his almighty dreadful little might. Well, I will love, write, sigh, pray, sue and groan: Some men must love my lady and some Joan.

**Host B:** Almighty dreadful little might. That is a lovely phrase.

**Host A:** It sums up Cupid perfectly. And the last line. Some men must love my lady, and some Joan. Joan was the standard name for a country girl, a common woman. He means, some men love great ladies, some love simple girls, and either way, everyone loves someone.

**Host B:** Which takes us right back to Armado and his dairy maid.

**Host A:** Exactly. The lord and the braggart are in the same position. Both swore off love, both have fallen, both have written letters, and both have given their letters to the same clown. Shakespeare has built the two plots so they rhyme.

**Host B:** What do critics say about Berowne at this moment?

**Host A:** Walter Pater had a famous idea about him. He suggested that in Berowne we can see, quote, a reflex of Shakespeare himself. A reflection of the young author. A clever young man who loves words, mocks fashionable love poetry, and cannot stop writing it. And Pater said that below all of Berowne's verbal tricks we can sometimes hear, quote, the unutterable longing. A real feeling under the fireworks. Though Pater also said that even at his best, Berowne is, quote, still chargeable with just a little affectation.

**Host B:** That sounds right. He means it, and he is also performing it.

**Host A:** And another critic, MacCary, traces a path through Berowne in this play, from loving an idealised woman to recognising women as real people who are unpredictable. This speech is near the start of that path. He has noticed that Rosaline is not a goddess on a pedestal. She is a sharp, dark eyed woman who beat him in a battle of wits. And he still loves her. In fact, he loves her because of it.

**Host B:** So, to pull it all together. What is the big idea of this episode?

**Host A:** I would put it like this. The comic characters below stairs are not a separate story. They are a mirror. Armado talks the way the lords talk, only worse. Moth mocks love the way Berowne mocked it, only smaller. Costard takes fancy words and asks what they are worth in coins. And by the end of Act Three, Berowne, the cleverest of the lords, has landed in the same place as the silliest man in the play.

**Host B:** In love, writing letters, and trusting them to Costard.

**Host A:** Which is never a good plan.

**Host B:** Here is something to try. Say Armado's line out loud: I do affect the very ground, which is base, where her shoe, which is baser, guided by her foot, which is basest, doth tread. Then say Berowne's line: Well, I will love, write, sigh, pray, sue and groan. Some men must love my lady and some Joan.

**Host A:** And ask yourself which of the two men sounds more foolish, and which one sounds more honest. I am not sure the answer is the obvious one.

**Host B:** Next time, letters go astray. The Princess goes hunting, a schoolmaster and a curate show off their Latin, and Costard makes the delivery mistake you have probably already guessed.

**Host A:** See you then.
