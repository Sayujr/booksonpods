# Love's Labour's Lost 3. Letters Gone Astray

**Host A:** Welcome back to Books on Pods. We are in Act Four of Love's Labour's Lost, and if you remember where Act Three left us, there are two love letters in the hands of one clown. Armado's letter to Jaquenetta, the dairy maid. Berowne's letter to Rosaline, the lady of France. Both given to Costard.

**Host B:** Who has a very relaxed attitude to detail.

**Host A:** Very relaxed. But before Costard arrives, Act Four opens somewhere new. The Princess is out hunting deer in the king's park. She has a bow, a forester is showing her where to stand, and she immediately catches him in a bit of careless flattery. He tells her she will have the fairest shoot from this spot. And she pretends he meant that she is fair.

**Princess:** I thank my beauty, I am fair that shoot, And thereupon thou speak'st the fairest shoot.

**Host B:** And the poor forester panics.

**Host A:** He says, pardon me, madam, I did not mean it that way. And she pounces. So I am not fair? Then he says, yes, madam, fair. And she says this.

**Princess:** Nay, never paint me now: Where fair is not, praise cannot mend the brow. Here, good my glass, take this for telling true: Fair payment for foul words is more than due.

**Host B:** She tips him.

**Host A:** She tips him, and she calls him her glass, her mirror, for telling the truth. Paint here means makeup, flattering paint. She says, do not paint me with compliments now, praise cannot fix a face. And she pays him for being honest. Remember Costard in the last act, judging fancy words by the coins that came with them? Here is the Princess doing the reverse. She pays for plain words.

**Host B:** It is the same scene she played with Boyet in Act Two. She does not want to be flattered.

**Host A:** Exactly the same. And then she says something unexpectedly serious about the hunt itself. She is about to kill a deer, and she stops to think about why.

**Princess:** And out of question so it is sometimes, Glory grows guilty of detested crimes, When, for fame's sake, for praise, an outward part, We bend to that the working of the heart; As I for praise alone now seek to spill The poor deer's blood, that my heart means no ill.

**Host B:** Glory grows guilty of detested crimes.

**Host A:** It is a beautiful line, and it quietly answers the king. The very first speech of the play was the king saying, let fame, that all hunt after in their lives, live on our tombs. Fame that all hunt after. And here is the Princess, actually hunting, and saying that people do cruel things purely for fame. She kills the deer to look skilful, not because she wants it dead.

**Host B:** So she sees through the king's whole project without even talking to him.

**Host B:** And now Costard arrives.

**Host A:** Costard arrives, carrying what he believes is the letter Berowne gave him for Rosaline. And he cannot tell which lady is the Princess.

**Costard:** God dig-you-den all! Pray you, which is the head lady?

**Princess:** Thou shalt know her, fellow, by the rest that have no heads.

**Host A:** Costard then cheerfully tells her she is the thickest lady present, and she lets it pass. He says he has a letter from Monsieur Biron to one Lady Rosaline, and the Princess, calling Berowne a good friend of hers, tells Boyet to open it. Boyet looks at it, and says this.

**Boyet:** This letter is mistook, it importeth none here; It is writ to Jaquenetta.

**Princess:** We will read it, I swear.

**Host B:** There it is. Costard has handed over the wrong letter.

**Host A:** He has given the Princess's party Armado's letter to the dairy maid. And they read the whole thing out loud for fun. So we get Armado's love letter, the one he promised in Act One would fill whole volumes in folio. Boyet reads it.

**Boyet:** By heaven, that thou art fair, is most infallible; true, that thou art beauteous; truth itself, that thou art lovely. More fairer than fair, beautiful than beauteous, truer than truth itself, have commiseration on thy heroical vassal!

**Host B:** More fairer than fair. That is not even grammatical.

**Host A:** It is double grammar. More fairer. He cannot stop adding. And then he reaches for a famous story to compare himself to. The story of King Cophetua and the beggar maid.

**Host B:** That is the ballad he asked Moth about in Act One.

**Host A:** Yes, Armado asked whether there was a ballad of the King and the Beggar, and Moth said the world was guilty of such a ballad some three ages ago. It was a popular old ballad. An African king who swore he would never love any woman, until he saw a beggar girl from his window and married her. Shakespeare mentions Cophetua in several plays from the 1590s, including Romeo and Juliet and A Midsummer Night's Dream, so the audience knew it well. And the story keeps coming back. Tennyson wrote a poem on it, The Beggar Maid. Edward Burne-Jones painted King Cophetua and the Beggar Maid in 1884. And Alice Munro used The Beggar Maid as the title of a story in 1980.

**Host B:** And Armado is the king in this version.

**Host A:** Of course he is. Listen to what he does with Julius Caesar's famous phrase, I came, I saw, I conquered.

**Boyet:** The magnanimous and most illustrate king Cophetua set eye upon the pernicious and indubitate beggar Zenelophon; and he it was that might rightly say, Veni, vidi, vici; which to annothanize in the vulgar, O base and obscure vulgar! videlicet, He came, saw, and overcame: he came, one; saw two; overcame, three.

**Boyet:** Who came? the king: why did he come? to see: why did he see? to overcome: to whom came he? to the beggar: what saw he? the beggar: who overcame he? the beggar. The conclusion is victory: on whose side? the king's. The captive is enriched: on whose side? the beggar's.

**Host B:** He is diagramming a sentence in a love letter.

**Host A:** He is doing a grammar lesson, with questions and answers, inside a love letter. And notice, he says, he came, one, saw, two, overcame, three. In Act One he would not say the word three. Now he counts to three in public. And he cannot even spell some of his long words. Annothanize is his attempt at anatomize, to dissect. And he calls the beggar maid pernicious, which means destructive. He uses words for their sound, not their meaning.

**Host B:** And then the ending.

**Boyet:** I am the king; for so stands the comparison: thou the beggar; for so witnesseth thy lowliness. Shall I command thy love? I may: shall I enforce thy love? I could: shall I entreat thy love? I will. What shalt thou exchange for rags? robes; for tittles? titles; for thyself? me.

**Host B:** For thyself, me. What a bargain.

**Host A:** That last line is genuinely funny. You give me yourself, and in exchange you get, me. He thinks that is the best deal anyone was ever offered. And yet, there is something almost touching in the middle of it. Shall I command thy love? I may. Shall I enforce it? I could. Shall I ask for it? I will. Underneath all the vanity, he ends up asking rather than commanding.

**Host B:** How do the ladies react?

**Princess:** What plume of feathers is he that indited this letter? What vane? what weathercock? did you ever hear better?

**Host A:** A plume of feathers, a weathervane, a weathercock. All things that are showy and spin in the wind. And Boyet explains who Armado is with a wonderful phrase.

**Boyet:** This Armado is a Spaniard, that keeps here in court; A phantasime, a Monarcho, and one that makes sport To the prince and his bookmates.

**Host B:** What is a Monarcho?

**Host A:** This is a lovely piece of history. Monarcho was a real person. Isaac Asimov, in his guide to Shakespeare, describes him as a harmless Italian at Queen Elizabeth's court in the 1570s who believed he was the monarch of the world. Everyone humoured him. So when Boyet calls Armado a Monarcho, the audience in London would have thought of a real local eccentric, a man whose grand titles existed only in his own head.

**Host B:** So the play is reaching out of Navarre into Elizabethan London.

**Host A:** It does that constantly. And then the Princess sorts out the mistake. She asks Costard who gave him the letter, and he says Berowne, for a lady of France called Rosaline. The Princess turns to Rosaline and says this.

**Princess:** Here, sweet, put up this: 'twill be thine another day.

**Host B:** Keep it, it will be yours another day. She knows Berowne's real letter is still out there somewhere.

**Host A:** She does. The Princess leaves, and what follows is a burst of very rude joking between Boyet, Rosaline, Maria and Costard. It is all built on archery, because they are on a hunt. Shooting, hitting the mark, pricks, which were the centre point of a target. And it gets steadily filthier. At one point Maria tells Boyet to stop.

**Maria:** Come, come, you talk greasily; your lips grow foul.

**Host B:** That is the play telling us it knows it is being dirty.

**Host A:** Exactly. And it matters for the critics, because this scene is one reason earlier readers were embarrassed by the play. Samuel Johnson, in the eighteenth century, said the play has, quote, many passages mean, childish, and vulgar. Though in the same breath he admitted there are, quote, many sparks of genius.

**Host B:** So he liked it and was embarrassed by it at once.

**Host A:** That is a fair summary of how the play was treated for two centuries. And then Costard, alone on stage, gives a little speech of pure joy about how much fun he just had.

**Costard:** By my soul, a swain! a most simple clown! Lord, Lord, how the ladies and I have put him down! O' my troth, most sweet jests! most incony vulgar wit!

**Costard:** Armado o' th' one side, O, a most dainty man! To see him walk before a lady and to bear her fan! To see him kiss his hand! and how most sweetly a' will swear! And his page o' t' other side, that handful of wit! Ah, heavens, it is a most pathetical nit!

**Host B:** That handful of wit. That is Moth.

**Host A:** A handful of wit, a pathetical nit, a tiny little louse. It is affectionate. Costard is a clown who knows he is a clown and loves being in on the joke. And that ends the scene.

**Host B:** Scene Two brings in some new people.

**Host A:** Two new people, and they may be the funniest pair in the play. Holofernes, a village schoolmaster, and Sir Nathaniel, the curate, the local clergyman. And with them is Constable Dull, who arrested Costard in Act One. They have been watching the Princess's hunt, and Holofernes describes the deer she shot. Listen to how he does it.

**Holofernes:** The deer was, as you know, sanguis, in blood; ripe as the pomewater, who now hangeth like a jewel in the ear of caelo, the sky, the welkin, the heaven; and anon falleth like a crab on the face of terra, the soil, the land, the earth.

**Host B:** The sky, the welkin, the heaven. The soil, the land, the earth.

**Host A:** He cannot say a word once. He says it in Latin, then in English, then gives you two more synonyms in case you missed it. He is a walking dictionary. A pomewater is a kind of apple, and a crab is a crab apple. So the deer was ripe as an apple, and now it has fallen like an apple to the ground.

**Host B:** And Nathaniel admires him.

**Host A:** Nathaniel admires everything Holofernes says. He says the epithets are sweetly varied. But he also says, actually, the deer was a buck of the first head, a young male deer. And Holofernes replies in Latin, haud credo, I do not believe it. And then Dull speaks up.

**Dull:** 'Twas not a haud credo; 'twas a pricket.

**Host B:** He thinks haud credo is a kind of deer.

**Host A:** Editors usually explain that Dull hears haud credo as something like old grey doe, an old female deer. So he corrects the schoolmaster: no, it was not an old grey doe, it was a pricket, a two year old buck. Dull is right about the deer, and completely lost in the language. And Holofernes is outraged that someone so uneducated would dare to interrupt.

**Holofernes:** Most barbarous intimation! yet a kind of insinuation, as it were, in via, in way, of explication; facere, as it were, replication, or rather, ostentare, to show, as it were, his inclination, after his undressed, unpolished, uneducated, unpruned, untrained, or rather, unlettered, or ratherest, unconfirmed fashion, to insert again my haud credo for a deer.

**Host B:** Or ratherest. That is my favourite word in the whole play so far.

**Host A:** Ratherest. He has used rather so many times that he needs a superlative. And look at that list. Undressed, unpolished, uneducated, unpruned, untrained, unlettered. Six ways of calling Dull ignorant, in one breath. And then Nathaniel joins in, and he puts it in terms of food.

**Nathaniel:** Sir, he hath never fed of the dainties that are bred in a book; he hath not eat paper, as it were; he hath not drunk ink: his intellect is not replenished; he is only an animal, only sensible in the duller parts.

**Host B:** He hath not eat paper. He hath not drunk ink.

**Host A:** Learning as eating. And this image runs through the whole play. William Carroll called his book on it The Great Feast of Language, and you can hear why. These men consume words. Dull has not had his meal of paper, so to them he is barely human. But notice who in the scene actually knows what kind of deer it was.

**Host B:** Dull.

**Host A:** Dull. And then Holofernes offers to compose an epitaph on the deer, made up on the spot. He says, I will something affect the letter, for it argues facility. To affect the letter means to use alliteration, lots of words starting with the same letter. Here is the result.

**Holofernes:** The preyful princess pierced and prick'd a pretty pleasing pricket; Some say a sore; but not a sore, till now made sore with shooting.

**Host B:** The preyful princess pierced and pricked a pretty pleasing pricket.

**Host A:** Seven words beginning with P in a row. It is the kind of verse that was fashionable a generation earlier, and by the 1590s it was a joke. Shakespeare does the same thing in A Midsummer Night's Dream, where the workmen's play has lines that pile up alliteration in exactly this way. And Holofernes is very proud of himself.

**Holofernes:** This is a gift that I have, simple, simple; a foolish extravagant spirit, full of forms, figures, shapes, objects, ideas, apprehensions, motions, revolutions: these are begot in the ventricle of memory, nourished in the womb of pia mater, and delivered upon the mellowing of occasion.

**Host B:** Pia mater is part of the brain, isn't it?

**Host A:** It is the membrane that covers the brain. So he describes his ideas as being conceived in his memory, carried in the womb of his brain, and born when the time is right. He takes the language of pregnancy and applies it to his own cleverness. And he says, simple, simple, a gift that I have. He is the least simple man alive.

**Host B:** Who is he? Is he based on someone?

**Host A:** People have tried very hard to answer that. His name comes from the Bible, from the story of Judith, where Holofernes is a general who gets his head cut off. And the scholar Harold Bloom pointed out that Rabelais, the French comic writer, gave the name Holofernes to a ridiculous tutor in Gargantua. So there is a tradition of the name meaning a pompous teacher. Beyond that, scholars have nominated real Elizabethans. Isaac Asimov mentions the theory that Holofernes mocks John Florio, the Italian language teacher and translator in London. Others have suggested Gabriel Harvey, the Cambridge academic. But most scholars think these identifications have had little success.

**Host B:** And there is a Florio link to the title too, isn't there?

**Host A:** There is. The historian Frances Yates, in 1935, connected the play's title to a line in Florio's first language book, His First Fruits, from 1578. So Florio keeps hovering around the edges of this play, even if Holofernes is not simply a portrait of him.

**Host B:** Now Jaquenetta comes in.

**Host A:** And here the letter plot returns. Jaquenetta and Costard arrive, and she has a letter she cannot read. She asks the parson to read it for her, because she thinks it is from Armado. But Costard has swapped them. This is Berowne's letter to Rosaline. And it is a love poem. Nathaniel reads it aloud.

**Nathaniel:** If love make me forsworn, how shall I swear to love? Ah, never faith could hold, if not to beauty vow'd! Though to myself forsworn, to thee I'll faithful prove: Those thoughts to me were oaks, to thee like osiers bow'd.

**Host B:** So Berowne writes to her about breaking his oath.

**Host A:** Straight away. If love makes me break my oath, how can I swear love to you? And then, my thoughts were as strong as oaks to me, but to you they bend like willows. An osier is a willow. And here is the ending of the poem.

**Nathaniel:** Celestial as thou art, O, pardon, love, this wrong, That sings heaven's praise with such an earthly tongue.

**Host B:** That is genuinely lovely.

**Host A:** It is. And here is a piece of publishing history. This poem was printed separately in 1599 in a little book called The Passionate Pilgrim, published by William Jaggard with Shakespeare's name on the title page. Most of the poems in that book are not by Shakespeare at all. Jaggard used his famous name to sell the collection. But this one really is Shakespeare's, lifted from the play. So Berowne's love letter, which in the play gets lost and misdelivered, went out into the real world and got borrowed by a publisher too.

**Host B:** And Holofernes, of course, has notes.

**Holofernes:** Here are only numbers ratified; but, for the elegancy, facility, and golden cadence of poesy, caret. Ovidius Naso was the man: and why, indeed, Naso, but for smelling out the odouriferous flowers of fancy, the jerks of invention? Imitari is nothing.

**Host B:** Caret meaning it is missing.

**Host A:** Caret is Latin for it lacks. He says the metre is correct, the numbers, but it lacks elegance and golden cadence. Then he praises Ovid, whose family name was Naso, which means nose, and he makes a joke that Ovid was called Naso because he could smell out the flowers of fancy. And he says imitari is nothing, imitation is nothing. Which is funny coming from a man who just wrote a poem copying an old fashioned style.

**Host B:** But then he notices who wrote it.

**Host A:** He reads the signature, Berowne, one of the king's lords, writing to one of the French ladies. And he realises this is evidence. A sworn scholar breaking the king's oath. So he tells Jaquenetta to take it straight to the king.

**Holofernes:** Trip and go, my sweet; deliver this paper into the royal hand of the king: it may concern much.

**Host B:** Oh no. That is going to be a problem for Berowne.

**Host A:** It is a ticking clock. Berowne's love poem is now walking toward the one man he swore to.

**Host B:** So what do the critics say about all these pedants?

**Host A:** This is where the critical story gets interesting, because for a long time the answer was, they are tedious. Lewis Theobald, who edited Shakespeare in 1733, finished his notes on this play by saying, quote, I have now done with this play, which in the main may be call'd a very bad one. And William Hazlitt, the Romantic essayist, wrote in 1817, quote, if we were to part with any of the author's comedies, it should be this.

**Host B:** That is harsh.

**Host A:** Very harsh. And the reason, partly, is scenes like this one. Long jokes about Latin, puns on deer, a schoolmaster showing off. Readers found them dated, private jokes for a learned audience. But the modern view has shifted a lot. William Carroll, in the Folger edition, argues that what earlier critics saw as youthful excess is really deliberate experiment. Shakespeare is testing language to its limits on purpose. And James Calderwood argued that the play treats language as an art form in itself, rather than as a way of conveying meaning. You are meant to listen to the words as words.

**Host B:** So Holofernes is not a mistake. He is the point.

**Host A:** He is one of the points. Every character in this act is judging language. The Princess judges the forester's flattery. The ladies judge Armado's letter. Holofernes judges Berowne's poem. And Dull, who cannot follow any of it, is the only one who knows what the deer actually was. The play keeps asking whether all this verbal brilliance gets anyone closer to the truth. And Harold Bloom, at the far end of the critical story from Hazlitt, called the play astonishing, and Shakespeare's first absolute achievement.

**Host B:** From the play we would give up first, to his first absolute achievement.

**Host A:** In about a hundred and eighty years. That is quite a journey.

**Host B:** So where does Act Four, Scene Two leave us?

**Host A:** Armado's letter has been read aloud and laughed at by the French ladies. Berowne's poem has been read aloud by a curate and is now on its way to the king. And the Princess, who never wanted flattery, knows that Berowne has a letter for Rosaline somewhere. Everyone knows a bit more than they should, and the men still think their secrets are safe.

**Host B:** Here is something to try. Say Holofernes' line out loud, as fast as you can. The preyful princess pierced and pricked a pretty pleasing pricket.

**Host A:** And then say the last line of Berowne's poem. Celestial as thou art, O pardon, love, this wrong, that sings heaven's praise with such an earthly tongue. One of them is showing off, and one of them means it. Listen for which sound tells you that.

**Host B:** Next time, the great eavesdropping scene. The king and his lords, one after another, each hiding and listening to the others.

**Host A:** See you then.
