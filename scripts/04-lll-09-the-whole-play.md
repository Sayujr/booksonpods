# Love's Labour's Lost 9. The Whole Play

**Host A:** Picture a park in Navarre in early summer. Four young men in their best clothes, one of them a king, standing in the sun and swearing that for three years they will study, fast, barely sleep, and never look at a woman. And the king, very solemn, says this.

**King:** Navarre shall be the wonder of the world; Our court shall be a little Academe, Still and contemplative in living art.

**Host B:** And a few acts later, the cleverest of the four men stands in the same park, oath in pieces, and says this.

**Berowne:** From women's eyes this doctrine I derive; They are the ground, the books, the academes From whence doth spring the true Promethean fire.

**Host A:** The books, the academes. He takes the king's own word, academe, and gives it to the women's eyes. That is Love's Labour's Lost in two quotations. A vow made of words, and a better set of words that talks its way out of it.

**Host B:** And then, right at the end, something arrives that no set of words can talk its way out of.

**Host A:** Which is where I want to start, actually, because I will be honest about my own view. I love this play. But I have always thought it was a dazzling play about clever people who cannot be serious, and that this is also its limit. It is all surface. Beautiful surface, but surface.

**Host B:** I half agree with you, which is the most annoying amount to agree with someone. So let's take your view seriously and push it as hard as it will go.

**Host A:** Then I have a lot of company. For about two hundred years, the critics were on my side, and they were much ruder than me. Lewis Theobald, editing Shakespeare in 1733, finished his notes on this play by saying, quote, I have now done with this play, which in the main may be call'd a very bad one.

**Host B:** That is a man closing a book with relief.

**Host A:** Francis Gentleman, in 1774, called it one of Shakespeare's weakest compositions. Samuel Johnson said it had, quote, many passages mean, childish, and vulgar. And William Hazlitt, in 1817, gave the cruellest verdict of all. Quote, if we were to part with any of the author's comedies, it should be this.

**Host B:** If we had to throw one overboard.

**Host A:** And the theatre agreed with them. After a couple of court performances in Shakespeare's lifetime, there is no known production until 1839, at Covent Garden. More than two centuries of silence. And when it finally came back, The Times reported that, quote, the play moved very heavily.

**Host B:** So the case for the prosecution is strong. What exactly is the complaint, though? If you had to put it in one sentence.

**Host A:** Too many words, not enough life. Think of Holofernes, who cannot say sky without adding the welkin and the heaven. Or Armado, who will not say three because it is a word for the base vulgar. Or the jokes that need footnotes four hundred years later. Monarcho, the Italian eccentric at Queen Elizabeth's court. Haud credo, mistaken for an old grey doe. And the longest word in Shakespeare, which Costard throws at Moth.

**Costard:** I marvel thy master hath not eaten thee for a word; for thou art not so long by the head as honorificabilitudinitatibus: thou art easier swallowed than a flap-dragon.

**Host B:** Honorificabilitudinitatibus. Say that at a dinner party.

**Host A:** It is a medieval Latin word that means something like the condition of being loaded with honours. And it is the perfect symbol of my complaint. A word so long it has stopped meaning anything, used purely to show off.

**Host B:** But wait. Who is showing off there?

**Host A:** Costard.

**Host B:** No, listen to what Costard is doing. He is mocking Holofernes and Armado for showing off. He says Armado could swallow Moth like a word. And just before that, listen to Moth.

**Moth:** They have been at a great feast of languages, and stolen the scraps.

**Costard:** O, they have lived long on the alms-basket of words.

**Host B:** The alms-basket was where leftover food was collected for the poor. So the servants are saying, these learned men live on scraps of other people's language. That is not the play showing off. That is the play laughing at showing off. Your complaint is already inside the play. The characters are making it for you.

**Host A:** That is a good point, but I am not sure it gets the play off the hook. A play can mock excess and still be excessive.

**Host B:** True. So let's push your view one step further and see what happens. You say the play is all words. Fine. Now look at what happens to the words in it. Armado cannot get his number out. Holofernes cannot say anything once. Berowne's love sonnet goes to the wrong person and ends up in the hands of the king. Armado's love letter gets read aloud for laughs by the very ladies it was not meant for. And in the last act, the men put on Russian costumes and masks to woo the women in disguise, and the women, who were warned, swap their tokens so that every man woos the wrong lady in the most elaborate language he can manage.

**Host A:** Yes, the Muscovite scene. It is a disaster for them.

**Host B:** So in a play that is all words, the words keep failing. Every single grand speech misfires. That is not decoration. That is a pattern.

**Host A:** So you are saying that if you take the all words complaint seriously, it turns into something else.

**Host B:** It turns into a subject. The play is not merely made of language. It is about language. About what happens when people try to live inside their own words. And that is exactly where the critical story turned. Walter Pater, writing in the late nineteenth century, called the play a, quote, foppery of delicate language, and said this fashionable plaything of his time is what Shakespeare is occupied with. Occupied with. He saw it as the subject, not just the style.

**Host A:** Though Pater also thought Berowne was, quote, still chargeable even at his best with just a little affectation.

**Host B:** He did, and I think that is fair. But then the twentieth century goes much further. James Calderwood argued that the play treats language as an art form in itself, not as a way of carrying meaning. William Carroll, who wrote a whole book called The Great Feast of Language, and the modern Folger introduction, argues that what earlier critics called youthful immaturity is really deliberate experiment. He calls the pun the linguistic DNA of the play. And John Pendergast says that no other Shakespeare play explores so fully the power and the limitations of language.

**Host A:** Power and limitations. That is the key phrase, isn't it.

**Host B:** It is. And the best evidence is in the play itself. After the Muscovite fiasco, Berowne, the cleverest talker of them all, makes a public renunciation. He swears off fancy language forever. Listen to how he does it.

**Berowne:** Taffeta phrases, silken terms precise, Three-piled hyperboles, spruce affectation, Figures pedantical; these summer-flies Have blown me full of maggot ostentation: I do forswear them; and I here protest, By this white glove; how white the hand, God knows! Henceforth my wooing mind shall be express'd In russet yeas and honest kersey noes: And, to begin, wench, so God help me, la! My love to thee is sound, sans crack or flaw.

**Host A:** Taffeta and silk on one side, russet and kersey on the other. Plain country cloth.

**Host B:** He is going to dress his love in homespun from now on. And how does Rosaline reply?

**Rosaline:** Sans sans, I pray you.

**Host A:** Oh, that's wonderful. He swears off fancy words and immediately uses a French one. Sans.

**Host B:** In the very same breath. Sans crack or flaw. And she catches him in two words. Without sans, please. And he admits it.

**Berowne:** Yet I have a trick Of the old rage: bear with me, I am sick; I'll leave it by degrees.

**Host A:** I'll leave it by degrees. He knows he cannot simply stop.

**Host B:** And that, I think, is where your view and mine meet. You said it is all surface. I say it is about surface. And Berowne's line shows the play knows something deeper still. You cannot simply step out of language into some plain, honest place. Even the vow to speak plainly comes out ornamented. The play does not believe in an easy escape from words.

**Host A:** All right. I will give you that much. It is a play about language, not just a play made of it. But here is my next problem. If it is about language, it is still a very closed room. Clever young aristocrats, talking about talking. Where does anything else get in?

**Host B:** Then let's push that one. If the play shows language failing, who sees through it?

**Host A:** The women. Always the women.

**Host B:** From the first moment. The Princess refuses Boyet's flattery in her first speech. Rosaline beats Berowne in their first exchange. The Princess pays the forester for an honest answer during the hunt. The dairy maid Jaquenetta says to Armado, I will tell thee wonders, and she replies, with that face? And at the end, when the men finally make their declarations, listen to how the Princess sums up all their letters and poems.

**Princess:** We have received your letters full of love; Your favours, the ambassadors of love; And, in our maiden council, rated them At courtship, pleasant jest and courtesy, As bombast and as lining to the time.

**Host A:** Bombast. Bombast was cotton stuffing used to pad out clothes.

**Host B:** So she says, we took your love poems as padding. Fashionable stuffing. That is the women's verdict on the men's language. And the Folger introduction makes a related point, that the play pushes back against the men's assumption that women are either temptresses or goddesses. The women in this play are neither. They are simply sharper.

**Host A:** And there are feminist readings that go much further. The critic Roberts reads it as an outright feminist play, and sets it beside Margaret Cavendish's The Convent of Pleasure. Another critic, Asp, argues that the women teach the men to give up a defensive kind of wit for a compassionate kind of humour.

**Host B:** So that is a happy reading. The women as teachers, the men as students, in a play that began with men trying to run an academy.

**Host A:** But I am going to push back now, because if we follow your logic, it does not stay happy for long.

**Host B:** Go on.

**Host A:** If the women see through the men's words, what do the women actually have, other than their own words? Look at who owns what in this play. The men have titles and estates and names. The king of Navarre, Lord Longaville, Lord Dumaine. Carroll points out, following Katharine Eisaman Maus, that the women have no surnames at all. And the Princess's whole diplomatic mission is about Aquitaine, a piece of land described in the play as a dowry for a queen. She is negotiating property that would follow her into a marriage.

**Host B:** So the women win the argument, but the world they win it in still belongs to the men.

**Host A:** And the poetry itself is part of the problem. Mark Breitenberg argues that the men's idealising love poetry is, quote, the textualized form of the male gaze. When Berowne praises Rosaline's eyes, he is also controlling her by turning her into an image. And the critic Hall goes further still. She argues that the women's actions, for all their wit, end up defending the existing patriarchal order rather than overturning it.

**Host B:** And there is Rosaline's darkness, which runs right through the play. When the king mocks her complexion, he says black is the badge of hell. Robin Williams, writing on his blog I Read Shakespeare, argues that we should read Rosaline as a Black woman, and that calling her darkness only a convention lets the text off too lightly.

**Host A:** So the play that seemed to be about the women seeing clearly is also, quietly, about the limits of what they can do.

**Host B:** That does not cancel the first reading, though. It sharpens it. The women's real power in this play is the power to judge, to refuse, and to delay. They cannot change who owns Aquitaine. But they can say not yet. And as we will see, not yet is how the whole play ends.

**Host A:** Before we get there, though, I want to try one more angle on my original complaint. Maybe the reason the play feels like a closed room is that it was literally written for one. A private joke for a particular audience in the 1590s.

**Host B:** That is a very old theory, and there is something to it.

**Host A:** The names alone are a giveaway. The King of Navarre in the 1590s was Henry of Navarre, who became Henry the Fourth of France. Berowne, Longaville and Dumaine are versions of real French nobles of the time, the duc de Biron, the duc de Longueville and the duc de Mayenne. Biron was well known in England, because the Earl of Essex had fought alongside him in 1591. So an English audience would have heard real, living names.

**Host B:** And there is Monarcho, the real eccentric at Elizabeth's court. And then the famous School of Night.

**Host A:** Yes. In Act Four, the king mocks Rosaline's darkness with a line that, in one early reading, says black is the school of night. In 1903, a writer named Arthur Acheson built a whole theory on that phrase. He argued that the play was satirising a secret circle of free thinkers around Sir Walter Raleigh, with Christopher Marlowe and the poet George Chapman and the mathematician Thomas Harriot. Later, Frances Yates and Muriel Bradbrook developed it. For a while it was very fashionable.

**Host B:** And now?

**Host A:** Now it has largely fallen out of favour. Other editions read the line as suit of night or scowl of night. And the scholar John Kerrigan argues that the line is simply a joke about Rosaline's dark hair, and that hunting for secret topical meanings is misleading. Though the School of Night lives on happily in novels and plays.

**Host B:** So the topical reading is half true. The play was clearly full of in-jokes. It was apparently performed at court before Queen Elizabeth at Christmas 1597. The critic Hunt reads the Princess as reflecting mixed feelings about the ageing Elizabeth, a female ruler with all the authority in the room.

**Host A:** Which is a striking idea. A queen watching a play about a princess who outwits a court of men.

**Host B:** But here is what I think the topical reading does to your complaint. If the play is full of jokes about one particular court, it is also about something that never goes away. A group of powerful men who think they can legislate desire out of existence by signing a document. That is not a joke about 1595. That is a joke about every institution that has ever tried it.

**Host A:** Fair. The particular names are dead. The pattern is very much alive.

**Host B:** So, where are we? You came in saying, all surface. We have pushed it into a play about language, then into a play about who gets to see through language, and then into a play about real power behind the pretty words. And now we have to deal with the ending. Because this is where the play does something no comedy of its time was supposed to do.

**Host A:** Let's set it up properly. The last act is a long, glittering party. The Muscovite masks have failed. The men have confessed. And the comic characters put on an entertainment for the lords and ladies, a pageant of the Nine Worthies, nine great heroes of history and legend. Holofernes plays Judas Maccabaeus, Armado plays Hector of Troy, Costard plays Pompey the Great, and so on.

**Host B:** And the lords heckle them mercilessly.

**Host A:** Mercilessly. They pun on every name, and turn Holofernes' noble Judas Maccabaeus into Judas the traitor. And then Holofernes, who has been a figure of fun all play, says one quiet line.

**Holofernes:** This is not generous, not gentle, not humble.

**Host B:** And suddenly the joke is on the lords.

**Host A:** Completely. The pompous schoolmaster is the one with dignity, and the young aristocrats look like bullies. And then Armado, as Hector, gets mocked in turn, and he says this.

**Armado:** The sweet war-man is dead and rotten; sweet chucks, beat not the bones of the buried: when he breathed, he was a man.

**Host B:** When he breathed, he was a man.

**Host A:** Of all the people in the play to say something so humane, it is Armado. The man who could not say three. And it lands, because the very next thing that happens is this. A messenger enters. His name is Mercade.

**Princess:** Welcome, Mercade; But that thou interrupt'st our merriment.

**Host B:** And the messenger says, the news I bring is heavy in my tongue. The king your father.

**Princess:** Dead, for my life!

**Host B:** And Berowne.

**Berowne:** Worthies, away! the scene begins to cloud.

**Host A:** The scene begins to cloud. One line, and the whole sunlit park goes dark.

**Host B:** Peter Holland, writing in Shakespeare Survey, points out that critics generally treat the last line of the play, the words of Mercury, as a kind of reality principle breaking up the play's fantasy world. And Mercade, the messenger, is the first embodiment of it. Mercury was the messenger god. Mercade brings the message.

**Host A:** And then the men try to keep the courtship going anyway. The king says, roughly, the timing is bad, but let's not let sorrow get in the way of love. And Berowne says something that sounds exactly like what we have been waiting for.

**Berowne:** Honest plain words best pierce the ear of grief.

**Host B:** Except, of course, he then goes on at length, in very elaborate argument.

**Host A:** He cannot help it. And the women do not accept the proposals. Not now. The Princess tells the king that his oath has already proved worthless, and sets him a test.

**Princess:** Your oath I will not trust; but go with speed To some forlorn and naked hermitage, Remote from all the pleasures of the world; There stay until the twelve celestial signs Have brought about the annual reckoning.

**Host B:** A year in a hermitage. Which is, if you think about it, the academy he wanted at the start. Fasting, solitude, no women. Only now it is real, and it is a penance.

**Host A:** That is such a good observation. The play gives the king his wish, and it is a punishment. And then Rosaline gives Berowne the most interesting test of all.

**Rosaline:** You shall this twelvemonth term from day to day Visit the speechless sick and still converse With groaning wretches; and your task shall be, With all the fierce endeavor of your wit To enforce the pained impotent to smile.

**Berowne:** To move wild laughter in the throat of death? It cannot be; it is impossible: Mirth cannot move a soul in agony.

**Host B:** And her answer is, I think, the moral centre of the whole play.

**Rosaline:** Why, that's the way to choke a gibing spirit, Whose influence is begot of that loose grace Which shallow laughing hearers give to fools: A jest's prosperity lies in the ear Of him that hears it, never in the tongue Of him that makes it.

**Host A:** A jest's prosperity lies in the ear of him that hears it, never in the tongue of him that makes it.

**Host B:** Everything Berowne has done all play has been about the tongue. How clever he sounds. And Rosaline says, a joke is only as good as the person hearing it. Go and tell your jokes to people who are dying. If they laugh, keep your wit. If they do not, give it up.

**Host A:** That is the answer to my original complaint, isn't it. I said the play is about clever people who cannot be serious. And the play agrees with me. It just does not stop there. It sends its cleverest man to a hospital for a year to find out whether his cleverness is worth anything.

**Host B:** And then Berowne says the line the whole play has been building toward.

**Berowne:** Our wooing doth not end like an old play; Jack hath not Jill: these ladies' courtesy Might well have made our sport a comedy.

**King:** Come, sir, it wants a twelvemonth and a day, And then 'twill end.

**Berowne:** That's too long for a play.

**Host A:** That's too long for a play. He is a character in a play, complaining that his play will not end properly.

**Host B:** It is breathtaking, really. The comedy steps outside itself and says, I know you expected weddings. You are not getting them.

**Host A:** And the critics have argued about this ending more than about anything else in the play. Some, like the critic Smidt, say the play begins as romance and ends as satire. Others, like Chaney, argue that Shakespeare deliberately avoided a conventionally happy ending. Erickson argues that the men's foolishness and the women's dominance make a wedding ending impossible. And Carroll, in the Folger introduction, simply says the ending breaks comic convention and leaves the resolution deferred.

**Host B:** And Louis Montrose, in a well known essay from 1977, reads the open ending as reconciling actuality and imagination. Not rejecting the play's fantasy, but setting it against the real world and asking them to meet.

**Host A:** And there is C. L. Barber, whose book Shakespeare's Festive Comedy, from 1959, has a chapter called The Folly of Wit and Masquerade in Love's Labour's Lost. Barber's big idea is that Shakespeare's comedies work like holidays. A period of release, misrule and play, which leads to clarification. You come back from the holiday seeing ordinary life more clearly.

**Host B:** And this play is the holiday that gets interrupted. The clarification arrives before anyone is ready for it.

**Host A:** Which raises the most tantalising mystery of all. Is there a sequel?

**Host B:** Ah, Love's Labour's Won.

**Host A:** In 1598, a writer named Francis Meres published a list of Shakespeare's comedies, and on it, next to Love's Labour's Lost, is a play called Love's Labour's Won. And in 1953, a scrap of paper turned up in the binding of an old book, a bookseller's list from 1603, which also includes Love's Labour's Won. So it seems to have existed, possibly even in print.

**Host B:** And nobody has a copy.

**Host A:** Nobody. Theories range from a lost sequel, to an alternative title for The Taming of the Shrew, to an early version of All's Well That Ends Well. And in 2014, the Royal Shakespeare Company had its own answer. Christopher Luscombe directed Love's Labour's Lost alongside Much Ado About Nothing, renamed Love's Labour's Won. He set them just before and just after the First World War, in a country house modelled on Charlecote Park in Warwickshire.

**Host B:** So the year of waiting becomes the war.

**Host A:** The young men go off to a year that turns into four, and come back changed. And the same two actors, Edward Bennett and Michelle Terry, played Berowne and Rosaline in one play, and Benedick and Beatrice in the other.

**Host B:** Which makes an argument just by casting. That Berowne and Rosaline, sparring and wounding and loving each other in short sharp lines, are a first sketch of Benedick and Beatrice.

**Host A:** And the critic Dominic Cavendish called the pairing the most blissfully entertaining and emotionally involving thing he had seen at the RSC in ages.

**Host B:** Which brings us to the question of why this play matters. Because I think by now we can say something stronger than it is clever and fun.

**Host A:** Let me try. And I will start from where I began, because my view has changed. I came in thinking cleverness was the play's limit. I now think cleverness is its subject, and the limit is something the play reaches on purpose. It is a play that takes language as far as it can go, and then walks it into a room with a death in it, and asks, what now?

**Host B:** And that is why, I think, it matters in Shakespeare's own development. Joseph Westlund calls it a prelude to the more extensive treatment of imagination in A Midsummer Night's Dream. The workmen's play in the Dream, with its overblown alliteration and its heckling aristocrats, is the Nine Worthies done again with more tenderness. And Berowne and Rosaline grow up into Benedick and Beatrice. So you can watch Shakespeare learning his craft here. Testing every trick he has, and then testing the tricks themselves.

**Host A:** Harold Bloom went furthest. In 1994 he called the play astonishing, and Shakespeare's first absolute achievement. From Hazlitt saying throw it overboard to Bloom saying first absolute achievement, in under two hundred years.

**Host B:** And on stage, the twentieth century rescued it too. The Old Vic in 1936. A twenty one year old Peter Brook directed it at Stratford in 1946. Laurence Olivier staged it at the National Theatre in 1968. In 1973 it became an opera, with music by Nicolas Nabokov and a libretto by W. H. Auden and Chester Kallman. Kenneth Branagh made it a 1930s film musical in 2000, which most critics did not like.

**Host A:** And it still divides people. Michael Billington, reviewing an RSC production in 1994, called it Shakespeare's most beguiling comedy. Ben Brantley, reviewing a touring production in 2009, called it sophomoric. Both are describing the same play, and in a way both are right. It is a play about sophomores, about clever students, and what makes it beguiling is that it knows it.

**Host B:** And there is one production I find especially moving. In 2005 it was staged in Kabul, in Dari. A few years after the fall of the Taliban, who had banned music and so many ordinary pleasures, a company performs a play about men who swear off pleasure, and the women who show them how foolish that was.

**Host A:** That is the part of the play that is not a 1590s in-joke at all.

**Host B:** So here is where I land on why it matters today. We live surrounded by talk that performs. Clever talk, branded talk, talk designed to win the room. This play is about exactly that, and it is very funny about it. But it also asks Rosaline's question. What is your cleverness worth to someone who is suffering? A jest's prosperity lies in the ear of him that hears it.

**Host A:** Where do we still disagree, though? I do not want to pretend we have agreed on everything.

**Host B:** I think you still find some of it hard going. The Latin jokes, the long scenes of pedantry.

**Host A:** I do. I think some of it is dead on the page and needs a really good production to live. And I think the topical satire is mostly gone, and no amount of footnotes brings it back. You?

**Host B:** I think the topical stuff is the least important thing in it. And I think even the dull scenes are doing work, because they set up the moment when the pedants turn out to be more humane than the lords. Holofernes saying, this is not generous, not gentle, not humble. That line only lands because we have spent an hour laughing at him.

**Host A:** So, fair to say, we agree on what it is, and we disagree on how much patience it is owed.

**Host B:** That seems right. And I think the play would approve of an ending with a disagreement left open.

**Host A:** It would. Because it does not end with an argument, or a wedding, or a moral. It ends with two songs. The comic characters perform a final debate between the cuckoo, who stands for spring, and the owl, who stands for winter. Spring first.

**Spring:** When daisies pied and violets blue And lady-smocks all silver-white And cuckoo-buds of yellow hue Do paint the meadows with delight, The cuckoo then, on every tree, Mocks married men; for thus sings he, Cuckoo; Cuckoo, cuckoo: O word of fear, Unpleasing to a married ear!

**Host B:** The cuckoo laid its eggs in other birds' nests, so its call was a joke about husbands being deceived. Even spring, in this play, comes with a warning about love.

**Host A:** And then winter.

**Winter:** When icicles hang by the wall And Dick the shepherd blows his nail And Tom bears logs into the hall And milk comes frozen home in pail, When blood is nipp'd and ways be foul, Then nightly sings the staring owl, Tu-whit; Tu-who, a merry note, While greasy Joan doth keel the pot.

**Winter:** When all aloud the wind doth blow And coughing drowns the parson's saw And birds sit brooding in the snow And Marian's nose looks red and raw, When roasted crabs hiss in the bowl, Then nightly sings the staring owl, Tu-whit; Tu-who, a merry note, While greasy Joan doth keel the pot.

**Host B:** Greasy Joan doth keel the pot. Keel means to cool, to stir it so it does not boil over.

**Host A:** And Joan. Remember Berowne's line in Act Three, some men must love my lady and some Joan. Here she is at last, the ordinary Joan, by a winter fire, with a red nosed Marian and a coughing parson. After a whole play of silk and taffeta, the last image is russet and kersey. Plain cloth, cold hands, real life.

**Host B:** Catherine McLay reads the songs as a movement from artifice to nature, from illusion to reality. And Barber's book has a section on them too. Spring and winter, the year turning. Which is exactly what the lovers now have to wait through.

**Host A:** A twelvemonth and a day. And then Armado, of all people, gets the last line of the play.

**Armado:** The words of Mercury are harsh after the songs of Apollo. You that way: we this way.

**Host B:** Apollo, the god of song and poetry. Mercury, the messenger, the god who brought the news. After the music comes the message. After the play, the world.

**Host A:** You that way, we this way. The characters part, and so do we.

**Host B:** Thank you for listening to Books on Pods.
