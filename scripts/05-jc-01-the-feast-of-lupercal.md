# Julius Caesar 1. The Feast of Lupercal

**Host A:** Rome, a February morning, and the streets are full. Shops are shut. Working men are out in their best clothes. And two officials, tribunes of the people, come striding through the crowd, furious.

**Flavius:** Hence! home, you idle creatures get you home: Is this a holiday? what! know you not, Being mechanical, you ought not walk Upon a labouring day without the sign Of your profession? Speak, what trade art thou?

**Host B:** Being mechanical. Meaning working men, manual workers. And the tribune is telling them they should not be out without their work clothes and tools. They are dressed for a holiday, and he wants them back in their place.

**Host A:** And this is how Julius Caesar begins. Not with Caesar. Not with Brutus. With a row in the street between officials and tradesmen. And the tradesman who answers is a cobbler, and he runs rings round them.

**Marullus:** What trade, thou knave? thou naughty knave, what trade?

**Cobbler:** Nay, I beseech you, sir, be not out with me: yet, if you be out, sir, I can mend you.

**Host B:** Be not out with me, meaning do not be angry with me. But out also means your shoes are worn out. And if you are out, I can mend you.

**Host A:** He has already told them he is a mender of bad soles. Soles of shoes, souls of men. Two puns in two lines, at the expense of a government official.

**Host B:** And the late seventeenth century critic Thomas Rymer hated this. He thought it was beneath the dignity of a Roman tragedy, and he sneered that Shakespeare was still running after his masters, the cobblers and parish clerks.

**Host A:** His masters, the cobblers. Which tells you something about Rymer. He wanted Romans to sound like marble statues, and here is a shoe mender making jokes.

**Host B:** But I think Shakespeare's choice is deliberate and deep. The first voice we hear from the Roman people is clever, cheerful and slightly disrespectful. These are not a mindless mob. Not yet. And the first thing the play's officials do is try to shut them up.

**Host A:** And what are they celebrating? Caesar has come home in triumph. And Marullus explodes at them.

**Marullus:** Wherefore rejoice? What conquest brings he home? What tributaries follow him to Rome, To grace in captive bonds his chariot-wheels? You blocks, you stones, you worse than senseless things! O you hard hearts, you cruel men of Rome, Knew you not Pompey?

**Marullus:** And do you now put on your best attire? And do you now cull out a holiday? And do you now strew flowers in his way That comes in triumph over Pompey's blood? Be gone!

**Host B:** That comes in triumph over Pompey's blood. This is the historical background, and it matters. Caesar had fought a civil war against Pompey the Great, his former ally. Pompey was murdered in Egypt. Caesar then defeated Pompey's sons in Spain. So this triumph is not over a foreign enemy. It is a Roman celebrating victory over other Romans.

**Host A:** Which, for a Roman audience, was something close to obscene. A triumph was meant to be for conquering foreigners, not for killing your fellow citizens.

**Host B:** And Shakespeare found that detail in his source. He took the story almost entirely from Plutarch's Lives of the Noble Grecians and Romans, in the English translation by Thomas North, published in 1579. And Plutarch says that this particular triumph displeased the Romans more than anything Caesar had done, precisely because it celebrated the ruin of Pompey's family.

**Host A:** But Shakespeare also moves things around. Historically, Caesar's triumph over Pompey's sons was in the autumn of forty five before Christ, and the feast of the Lupercal, where Caesar was offered a crown, was months later, in February forty four. Shakespeare squeezes them into the same day.

**Host B:** So the triumph and the crown happen together. The victory over Romans, and the offer of kingship, in one festival. It is a dramatist's compression, and it makes the political temperature much higher.

**Host A:** And then the tribunes do something that turns out to be very dangerous. They go round the city tearing decorations off Caesar's statues.

**Flavius:** These growing feathers pluck'd from Caesar's wing Will make him fly an ordinary pitch, Who else would soar above the view of men And keep us all in servile fearfulness.

**Host B:** A falconry image. Pluck the feathers from the bird of prey so that it cannot fly too high.

**Host A:** And a few scenes later, Casca reports, almost in passing, that Marullus and Flavius, for pulling scarves off Caesar's images, are put to silence.

**Host B:** Put to silence. Nobody explains what that means. Silenced. Removed. Maybe killed. And it is mentioned as a piece of gossip, between a joke about Cicero speaking Greek and Casca saying there was more foolery yet if he could remember it.

**Host A:** That is one of the most chilling understatements in the play. The first two men who spoke out against Caesar disappear, and nobody is especially bothered.

**Host B:** And notice one more word. When the commoners slink off, ashamed, Flavius says, see whether their basest metal be not moved. Their basest metal.

**Host A:** Metal. That is a word to watch. Because the critic G. Wilson Knight, in the nineteen thirties, traced how the play is full of images of metal, alongside images of animals and blood. Base metal, noble metal. And it comes back very soon, about Brutus.

**Host B:** So the first scene sets up almost everything. The people as an audience that can be moved. Officials who think they know better. The shadow of Pompey. And a holiday that turns into a political crisis.

**Host A:** Now the second scene. And here the play's real subject arrives. Caesar enters with his whole entourage on the way to the games of the Lupercal. And his first lines are to his wife.

**Caesar:** Stand you directly in Antonius' way, When he doth run his course. Antonius!

**Caesar:** Forget not, in your speed, Antonius, To touch Calpurnia; for our elders say, The barren, touched in this holy chase, Shake off their sterile curse.

**Host B:** We should explain the Lupercal, because it is so strange. It was an ancient Roman festival in February. Young men of good families ran through the city, almost naked, carrying strips of goatskin, and struck the people they passed, especially women, because it was believed to cure barrenness and help childbirth. Plutarch describes it.

**Host A:** And Antony is one of the runners. So the first thing we learn about Mark Antony is that he is an athlete at a fertility rite.

**Host B:** And the first thing we learn about Caesar is that he has no heir, and that he wants one. In front of everyone. The most powerful man in the world, publicly asking a friend to strike his wife with goatskin so that she might have a child.

**Host A:** And his wife is publicly described as barren. Which is not a small thing to say aloud.

**Host B:** The scholar Naomi Conn Liebler wrote about how this festival frames the whole play. The Lupercal was a ritual of purification, of cleansing the city. And Brutus, later, will try to make the murder into exactly that kind of ritual. A sacrifice. A purging. So the play opens with one Roman ritual, and the killers will try to stage another.

**Host A:** And then, out of the crowd, a voice.

**Soothsayer:** Caesar!

**Caesar:** Ha! who calls?

**Host B:** And Casca, the eager courtier, shouts for silence. Peace, ho. Bid every noise be still.

**Caesar:** Who is it in the press that calls on me? I hear a tongue, shriller than all the music, Cry 'Caesar!' Speak; Caesar is turn'd to hear.

**Soothsayer:** Beware the ides of March.

**Host A:** Beware the ides of March. The fifteenth of March. Probably the most famous warning in literature.

**Host B:** And look at how Caesar responds. He asks to see the man's face. He hears the warning a second time. And then he says, he is a dreamer, let us leave him, pass. And the procession moves on.

**Host A:** And listen to how Caesar speaks. Caesar is turned to hear. Not I am turned. Caesar. He speaks about himself in the third person, as if he were already a statue of himself.

**Host B:** And that habit runs through the whole play. Caesar shall go forth. Always I am Caesar. And the scholar M. W. MacCallum, writing in 1910, noticed Caesar's constant harping on his own fearlessness, and said that it should make us suspicious. And MacCallum saw in Caesar a touch of self deception as well as of superstition.

**Host A:** A man who keeps saying he is not afraid.

**Host B:** Which is usually a man who is afraid. Now everyone leaves for the games except Brutus and Cassius. And this is the scene the whole play turns on. Cassius, who hates Caesar, sets out to recruit Brutus, who is respected by everyone. And he does it so skilfully that it repays the closest attention.

**Host A:** He starts gently. He says Brutus has seemed cold to him lately. Brutus says, no, I am at war with myself, I have private troubles, do not take it personally. And Cassius uses that opening.

**Cassius:** Tell me, good Brutus, can you see your face?

**Brutus:** No, Cassius; for the eye sees not itself, But by reflection, by some other things.

**Cassius:** 'Tis just: And it is very much lamented, Brutus, That you have no such mirrors as will turn Your hidden worthiness into your eye, That you might see your shadow.

**Host B:** This is brilliant. Cassius offers to be Brutus's mirror.

**Cassius:** And since you know you cannot see yourself So well as by reflection, I, your glass, Will modestly discover to yourself That of yourself which you yet know not of.

**Host A:** I, your glass. I will be your mirror, and show you a part of yourself you do not yet know.

**Host B:** And this is exactly where several twentieth century critics pinned Brutus's tragedy. Marvin Vawter said Brutus is, quote, unable to see himself. And that is not an accident of character. It is set up here, in the first conversation, when Brutus admits that the eye cannot see itself, and then lets the man who wants to use him hold up the mirror.

**Host A:** And notice Brutus's reply, which is almost a warning to himself.

**Brutus:** Into what dangers would you lead me, Cassius, That you would have me seek into myself For that which is not in me?

**Host B:** For that which is not in me. He suspects it. He knows Cassius is leading him somewhere. And he keeps listening.

**Host A:** Then offstage, a shout. The crowd at the games is roaring. And Brutus says something revealing.

**Brutus:** What means this shouting? I do fear, the people Choose Caesar for their king.

**Cassius:** Ay, do you fear it? Then must I think you would not have it so.

**Brutus:** I would not, Cassius; yet I love him well.

**Host A:** I would not, yet I love him well. The whole tragedy of Brutus is already in that line.

**Host B:** And Cassius pounces on the word fear. Do you fear it? Then you do not want it. He makes Brutus say out loud what he was only half thinking.

**Host A:** And then Brutus gives Cassius the key to his whole personality.

**Brutus:** If it be aught toward the general good, Set honour in one eye and death i' the other, And I will look on both indifferently, For let the gods so speed me as I love The name of honour more than I fear death.

**Host B:** The name of honour. Not honour. The name of honour.

**Host A:** That is such a telling phrase.

**Host B:** It is, and the critic R. A. Foakes put his finger exactly on it. He said that Brutus's idealistic aim is undone by his self deception, an obsession with names, and an ignorance of reality. An obsession with names. Brutus loves the name of honour. And Cassius, who is listening very carefully, is about to talk to him almost entirely about names.

**Host A:** But first Cassius tells two stories about Caesar's body. And these are some of the most interesting speeches in the play, because they are about what makes a Roman man.

**Cassius:** I was born free as Caesar; so were you: We both have fed as well, and we can both Endure the winter's cold as well as he: For once, upon a raw and gusty day, The troubled Tiber chafing with her shores, Caesar said to me 'Darest thou, Cassius, now Leap in with me into this angry flood, And swim to yonder point?' Upon the word, Accoutred as I was, I plunged in And bade him follow; so indeed he did.

**Cassius:** But ere we could arrive the point proposed, Caesar cried 'Help me, Cassius, or I sink!' I, as Aeneas, our great ancestor, Did from the flames of Troy upon his shoulder The old Anchises bear, so from the waves of Tiber Did I the tired Caesar. And this man Is now become a god.

**Host A:** So Cassius rescued Caesar from drowning, like Aeneas carrying his father out of burning Troy.

**Host B:** Aeneas was the legendary founder of the Roman people, who escaped Troy carrying his old father on his back. So Cassius is casting himself as the founder of Rome, and Caesar as the feeble old man who had to be carried.

**Host A:** And then the second story.

**Cassius:** He had a fever when he was in Spain, And when the fit was on him, I did mark How he did shake: 'tis true, this god did shake; His coward lips did from their colour fly, And that same eye whose bend doth awe the world Did lose his lustre: I did hear him groan: Ay, and that tongue of his that bade the Romans Mark him and write his speeches in their books, Alas, it cried 'Give me some drink, Titinius,' As a sick girl.

**Host B:** As a sick girl. That is the insult the whole speech has been building to.

**Host A:** And this is where Coppélia Kahn's work on the Roman plays is so illuminating. In her book Roman Shakespeare, Kahn argues that the republic in these plays is a masculine sphere, where men prove their manhood in competition with each other. The Latin word for virtue, virtus, comes from vir, a man. To be virtuous was literally to be manly.

**Host B:** So what Cassius is doing is not really a political argument. He is not saying Caesar is a tyrant who has broken the law. He is saying Caesar is physically weak, he cannot swim as well as me, he cried like a girl when he had a fever. And therefore he does not deserve to be greater than us.

**Host A:** It is the logic of the playground. Or the gym.

**Host B:** And it reveals Cassius's real motive. Which a number of critics have named directly. Envy. And notice that, at the end of the play, Antony will say that all the conspirators except Brutus acted out of envy of great Caesar. Cassius's speeches here are the evidence.

**Host A:** And then the most famous speech of the scene, which we have to hear in full.

**Cassius:** Why, man, he doth bestride the narrow world Like a Colossus, and we petty men Walk under his huge legs and peep about To find ourselves dishonourable graves. Men at some time are masters of their fates: The fault, dear Brutus, is not in our stars, But in ourselves, that we are underlings.

**Cassius:** Brutus and Caesar: what should be in that 'Caesar'? Why should that name be sounded more than yours? Write them together, yours is as fair a name; Sound them, it doth become the mouth as well; Weigh them, it is as heavy; conjure with 'em, Brutus will start a spirit as soon as Caesar.

**Host B:** There it is. The names. Write them, sound them, weigh them, conjure with them. Foakes's obsession with names, played on directly.

**Host A:** The Colossus was the giant bronze statue of the sun god at Rhodes, one of the wonders of the ancient world, which legend said stood astride the harbour. And Cassius's image is of Caesar as that statue, and everyone else walking underneath, looking for somewhere to be buried.

**Host B:** And then he plays the trump card. Brutus's ancestry.

**Cassius:** O, you and I have heard our fathers say, There was a Brutus once that would have brook'd The eternal devil to keep his state in Rome As easily as a king.

**Host A:** This is Lucius Junius Brutus, the legendary founder of the Roman republic, who drove out the last king of Rome, Tarquin, about five hundred years earlier. Marcus Brutus believed he was descended from him.

**Host B:** And the critic Geoffrey Miles said something very sharp about this. He said Brutus seems unaware of how much personal and family pride drives his decision. He thinks he is thinking about Rome. He is also thinking about being a Brutus.

**Host A:** And the critic John Anson went further. He argued that Brutus's love of his country conceals envy of Caesar too. That even the noblest conspirator has some of Cassius in him.

**Host B:** Which is a reading that the play does not quite confirm and does not quite deny. Brutus himself answers Cassius very carefully.

**Brutus:** What you have said I will consider; what you have to say I will with patience hear, and find a time Both meet to hear and answer such high things. Till then, my noble friend, chew upon this: Brutus had rather be a villager Than to repute himself a son of Rome Under these hard conditions as this time Is like to lay upon us.

**Host A:** Brutus had rather be a villager. Brutus. He talks about himself in the third person too.

**Host B:** Just like Caesar. That is a lovely catch. Both of them have started speaking of themselves as names.

**Host A:** And now Caesar comes back from the games, looking angry. And he sees Cassius, and says the lines that everyone knows.

**Caesar:** Let me have men about me that are fat; Sleek-headed men and such as sleep o' nights: Yond Cassius has a lean and hungry look; He thinks too much: such men are dangerous.

**Host B:** And this is a good moment to see how Shakespeare worked with Plutarch. In North's translation, Plutarch has Caesar say something like this. That he did not fear fat, smooth combed men, but the pale and lean ones, and he meant Brutus and Cassius. Shakespeare takes that bare remark and turns it into a little character study.

**Caesar:** He reads much; He is a great observer and he looks Quite through the deeds of men: he loves no plays, As thou dost, Antony; he hears no music; Seldom he smiles.

**Host A:** He loves no plays. Spoken in a theatre.

**Host B:** That is a lovely joke. The man who does not like plays is the dangerous one. And the audience, who have paid to see a play, are on the safe side.

**Host A:** And then, the line that undercuts everything.

**Caesar:** I rather tell thee what is to be fear'd Than what I fear; for always I am Caesar. Come on my right hand, for this ear is deaf, And tell me truly what thou think'st of him.

**Host B:** For always I am Caesar. Come on my right hand, for this ear is deaf.

**Host A:** The god and the old man in two lines. He claims to be above fear, and in the very next breath he admits he cannot hear properly. Shakespeare gives the most powerful man in the world a bad ear, at the exact moment he claims to be beyond human weakness.

**Host B:** And Cassius's stories of the swim and the fever, which we might have dismissed as spite, suddenly have some confirmation. Caesar is physically frail. The question the play poses is whether that matters.

**Host A:** And then Casca. The blunt, sardonic Casca, who tells Brutus and Cassius what happened at the games. And here we get one of the play's cleverest techniques. The most important political event of the day, Caesar being offered a crown, happens offstage. We only hear Casca's version.

**Casca:** Why, there was a crown offered him: and being offered him, he put it by with the back of his hand, thus; and then the people fell a-shouting.

**Casca:** Ay, marry, was't, and he put it by thrice, every time gentler than other, and at every putting-by mine honest neighbours shouted.

**Host B:** Every time gentler than other. That is a superb piece of reporting. Caesar refuses, but each refusal is softer, as if he is hoping to be asked again.

**Host A:** And Casca adds, to my thinking, he would fain have had it. He would have liked to have it.

**Host B:** But that is Casca's thinking. We never see it. So we have to decide whether to trust a cynic. And that is the position the audience is in for the rest of the play. Every important fact reaches us through someone with an interest.

**Host A:** And then Casca describes Caesar collapsing.

**Casca:** He fell down in the market-place, and foamed at mouth, and was speechless.

**Brutus:** 'Tis very like: he hath the falling sickness.

**Cassius:** No, Caesar hath it not; but you and I, And honest Casca, we have the falling sickness.

**Host B:** The falling sickness is epilepsy. And Cassius turns it into a bitter joke. No, it is not Caesar who is falling, it is us. We are the ones falling down in front of him.

**Host A:** And this detail, the fit, is one of the things that the French thinker René Girard noticed. In A Theater of Envy, his book on Shakespeare, Girard reads Caesar as a kind of scapegoat. A society in crisis, rivalry and envy everywhere, and all of it focusing on one figure. And he points out that scapegoats in myth and ritual often have a physical mark. A limp, a deformity, a strange illness. Caesar's epilepsy is that mark.

**Host B:** And Casca's description of the crowd is fascinating in the same light.

**Casca:** If the tag-rag people did not clap him and hiss him, according as he pleased and displeased them, as they use to do the players in the theatre, I am no true man.

**Host A:** As they do the players in the theatre.

**Host B:** In a theatre. To an audience standing in the yard. Shakespeare is quietly telling the Globe audience that they are the Roman crowd. They clap and hiss Caesar the way they clap and hiss actors.

**Host A:** And remember, this was one of the first plays performed at the new Globe. A Swiss traveller, Thomas Platter, saw a performance of a tragedy about Julius Caesar on the twenty first of September 1599, at a thatched playhouse on the south bank. So the newest theatre in London was opening its doors with a play about a crowd being worked on by performers.

**Host B:** Then Casca says one more thing, almost in passing, which has become one of the most famous phrases in the language.

**Casca:** Nay, an I tell you that, I'll ne'er look you i' the face again: but those that understood him smiled at one another and shook their heads; but, for mine own part, it was Greek to me.

**Host A:** It was Greek to me. Cicero, the great orator, spoke in Greek, which the educated understood and Casca did not. Or pretended not to.

**Host B:** And it is a sly joke on Casca, because he is clearly not stupid. He may simply be refusing to report what Cicero said. Cicero, the greatest speaker in Rome, is silenced in this play by being made incomprehensible.

**Host A:** And then, the scene ends with Cassius alone. And we hear what he really thinks.

**Cassius:** Well, Brutus, thou art noble; yet, I see, Thy honourable metal may be wrought From that it is disposed: therefore it is meet That noble minds keep ever with their likes; For who so firm that cannot be seduced?

**Host B:** Thy honourable metal may be wrought. There is the metal again. Brutus is noble metal, but metal can be worked, hammered into another shape.

**Host A:** For who so firm that cannot be seduced. He is admitting that he is seducing Brutus.

**Host B:** And he tells us his plan. He will forge letters, in different handwriting, as if from several citizens, and throw them through Brutus's window at night. Letters praising Brutus's name, and hinting at Caesar's ambition.

**Host A:** So the people's voice that will persuade Brutus is a fake. It is Cassius, disguising his handwriting.

**Host B:** And the critic Hazlitt, writing in the early nineteenth century, thought this was crucial to the whole play. He said the conspiracy failed because of Brutus's generous temper and overweening confidence. Brutus believes that the people want what he wants, partly because he has been shown forged evidence that they do.

**Host A:** So we have the whole machinery of the tragedy set up in two scenes. A city on holiday. A crowd that cheers whoever is in front of it. Two officials silenced. A frail man who calls himself Caesar. A noble man who loves the name of honour. And a clever, envious man who has offered to be his mirror.

**Host B:** And it is worth asking, at the end of these scenes, whose side the play is on. And I think the honest answer is that it is on nobody's side yet. Caesar is vain, deaf, superstitious, possibly ambitious, but he has done nothing tyrannical in front of us. He refused the crown. Cassius is envious and manipulative, but he is also right that Rome is becoming one man's city. Brutus is honourable and self deceived. The commoners are witty and fickle.

**Host A:** Coleridge, who adored Shakespeare, admitted that he could not see what Shakespeare intended with Brutus. And I think that confusion is the point. The play does not let you settle.

**Host B:** Which is why it has been argued over for four hundred years.

**Host A:** Something to listen for, if you go on to the next part of the play. Count the times people speak of themselves in the third person. Caesar does it constantly. Brutus has already started. Watch how it spreads, and what it says about the men who do it.

**Host B:** Next, a night of storms, strange omens, letters through the window, and Brutus alone in his orchard deciding whether Caesar must die.

**Host A:** Thank you for listening to Books on Pods.
