# Julius Caesar 2. Storm and Conscience

**Host A:** The night before the ides of March. A thunderstorm over Rome, unlike anything anyone has seen. And a man is running through the streets with his sword drawn, out of breath, terrified. It is Casca, the blunt cynic from the last scene, who joked about Caesar's crown and Cicero's Greek. And now he is shaking.

**Casca:** I have seen tempests, when the scolding winds Have rived the knotty oaks, and I have seen The ambitious ocean swell and rage and foam, To be exalted with the threatening clouds: But never till to-night, never till now, Did I go through a tempest dropping fire.

**Host B:** A tempest dropping fire. And he lists the omens he has seen. A slave whose hand burned like twenty torches without being scorched. A lion near the Capitol that glared at him and walked past. A crowd of women who swore they saw men all in fire walking in the streets. An owl, the bird of night, hooting in the marketplace at midday.

**Host A:** And he is talking to Cicero. The great orator, who in the last scene was reduced to speaking Greek that Casca would not translate. And here, in his one real speech in the play, Cicero says something that could stand as the motto of the whole tragedy.

**Cicero:** Indeed, it is a strange-disposed time: But men may construe things after their fashion, Clean from the purpose of the things themselves.

**Host B:** Men may construe things after their fashion, clean from the purpose of the things themselves. People interpret signs to mean what they want, regardless of what the signs actually mean.

**Host A:** And then Cicero says good night and leaves, and is barely heard from again. The wisest sentence in the play is spoken by a man who then walks out of it.

**Host B:** That line has become a kind of key for critics who read the play as being about knowledge itself. The critic Fortin argued that the play explores what he called man's epistemological situation, in the manner of the French essayist Montaigne. How do we know anything? How do we read signs, faces, letters, omens? And the answer the play keeps giving is, badly, and in our own interest.

**Host A:** And the very next person to read the storm proves Cicero right. Cassius arrives, and he is not frightened at all. He is exhilarated.

**Cassius:** For my part, I have walk'd about the streets, Submitting me unto the perilous night, And, thus unbraced, Casca, as you see, Have bared my bosom to the thunder-stone; And when the cross blue lightning seem'd to open The breast of heaven, I did present myself Even in the aim and very flash of it.

**Host B:** He has been walking around in the storm with his shirt open, offering his chest to the lightning.

**Host A:** And Casca, who has just seen burning men in the street, asks why on earth he would tempt the gods like that. And Cassius reads the storm in exactly the opposite way. He says the heavens are warning Rome about a monstrous state of affairs. And what is the monstrous thing? A man, he says, no mightier than thyself or me, who has grown prodigious and fearful, like these strange eruptions.

**Host B:** So for Casca, the storm is a sign of divine anger. For Cassius, the storm is a sign that Caesar is the monster. Same lightning, two readings.

**Host A:** And the critic Maurice Charney noticed that this is how all the great images work in the play. He traced three image themes, storm, blood and fire, and argued that their meaning depends on whose side you are on. To the conspirators, the storm is a portent against Caesar. To Caesar's supporters, it is a portent against the conspirators. The play never tells you who is right.

**Host B:** And then Cassius says something which, with hindsight, is chilling.

**Cassius:** I know where I will wear this dagger then; Cassius from bondage will deliver Cassius: Therein, ye gods, you make the weak most strong; Therein, ye gods, you tyrants do defeat.

**Host A:** He means that if Caesar becomes king, he will kill himself rather than live as a slave. Cassius from bondage will deliver Cassius.

**Host B:** This is the Stoic idea of freedom. That a man who is willing to die can never truly be enslaved. And it was a real current of Roman thought. The critic Geoffrey Miles traced how deeply the play is soaked in the Stoic traditions that came down through Cicero and Seneca. Constancy. Self command. The right to choose one's own death rather than submit.

**Host A:** And the play will test that ideal very hard before it ends. Hold onto it.

**Host B:** Then Cassius recruits Casca in about thirty seconds. Casca, who was terrified a moment ago, shakes his hand and says he will go as far as anyone. And they discuss the one man they still need.

**Casca:** O, he sits high in all the people's hearts: And that which would appear offence in us, His countenance, like richest alchemy, Will change to virtue and to worthiness.

**Host A:** Brutus.

**Host B:** Brutus. And the image is alchemy. The medieval art of turning base metal into gold.

**Host A:** There is the metal again. Flavius called the commoners base metal. Cassius said Brutus's honourable metal may be wrought. And now Casca says Brutus is the alchemist who will turn their crime into virtue.

**Host B:** The critic Robert Reynolds called that alchemy image ironic. And you can see why. In Shakespeare's London, alchemy was widely mocked as a con. Ben Jonson would write a whole comedy about it a few years later, The Alchemist. So the conspirators are relying on Brutus's reputation to transform murder into virtue, by a process everyone half suspects does not work.

**Host A:** And then the forged letters. Cassius gives Cinna papers to leave in Brutus's chair, throw through his window, and stick with wax to the statue of old Brutus, the ancestor who drove out the kings.

**Host B:** Three parts of him is ours already, says Cassius. And the man entire upon the next encounter yields him ours.

**Host A:** Now Act Two. Brutus's orchard. And it is still that same night. Brutus has not slept. He calls for his servant boy, Lucius, and tells him to light a candle in his study. And then he is alone. And we hear him make up his mind.

**Brutus:** It must be by his death: and for my part, I know no personal cause to spurn at him, But for the general. He would be crown'd: How that might change his nature, there's the question. It is the bright day that brings forth the adder; And that craves wary walking.

**Host B:** Notice where he starts. It must be by his death. He begins with the conclusion. The rest of the speech is the argument for a decision he has already made.

**Host A:** That is such an important observation.

**Host B:** And watch how the argument works.

**Brutus:** And, to speak truth of Caesar, I have not known when his affections sway'd More than his reason. But 'tis a common proof, That lowliness is young ambition's ladder, Whereto the climber-upward turns his face; But when he once attains the upmost round, He then unto the ladder turns his back, Looks in the clouds, scorning the base degrees By which he did ascend. So Caesar may.

**Host A:** So Caesar may. Not so Caesar has. So Caesar may.

**Host B:** He admits, honestly, that Caesar has never let his passions overrule his reason. And then he reaches for a general rule, a common proof, that ambitious people climb a ladder and then kick it away. And he applies the rule to Caesar. So Caesar may.

**Brutus:** Then, lest he may, prevent. And, since the quarrel Will bear no colour for the thing he is, Fashion it thus; that what he is, augmented, Would run to these and these extremities: And therefore think him as a serpent's egg Which, hatch'd, would, as his kind, grow mischievous, And kill him in the shell.

**Host A:** Since the quarrel will bear no colour for the thing he is. Since there is no case against Caesar as he is now.

**Host B:** Fashion it thus. Shape the argument. And then the image of the serpent's egg.

**Host A:** And Norman Rabkin, in one of the most quoted lines of criticism on this play, said that this soliloquy shows in Brutus, quote, a capacity to be deceived by analogies of his own making.

**Host B:** Analogies of his own making. The ladder. The adder that comes out in sunshine. The serpent's egg. None of these are facts about Caesar. They are pictures Brutus paints, and then he believes the pictures.

**Host A:** That is exactly what Cicero just warned about. Men construe things after their fashion.

**Host B:** Brutus is Cicero's sentence in action. And then the letter arrives. Lucius finds it at the window. Brutus reads it by the light of the meteors in the sky.

**Brutus:** 'Brutus, thou sleep'st: awake, and see thyself. Shall Rome, et cetera. Speak, strike, redress! Brutus, thou sleep'st: awake!'

**Host A:** Shall Rome, et cetera. The letter is so vague it does not even finish its sentence.

**Host B:** And Brutus finishes it for himself.

**Brutus:** 'Shall Rome, et cetera.' Thus must I piece it out: Shall Rome stand under one man's awe? What, Rome? My ancestors did from the streets of Rome The Tarquin drive, when he was call'd a king.

**Host A:** Thus must I piece it out. He fills in the blank with his own thoughts, and then treats it as the voice of Rome.

**Host B:** And remember who wrote it. Cassius. In disguised handwriting. So the voice of the Roman people that Brutus thinks he is hearing is his manipulator, and the words he thinks are theirs are his own.

**Host A:** That is a perfect little machine of self deception. Brutus is persuaded by a letter he partly writes himself.

**Host B:** And notice his ancestors again. My ancestors drove out the Tarquin. Geoffrey Miles's point about family pride. Brutus cannot think about Rome without thinking about being a Brutus.

**Host A:** And then the most remarkable lines in the scene. Brutus describes what it feels like to have decided to do something terrible, but not to have done it yet.

**Brutus:** Since Cassius first did whet me against Caesar, I have not slept. Between the acting of a dreadful thing And the first motion, all the interim is Like a phantasma, or a hideous dream: The Genius and the mortal instruments Are then in council; and the state of man, Like to a little kingdom, suffers then The nature of an insurrection.

**Host B:** The state of man, like to a little kingdom, suffers then the nature of an insurrection.

**Host A:** His own body and mind are in rebellion. A civil war inside one man, before the civil war in Rome.

**Host B:** And Marvin Vawter wrote about exactly this division in Brutus. The divided mind and body, the man who is, in Vawter's words, unable to see himself. Brutus can describe his inner insurrection perfectly. What he cannot see is that it is telling him something.

**Host A:** That speech is also so close to Hamlet, which Shakespeare would write very soon after. The interval between deciding and doing.

**Host B:** Then the knocking at the gate. Lucius goes to see. And comes back.

**Lucius:** No, sir; their hats are pluck'd about their ears, And half their faces buried in their cloaks, That by no means I may discover them By any mark of favour.

**Host A:** Their hats are plucked about their ears. Which caused a small eighteenth century scandal among editors.

**Host B:** It did. Alexander Pope, when he edited Shakespeare, could not bear the idea of Roman patricians wearing hats. He believed that Romans did not wear hats. So he printed a dash instead of the word. Their dash is plucked about their ears.

**Host A:** He censored a hat.

**Host B:** He censored a hat. And Lewis Theobald, who edited Shakespeare after Pope and loved to correct him, called that kind of nitpicking hypercritical. Which it was. But it shows you something real. Shakespeare's Rome is full of Elizabethan things. Hats, doublets, a clock that will strike in a moment. It is ancient Rome as if it were happening in London.

**Host A:** And then Brutus, alone for a second before they come in, speaks to the conspiracy itself.

**Brutus:** They are the faction. O conspiracy, Shamest thou to show thy dangerous brow by night, When evils are most free? O, then by day Where wilt thou find a cavern dark enough To mask thy monstrous visage? Seek none, conspiracy; Hide it in smiles and affability.

**Host B:** Monstrous visage. He knows what this is. He calls it monstrous. And then he invites it in.

**Host A:** And then comes the meeting. And here I think the play gives us a brilliant study of how Brutus actually behaves as a leader. Because he makes three decisions, one after another, and overrules Cassius every time.

**Host B:** The first is about an oath. Cassius suggests they swear to their resolution. And Brutus refuses.

**Brutus:** No, not an oath: if not the face of men, The sufferance of our souls, the time's abuse, If these be motives weak, break off betimes, And every man hence to his idle bed.

**Brutus:** What need we any spur but our own cause, To prick us to redress? what other bond Than secret Romans, that have spoke the word, And will not palter?

**Host A:** Secret Romans who have given their word and will not palter. Will not shift or equivocate.

**Host B:** It is a magnificent speech. And it is also, in its way, an insult to everyone in the room. If you need an oath, he says, you are a priest or a coward or a feeble old man. Our cause is so noble that it does not need to be sworn.

**Host A:** And the irony is that oaths are exactly what hold conspiracies together. Brutus refuses the one practical safeguard because it is not noble enough.

**Host B:** The second decision is about Cicero. The others want to bring him in. His silver hairs, says Metellus, will buy us a good reputation. Our youth and wildness will be buried in his gravity.

**Brutus:** O, name him not: let us not break with him; For he will never follow any thing That other men begin.

**Host A:** And everyone instantly agrees. Then leave him out. Indeed he is not fit.

**Host B:** Which is a very funny moment, if you watch it. Every man who was enthusiastically for Cicero a second ago is now enthusiastically against him, because Brutus has spoken.

**Host A:** So we see what the conspiracy really is. Not a group of equals. A group who have borrowed Brutus's authority and will now follow it wherever it goes.

**Host B:** And then the third and fatal decision. Cassius says they should kill Mark Antony too. He is too clever, too dangerous, too close to Caesar. And Brutus says no.

**Brutus:** Let us be sacrificers, but not butchers, Caius. We all stand up against the spirit of Caesar; And in the spirit of men there is no blood: O, that we then could come by Caesar's spirit, And not dismember Caesar! But, alas, Caesar must bleed for it! And, gentle friends, Let's kill him boldly, but not wrathfully; Let's carve him as a dish fit for the gods, Not hew him as a carcass fit for hounds.

**Host A:** Let's carve him as a dish fit for the gods. That is the most disturbing image in the scene.

**Host B:** And this is where Brents Stirling's work is so useful. Stirling argued that Brutus tries to dignify the assassination by raising it to the level of rite and ceremony. A ritual. A sacrifice. And Brutus goes on.

**Brutus:** This shall make Our purpose necessary and not envious: Which so appearing to the common eyes, We shall be call'd purgers, not murderers. And for Mark Antony, think not of him; For he can do no more than Caesar's arm When Caesar's head is off.

**Host A:** We shall be called purgers, not murderers. Which so appearing to the common eyes. He is thinking about how it will look.

**Host B:** And that is the deepest irony of the whole speech. Brutus, the man who refused to swear an oath because his cause was so pure, is now planning the public relations of the murder. It must appear necessary and not envious. To the common eyes.

**Host A:** And Naomi Conn Liebler links this to the Lupercal we saw in the first act. A festival of ritual purification. Brutus wants the killing to be a Lupercal for the republic. A cleansing.

**Host B:** And on Antony, he is catastrophically wrong. Antony can do no more than Caesar's arm when Caesar's head is off. In two acts, Antony will destroy all of them.

**Host A:** And Trebonius adds, there is no fear in him, let him not die, for he will live and laugh at this hereafter. Which is a terrible thing to say, given what is coming.

**Host B:** And then, the clock.

**Cassius:** The clock hath stricken three.

**Host A:** The famous anachronism. There were no mechanical striking clocks in ancient Rome.

**Host B:** Generations of schoolteachers have pointed it out as a howler. But I do not think Shakespeare cared, and I think the effect is deliberate in its way. This conspiracy is happening in clock time, Elizabethan time, the audience's time. The Roman story is being acted now, in London, with hats and clocks.

**Host A:** And then Decius says something wonderful about how he will get Caesar to the Capitol, even if Caesar is frightened by the omens.

**Decius:** Never fear that: if he be so resolved, I can o'ersway him; for he loves to hear That unicorns may be betray'd with trees, And bears with glasses, elephants with holes, Lions with toils and men with flatterers; But when I tell him he hates flatterers, He says he does, being then most flattered.

**Host B:** That is a superb piece of psychology. You trap unicorns by getting them to charge into trees, bears with mirrors, elephants with pits, lions with nets, and men with flattery. And the best flattery of all is to tell a man he is too wise to be flattered.

**Host A:** He says he does, being then most flattered.

**Host B:** Which, notice, is exactly what Cassius did to Brutus. He told Brutus that he was the one honest man in Rome. And Brutus believed it, being then most flattered.

**Host A:** The conspirators leave. And the next small moment is one I love. Brutus looks at Lucius, his servant boy, who has fallen asleep.

**Host B:** He envies the boy. He says something like, enjoy the honey heavy dew of slumber. You have no figures, no fantasies, which busy care draws in the brains of men, and so you sleep so soundly.

**Host A:** No figures and no fantasies. Exactly what Brutus has been tormented by all night. The ladder, the adder, the serpent's egg.

**Host B:** And then Portia comes out into the night garden. Brutus's wife. Daughter of Cato, the great Stoic republican who killed himself rather than submit to Caesar. And she will not be sent back to bed.

**Portia:** Within the bond of marriage, tell me, Brutus, Is it excepted I should know no secrets That appertain to you? Am I yourself But, as it were, in sort or limitation, To keep with you at meals, comfort your bed, And talk to you sometimes? Dwell I but in the suburbs Of your good pleasure? If it be no more, Portia is Brutus' harlot, not his wife.

**Host A:** Dwell I but in the suburbs of your good pleasure. In Shakespeare's London, the suburbs, outside the city walls, were where the brothels and the theatres were.

**Host B:** So she is saying, am I only on the outskirts of your life? If so, I am not your wife, I am your mistress.

**Host A:** That is a fierce speech. And Brutus answers tenderly. You are my true and honourable wife, as dear to me as the ruddy drops that visit my sad heart.

**Host B:** But he still does not tell her. And so she does something extraordinary.

**Portia:** I grant I am a woman; but withal A woman well-reputed, Cato's daughter. Think you I am no stronger than my sex, Being so father'd and so husbanded? Tell me your counsels, I will not disclose 'em: I have made strong proof of my constancy, Giving myself a voluntary wound Here, in the thigh: can I bear that with patience, And not my husband's secrets?

**Host A:** She has stabbed herself in the thigh to prove she can keep a secret.

**Host B:** And this is the scene that Coppélia Kahn has made central to modern readings of the play. Kahn reads Portia's self wounding as a woman's attempt to match a man's constancy. In a world where Roman virtue is manly endurance, the only way for Portia to prove she is worthy of the secret is to wound her own body and bear the pain without complaint.

**Host A:** She proves she is a Roman man.

**Host B:** And in Kahn's reading, it is a kind of mirror of what will happen to Caesar tomorrow. Portia wounds herself to enter the circle of men. The men will wound Caesar to drive him out of it.

**Host A:** And it is one of the most painful moments in the play, because it works. Brutus is moved. He says, O ye gods, render me worthy of this noble wife. And he promises to tell her everything.

**Host B:** And then there is a knock, and he sends her away, and we never see him tell her. The next time we hear of Portia is something else entirely.

**Host A:** And then one last visitor, who is a kind of grotesque mirror of the whole conspiracy. Caius Ligarius, a sick man, wearing a kerchief around his head.

**Ligarius:** I am not sick, if Brutus have in hand Any exploit worthy the name of honour.

**Host B:** Worthy the name of honour. There it is again. The name.

**Ligarius:** By all the gods that Romans bow before, I here discard my sickness! Soul of Rome! Brave son, derived from honourable loins! Thou, like an exorcist, hast conjured up My mortified spirit.

**Host A:** Brave son, derived from honourable loins. Ligarius cannot see Brutus without seeing his ancestry.

**Host B:** And Brutus says, a piece of work that will make sick men whole. And Ligarius, a sick man, replies, but are not some whole that we must make sick? Meaning Caesar.

**Host A:** Healing and killing, in one breath. Which is exactly Brutus's fantasy. The murder as medicine. A purge.

**Host B:** So by the end of this night, look what we have. A storm that every man reads his own way. A conspiracy that refuses oaths, excludes the wisest man in Rome and spares the most dangerous one. A noble leader who has persuaded himself with analogies and a forged letter. A wife who has wounded herself to share a secret she is not told. And a sick man cured by the mere name of honour.

**Host A:** Where do the critics come down on Brutus in this act?

**Host B:** Hazlitt blamed Brutus's generous temper and overweening confidence. And you can see both in this scene. He is generous to Antony, which kills them. He is confident that their cause is so noble it needs no oath. John Anson went further and argued that under Brutus's love of country lies envy of Caesar. I am less sure about that. I think what the scene shows most clearly is something the critic Geoffrey Miles noticed. That Brutus seems unaware how much personal and family pride is driving him.

**Host A:** I think the scene is kinder to Brutus than that. Because he suffers. He has not slept. He feels the insurrection inside himself. Cassius never doubts. Casca never doubts. Brutus doubts the whole night, and then acts anyway, and that is exactly what makes him tragic and not just foolish.

**Host B:** That is fair. He is the only conspirator with a conscience. And the conscience does not stop him. It just makes him suffer while he does it.

**Host A:** Something to listen for. Count the decisions Brutus makes in this act against Cassius's advice. The oath, Cicero, Antony. Then, as the play goes on, watch how many more there will be. And ask yourself each time whether Brutus is right.

**Host B:** Next, the morning of the ides. Caesar's wife has had a dream. And Decius has an interpretation ready.

**Host A:** Thank you for listening to Books on Pods.
