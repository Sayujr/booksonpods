# Julius Caesar 3. The Ides

**Host A:** Still the same night of storms. Caesar's house. The ruler of the Roman world comes out in his nightgown, unable to sleep.

**Caesar:** Nor heaven nor earth have been at peace to-night: Thrice hath Calpurnia in her sleep cried out, 'Help, ho! they murder Caesar!' Who's within?

**Host B:** Three times, in her sleep, his wife has screamed that they are murdering Caesar. And notice the parallel. In the last scene, Brutus could not sleep either. Two great men awake on the same night, in two houses, both being drawn toward the same morning.

**Host A:** And Norman Rabkin made exactly that parallel central to his reading. He paired the orchard scene, Brutus's night, with this scene, Caesar's night, and saw in both men the same mixture, what he called a balance of perception and self righteous blindness. Each sees something true. Each is blind to something fatal.

**Host B:** Caesar sends a servant to the priests to make a sacrifice and read the omens. And then Calpurnia comes in, and she is not asking. She is telling.

**Calpurnia:** What mean you, Caesar? think you to walk forth? You shall not stir out of your house to-day.

**Caesar:** Caesar shall forth: the things that threaten'd me Ne'er look'd but on my back; when they shall see The face of Caesar, they are vanished.

**Host A:** Caesar shall forth. Third person again. As if Caesar is a statue that walks.

**Host B:** And Calpurnia describes the omens, which are even wilder than Casca's. A lioness giving birth in the streets. Graves opening. Fiery warriors fighting in the clouds and drizzling blood on the Capitol. Ghosts shrieking in the streets. And she says she has never cared for omens before, but now they frighten her.

**Host A:** And Caesar gives what I think is the most dignified speech he has in the whole play.

**Caesar:** What can be avoided Whose end is purposed by the mighty gods? Yet Caesar shall go forth; for these predictions Are to the world in general as to Caesar.

**Calpurnia:** When beggars die, there are no comets seen; The heavens themselves blaze forth the death of princes.

**Caesar:** Cowards die many times before their deaths; The valiant never taste of death but once. Of all the wonders that I yet have heard, It seems to me most strange that men should fear; Seeing that death, a necessary end, Will come when it will come.

**Host B:** Cowards die many times before their deaths. The valiant never taste of death but once. It is magnificent. And it is genuinely brave.

**Host A:** And Calpurnia's line is magnificent too. When beggars die, there are no comets seen. The heavens themselves blaze forth the death of princes. She is saying, these omens are not for everyone. They are for you.

**Host B:** And then the servant comes back from the priests.

**Caesar:** What say the augurers?

**Servant:** They would not have you to stir forth to-day. Plucking the entrails of an offering forth, They could not find a heart within the beast.

**Host A:** An animal without a heart. The worst possible omen.

**Host B:** And watch what Caesar does with it. Exactly what Cicero warned. He construes it after his fashion.

**Caesar:** The gods do this in shame of cowardice: Caesar should be a beast without a heart, If he should stay at home to-day for fear. No, Caesar shall not: danger knows full well That Caesar is more dangerous than he: We are two lions litter'd in one day, And I the elder and more terrible.

**Host A:** We are two lions littered in one day, and I the elder and more terrible.

**Host B:** He is saying that Caesar and Danger are twin lions, and Caesar is the older and scarier one. Which is either sublime or absurd, depending on how you play it.

**Host A:** And this is where M. W. MacCallum, writing in 1910, saw something important. He noticed Caesar's constant harping on his own fearlessness, and said it should make us suspicious. And he saw in Caesar a touch of self deception as well as of superstition. A man who keeps telling everyone he is not afraid.

**Host B:** And then, in the very next moment, he gives in to his wife after all. Calpurnia kneels, and Caesar says, fine, Mark Antony shall say I am not well, and for thy humour I will stay at home.

**Host A:** So the brave speech, and then the retreat. Both are real.

**Host B:** And then Decius arrives. The flatterer. The man who said, I can o'ersway him, tell him he hates flatterers and he is most flattered. And Caesar tells him, quite proudly, the real reason he is staying home. His wife's dream.

**Caesar:** She dreamt to-night she saw my statua, Which, like a fountain with an hundred spouts, Did run pure blood: and many lusty Romans Came smiling, and did bathe their hands in it.

**Host A:** A statue of Caesar spouting blood from a hundred holes, and Romans smiling and washing their hands in it. Which is, almost exactly, what will happen in a few hours.

**Host B:** And Decius reinterprets it on the spot.

**Decius:** This dream is all amiss interpreted; It was a vision fair and fortunate: Your statue spouting blood in many pipes, In which so many smiling Romans bathed, Signifies that from you great Rome shall suck Reviving blood, and that great men shall press For tinctures, stains, relics and cognizance.

**Host A:** From you great Rome shall suck reviving blood. And this is one of the richest moments in the play for modern criticism, because three different scholars have read that image three different ways, and all of them are illuminating.

**Host B:** Gail Kern Paster, in a famous essay from 1985, argued that Decius, in her words, allegorizes Caesar as a lactating figure. Rome sucks from Caesar like a baby at the breast. And Paster's larger argument is that the conspirators keep imagining Caesar's body as a woman's body. Open, bleeding, nursing. A body that can be entered.

**Host A:** Coppélia Kahn reads the same image differently. She hears the founding myth of Rome in it. Romulus and Remus, the twins who founded Rome, suckled by a she wolf. Decius turns Caesar into the nurturing mother of the city.

**Host B:** And David Kaula reads it in terms of religion. Great men shall press for tinctures, stains, relics. Tinctures and stains are cloths dipped in a martyr's blood. Relics are fragments of a saint's body. And Kaula pointed out that in Shakespeare's England, English Catholics were secretly collecting exactly those things from Catholic martyrs who had been executed by the Protestant state.

**Host A:** So Decius's flattering interpretation is full of Catholic martyrdom.

**Host B:** And Kaula went further, and argued that the blood imagery in the play makes Caesar, in his words, a redeemer who voluntarily sheds his blood. Caesar as a sort of Christ figure. Which is a startling reading of a Roman tyrant.

**Host A:** And it is exactly the reading Brutus wanted. A sacrifice. A dish fit for the gods. Except Decius means it as a lie, and it comes true in a way none of them intend.

**Host B:** And then the final push. Decius tells Caesar the Senate has decided to give him a crown today. And if Caesar stays at home because of his wife's dreams, people will say, let us break up the Senate till Caesar's wife has better dreams.

**Host A:** Mockery. The one thing Caesar cannot bear.

**Caesar:** How foolish do your fears seem now, Calpurnia! I am ashamed I did yield to them. Give me my robe, for I will go.

**Host B:** And then the conspirators arrive to escort him. And Caesar offers them wine. And Brutus, aside, says that every like is not the same. That it grieves him that these men look like friends and are not. And Caesar, warmly, calls them good friends.

**Host A:** There is a short scene next that I always find unbearably tense. A teacher of rhetoric called Artemidorus stands in the street and reads aloud a letter he has written to Caesar.

**Host B:** He names every one of them. Beware of Brutus, take heed of Cassius, come not near Casca, have an eye to Cinna, trust not Trebonius. And he says, there is but one mind in all these men, and it is bent against Caesar.

**Artemidorus:** Here will I stand till Caesar pass along, And as a suitor will I give him this. My heart laments that virtue cannot live Out of the teeth of emulation. If thou read this, O Caesar, thou mayst live; If not, the Fates with traitors do contrive.

**Host A:** Virtue cannot live out of the teeth of emulation. Emulation meant envious rivalry. Goodness cannot survive in a world of jealous competitors.

**Host B:** And that word emulation is the key to René Girard's reading. Girard called his book on Shakespeare A Theater of Envy. And his argument is that Shakespeare's plays are driven by mimetic rivalry. People wanting what others have, copying each other, competing, until the rivalry has to discharge itself on a single victim. Artemidorus has just described Girard's whole theory in two lines.

**Host A:** And then Portia. In a tiny scene, she sends the boy Lucius running to the Senate and then cannot tell him what to do there, because she cannot give away the secret Brutus has finally told her.

**Portia:** O constancy, be strong upon my side, Set a huge mountain 'tween my heart and tongue! I have a man's mind, but a woman's might. How hard it is for women to keep counsel!

**Host B:** I have a man's mind, but a woman's might. That line is Kahn's whole argument in a sentence. Portia has made herself a Roman in mind, but her body betrays her. She nearly faints.

**Host A:** And it is very sad, because it shows that the secret has not given her strength. It has wrecked her. The wound in her thigh was supposed to prove she could bear anything. She cannot bear this.

**Host B:** And now the Capitol. The ides of March. And the play has a wonderful sense of timing here. Caesar enters in procession and sees the Soothsayer in the crowd.

**Caesar:** The ides of March are come.

**Soothsayer:** Ay, Caesar; but not gone.

**Host A:** Ay, Caesar, but not gone. One of the great comebacks in drama.

**Host B:** And then Artemidorus pushes through and tries to give Caesar his letter.

**Artemidorus:** O Caesar, read mine first; for mine's a suit That touches Caesar nearer: read it, great Caesar.

**Caesar:** What touches us ourself shall be last served.

**Host A:** What touches us ourself shall be last served. Caesar's own affairs will come last.

**Host B:** Which is, on the face of it, a noble statement. Public business before private. And it kills him. If he had been a little more selfish, he would have read the letter and lived.

**Host A:** That is a beautiful irony. Caesar's one moment of public spirit is fatal.

**Host B:** And then a near disaster for the conspirators. A senator called Popilius Lena says to Cassius, I wish your enterprise today may thrive. And then walks over to talk to Caesar.

**Host A:** And Cassius panics completely. He says, if this is known, Cassius or Caesar will never come back, for I will kill myself.

**Host B:** And Brutus is calm. Cassius, be constant. Popilius Lena speaks not of our purposes, for look, he smiles, and Caesar doth not change.

**Host A:** Be constant. Brutus's word. And it will be Caesar's word in a moment too.

**Host B:** Trebonius draws Antony out of the way, as planned. And the trap closes. Metellus Cimber kneels to beg Caesar to recall his banished brother. And the others join him, kneeling, one after another, and Caesar refuses each of them with more and more grandeur.

**Caesar:** Thy brother by decree is banished: If thou dost bend and pray and fawn for him, I spurn thee like a cur out of my way. Know, Caesar doth not wrong, nor without cause Will he be satisfied.

**Host A:** Caesar doth not wrong, nor without cause will he be satisfied.

**Host B:** And that line has a famous history. Ben Jonson, Shakespeare's friend and rival, remembered it differently. In his notebooks, published after his death, Jonson quotes Shakespeare as having written, Caesar did never wrong, but with just cause. And Jonson calls lines like that ridiculous.

**Host A:** Because if you never do wrong except with just cause, then you have done wrong, but you had a reason. It is a contradiction.

**Host B:** Or, a brilliant paradox about tyranny. A ruler who does wrong and always has a reason. Which is how every tyrant talks. But the text printed in the First Folio in 1623 does not have Jonson's version. It has the smoother line. And many scholars think Shakespeare, or someone, revised the line after it was mocked.

**Host A:** So we may be hearing a line that was edited because Ben Jonson laughed at it.

**Host B:** Quite possibly. And then, the speech every conspirator has been waiting for.

**Caesar:** But I am constant as the northern star, Of whose true-fix'd and resting quality There is no fellow in the firmament. The skies are painted with unnumber'd sparks, They are all fire and every one doth shine, But there's but one in all doth hold his place: So in the world; 'tis furnish'd well with men, And men are flesh and blood, and apprehensive; Yet in the number I do know but one That unassailable holds on his rank, Unshaked of motion: and that I am he.

**Host A:** Constant as the northern star. The one star that does not move. And men are flesh and blood, but he is not.

**Host B:** Seconds before he is turned into flesh and blood on the floor.

**Host A:** Wilson Knight called the imagery of this play all visual, vivid. And there is no more vivid image than this. A man claiming to be a fixed star, surrounded by kneeling men with knives in their cloaks.

**Caesar:** Hence! wilt thou lift up Olympus?

**Decius:** Great Caesar.

**Caesar:** Doth not Brutus bootless kneel?

**Host B:** Doth not Brutus bootless kneel. Even Brutus kneels in vain. Caesar's last words to Brutus before the knives are a compliment. Brutus is the one man whose kneeling should have moved him.

**Casca:** Speak, hands for me!

**Host A:** And Casca strikes first. From behind, according to Plutarch, in the neck.

**Caesar:** Et tu, Brute! Then fall, Caesar.

**Host B:** Et tu, Brute. You too, Brutus. Then fall, Caesar.

**Host A:** And the famous Latin is not from any ancient source. Plutarch says Caesar said nothing, and that when he saw Brutus among the attackers he pulled his toga over his head. The Roman biographer Suetonius reports a rumour that Caesar said, in Greek, kai su, teknon. You too, child. But Suetonius himself doubted it.

**Host B:** And the Latin phrase, et tu Brute, was already circulating in English drama before Shakespeare used it. It appears in a play printed in 1595, The True Tragedy of Richard Duke of York, which is an early version of Shakespeare's own Henry the Sixth, Part Three. So Shakespeare's audience may already have known the line, and been waiting for it.

**Host A:** And then fall, Caesar. Third person, to the very end. He commands his own death, as if it were an order to a subordinate.

**Host B:** And the conspirators immediately start shouting.

**Cinna:** Liberty! Freedom! Tyranny is dead! Run hence, proclaim, cry it about the streets.

**Host A:** And here is something the play does that is often missed. Everyone runs. The senators flee in terror. The people in the street panic. The conspirators thought the city would rise up in gratitude. Instead, Rome runs away.

**Host B:** And so Brutus does the thing he imagined in the orchard. The ritual.

**Brutus:** Stoop, Romans, stoop, And let us bathe our hands in Caesar's blood Up to the elbows, and besmear our swords: Then walk we forth, even to the market-place, And, waving our red weapons o'er our heads, Let's all cry 'Peace, freedom and liberty!'

**Host A:** Bathe our hands in Caesar's blood up to the elbows. That is exactly Calpurnia's dream. The smiling Romans washing their hands in the blood from the statue.

**Host B:** The dream has come true, and the man fulfilling it is the noblest Roman of them all. And this is where the critics who read the play's blood imagery come into their own. Leo Kirschbaum, in 1949, insisted that this blood is not metaphorical at all. It is real stage blood. Actors' arms dripping red. And he said the smeared blood is the symbol and mark of the blood and destruction that will continue through the rest of the play.

**Host A:** And Maurice Charney called this moment Brutus's fearful blood ritual.

**Host B:** And Mark Rose called the whole murder a kind of political Mass. The breaking of a sacred body, the sharing of its blood, to create a new community. And Stephen Greenblatt, in a related argument, suggested that Elizabethan theatre took sacred signs and emptied them out, even as it seemed to honour them. You see the shape of a sacrament. And you see that it is a slaughter.

**Host A:** And then Cassius says the lines that seem to step outside the play entirely.

**Cassius:** Stoop, then, and wash. How many ages hence Shall this our lofty scene be acted over In states unborn and accents yet unknown!

**Brutus:** How many times shall Caesar bleed in sport, That now on Pompey's basis lies along No worthier than the dust!

**Host B:** Caesar bleeding in sport. In play. On stage. And the audience at the Globe is watching it happen. They are the state unborn.

**Host A:** And the line about Pompey's basis. Caesar has fallen at the foot of Pompey's statue. Plutarch says that the statue was spattered with his blood, as if Pompey himself were taking revenge on his old enemy.

**Host B:** So Caesar, who rode into Rome at the start of the play in triumph over Pompey's blood, now lies in his own blood at Pompey's feet. The circle closes.

**Host A:** And Cassius ends the exchange: so often shall the knot of us be called the men that gave their country liberty.

**Host B:** Which is exactly what they are not called, for most of history. They are called the assassins. The irony is total.

**Host A:** And then a servant arrives from Mark Antony. And here the play turns, in a few lines, from one man's tragedy into another man's triumph. Antony sends a message, very carefully worded. If Brutus will guarantee his safety and explain why Caesar deserved to die, Antony will follow him.

**Host B:** And Brutus, of course, agrees. He thinks Antony is just Caesar's arm. And Cassius says, quietly, I still fear him.

**Host A:** And then Antony comes in. And sees the body.

**Antony:** O mighty Caesar! dost thou lie so low? Are all thy conquests, glories, triumphs, spoils, Shrunk to this little measure? Fare thee well.

**Host B:** Shrunk to this little measure. The Colossus who bestrode the world, reduced to a body on the floor.

**Host A:** And then Antony does something astonishing. He invites them to kill him too. He says there is no hour so fit as this, no instrument so worthy as their swords, made rich with Caesar's blood.

**Host B:** Which is either genuine grief or brilliant theatre. Probably both. He is offering them the chance to do it, knowing that Brutus will not. And Brutus answers with a speech that is almost unbearably earnest.

**Brutus:** O Antony, beg not your death of us. Though now we must appear bloody and cruel, As, by our hands and this our present act, You see we do, yet see you but our hands And this the bleeding business they have done: Our hearts you see not; they are pitiful.

**Host A:** Our hearts you see not. You see the hands. Look at the hearts.

**Host B:** But nobody can see hearts. That is the whole problem of the play. The crowd will see hands. Red hands.

**Host A:** And then Antony asks for the hands.

**Antony:** Let each man render me his bloody hand: First, Marcus Brutus, will I shake with you; Next, Caius Cassius, do I take your hand; Now, Decius Brutus, yours: now yours, Metellus; Yours, Cinna; and, my valiant Casca, yours; Though last, not last in love, yours, good Trebonius.

**Host B:** He shakes every bloody hand, one by one, naming each man.

**Host A:** And think about what that does physically. His own hands are now covered in Caesar's blood too. He has marked himself. But he has also, very precisely, counted them. He now knows the name of every man who did it.

**Host B:** It is like a roll call before an execution. And then he turns to the body and speaks to it, in front of them.

**Antony:** Pardon me, Julius! Here wast thou bay'd, brave hart; Here didst thou fall; and here thy hunters stand, Sign'd in thy spoil, and crimson'd in thy lethe. O world, thou wast the forest to this hart; And this, indeed, O world, the heart of thee. How like a deer, strucken by many princes, Dost thou here lie!

**Host A:** A hart, a deer. And the hunters stand around it, signed in its blood.

**Host B:** And notice that pun, hart and heart. The forest of the world, and Caesar was its heart.

**Host A:** And this is Charney's point exactly. Brutus wanted a sacrifice. Antony's image is a hunt. Not a priest at an altar. Hunters standing over a deer they have chased down. Antony is already redescribing the murder, in front of the murderers, and they do not notice.

**Host B:** Cassius notices. He says, Mark Antony, are you with us or not? And Antony says he will be, if they give him reasons. And then he asks for one more thing. To speak at Caesar's funeral.

**Cassius:** You know not what you do: do not consent That Antony speak in his funeral: Know you how much the people may be moved By that which he will utter?

**Host A:** You know not what you do.

**Host B:** And Brutus overrules Cassius. Again. That is the fourth time. The oath, Cicero, sparing Antony, and now letting him speak. Brutus says he will go into the pulpit first, explain everything, and then Antony can speak, but only by their permission, and only good things about Caesar, and without blaming the conspirators.

**Host A:** It is so reasonable. And so fatal.

**Host B:** Hazlitt's overweening confidence. Brutus believes that if he explains his reasons clearly, the people will understand. He has no idea what Antony is about to do.

**Host A:** And then they leave. And Antony is alone with the body. And now we hear what he really thinks.

**Antony:** O, pardon me, thou bleeding piece of earth, That I am meek and gentle with these butchers! Thou art the ruins of the noblest man That ever lived in the tide of times. Woe to the hand that shed this costly blood!

**Host B:** These butchers. There it is. Brutus said, let us be sacrificers, not butchers. And the first word Antony uses, alone, is butchers.

**Host A:** And then he prophesies.

**Antony:** Over thy wounds now do I prophesy, Which, like dumb mouths, do ope their ruby lips, To beg the voice and utterance of my tongue, A curse shall light upon the limbs of men; Domestic fury and fierce civil strife Shall cumber all the parts of Italy.

**Antony:** And Caesar's spirit, ranging for revenge, With Ate by his side come hot from hell, Shall in these confines with a monarch's voice Cry 'Havoc,' and let slip the dogs of war; That this foul deed shall smell above the earth With carrion men, groaning for burial.

**Host B:** The wounds are dumb mouths, with ruby lips, begging him to speak for them. And that image is the key to the next scene. Antony is going to lend his voice to the wounds. He is going to make them talk.

**Host A:** And Ate is the Greek goddess of ruin and reckless destruction. Caesar's spirit and the goddess of ruin together. Cry havoc, the signal for an army to plunder without restraint. And let slip the dogs of war.

**Host B:** And Caesar's spirit. Brutus wanted to kill the spirit and leave the man. Antony has just told us that the spirit is about to be set loose.

**Host A:** So where are we, at the end of the most famous scene in all of Shakespeare's Roman plays?

**Host B:** I think the deepest thing this act shows is how badly every character reads the signs. Caesar reads the heartless beast as a call to courage. Decius reads the bloody statue as a blessing. Caesar ignores the letter that would save him because it touches him personally. Brutus reads Popilius Lena's smile correctly, and then reads Antony completely wrongly. Cassius reads Antony correctly, and is overruled. And only Antony reads everything right.

**Host A:** And the critic J. E. Phillips put the whole thing into one sentence. He called Caesar's spirit the nemesis against which Brutus's efforts, however highly motivated, are of no avail. And you can feel that nemesis being born in this scene. In the hands Antony shakes, in the wounds he makes speak, in the word butchers.

**Host B:** And I think the most remarkable thing about the scene is how it makes us complicit. Cassius says, how many ages hence shall this our lofty scene be acted over. And we, the audience, are the ones it is being acted over for. We are watching Caesar bleed in sport. We are the reason the scene exists.

**Host A:** Something to listen for. Brutus says, our hearts you see not. Watch how, in the next act, everything depends on what the crowd can see. Not hearts. Hands, wounds, a cloak, a will. The play is about to become a contest of what can be shown.

**Host B:** Next, the forum. Two speeches. One in prose, one in verse. And Rome changes its mind.

**Host A:** Thank you for listening to Books on Pods.
