# Julius Caesar 4. Two Speeches

**Host A:** The Forum in Rome, an hour or two after the murder. The crowd is frightened, angry, and demanding answers. Cassius takes half of them off to hear his explanation in another street. And Brutus climbs into the public pulpit to speak to the rest. And what he says, and then what Mark Antony says after him, is perhaps the most famous demonstration in all of literature of how persuasion works.

**Host B:** And Shakespeare stages it as a contest. Two speeches, back to back, to the same audience, about the same body. And the audience in the theatre gets to judge them too.

**Host A:** Before we hear them, a word about where these speeches come from, because it is surprisingly interesting. Plutarch, Shakespeare's main source, tells us very little about what was said. He says Brutus spoke and was heard without much warmth, and then Antony read the will and showed them Caesar's bloody gown, and they went wild and burned the body on benches and tables pulled from the forum.

**Host B:** The Roman biographer Suetonius actually says no formal eulogy was given at all. And the classical scholar Aldo Setaioli, in an article from 2017, argued that Shakespeare probably also used a Tudor English translation of another Greek historian, Appian, who gives a much more theatrical account of Antony's speech. And Setaioli suggests that the line I come to bury Caesar, not to praise him, may even be Shakespeare's playful answer to Suetonius saying there was no praise.

**Host A:** So the speeches themselves are almost entirely Shakespeare's invention. He took a historical fact, a crowd turned by a funeral, and wrote the speeches that would explain it.

**Host B:** And there is one more detail from Plutarch that Shakespeare uses brilliantly. Plutarch says that when Brutus wrote letters in Greek, he affected the brevity of the Spartans. The clipped, pithy style that the Greeks called laconic, after Laconia, the region around Sparta. Plutarch even quotes a couple of Brutus's short, sharp letters.

**Host A:** So Shakespeare knew that the historical Brutus prized brevity and balance. And he gives him a speech built exactly that way. And here it is. Listen to the shape of it.

**Brutus:** Romans, countrymen, and lovers! hear me for my cause, and be silent, that you may hear: believe me for mine honour, and have respect to mine honour, that you may believe: censure me in your wisdom, and awake your senses, that you may the better judge.

**Host B:** Hear me for my cause, and be silent that you may hear. Believe me for mine honour, and respect mine honour that you may believe. Censure me in your wisdom, and awake your senses that you may better judge.

**Host A:** Every clause loops back on itself. A, B, B, A. Hear, silent, silent, hear. Believe, honour, honour, believe.

**Host B:** That figure is called chiasmus, after the Greek letter chi, which is shaped like an X. The words cross over. And Shakespeare's audience would have recognised it. Grammar school boys in Elizabethan England were drilled in exactly these rhetorical figures, from Latin manuals and from books like Erasmus's guide to style. T. W. Baldwin's great study of what Shakespeare learned at school showed just how much classical rhetoric was hammered into boys of his generation.

**Host A:** And Garry Wills, in his book Rome and Rhetoric, which is entirely about this play, has a brilliant observation about it. He says that Brutus's chiasmus circles back upon itself, rather than moving an argument forward. It goes round and round. And Wills says that what Brutus is really asking the audience to do is, in his words, to come into his own conviction of his own perfection.

**Host B:** Believe me for mine honour. Not believe me because of evidence. Believe me because I am honourable.

**Host A:** And then the heart of the speech.

**Brutus:** If there be any in this assembly, any dear friend of Caesar's, to him I say, that Brutus' love to Caesar was no less than his. If then that friend demand why Brutus rose against Caesar, this is my answer: Not that I loved Caesar less, but that I loved Rome more.

**Host B:** Not that I loved Caesar less, but that I loved Rome more. A perfect line. You could carve it on a monument.

**Host A:** And notice, again, Brutus' love. Why Brutus rose. Third person. Brutus is speaking about Brutus.

**Brutus:** Had you rather Caesar were living and die all slaves, than that Caesar were dead, to live all free men? As Caesar loved me, I weep for him; as he was fortunate, I rejoice at it; as he was valiant, I honour him: but, as he was ambitious, I slew him. There is tears for his love; joy for his fortune; honour for his valour; and death for his ambition.

**Host B:** Tears for his love, joy for his fortune, honour for his valour, death for his ambition. Four matching pairs. It is perfectly balanced. That figure is called isocolon. Clauses of equal length and structure.

**Host A:** And it sounds like justice, doesn't it? Like a scale. Everything weighed and allotted.

**Host B:** It sounds like a verdict. And then he turns to the crowd with questions they cannot answer no to.

**Brutus:** Who is here so base that would be a bondman? If any, speak; for him have I offended. Who is here so rude that would not be a Roman? If any, speak; for him have I offended. Who is here so vile that will not love his country? If any, speak; for him have I offended. I pause for a reply.

**Host A:** I pause for a reply. And of course nobody replies. Because who would stand up in a crowd and say, yes, I would like to be a slave, I do not love my country?

**Host B:** And the crowd shouts, none, Brutus, none. It is a rhetorical trap, beautifully built.

**Host A:** But here is what I think is the crucial thing about this speech. It contains no evidence. Brutus says Caesar was ambitious. He never says what Caesar did. There is no list of crimes, no example, no proof. It is all assertion, beautifully balanced.

**Host B:** That is exactly right. And remember the orchard. He admitted to himself that the quarrel would bear no colour for the thing Caesar is. There was no case. So there is no case in the speech either. Just form. Just honour.

**Host A:** And then he says something that will come back to haunt him.

**Brutus:** With this I depart, that, as I slew my best lover for the good of Rome, I have the same dagger for myself, when it shall please my country to need my death.

**Host B:** I have the same dagger for myself. He means it as a noble gesture. It is also a prophecy.

**Host A:** And the crowd's response is the most chilling moment of the scene.

**Citizen:** Bring him with triumph home unto his house.

**Citizen:** Give him a statue with his ancestors.

**Citizen:** Let him be Caesar.

**Citizen:** Caesar's better parts Shall be crown'd in Brutus.

**Host B:** Let him be Caesar. They want to make Brutus the very thing he killed Caesar to prevent.

**Host A:** Give him a statue. They have understood nothing. They have been moved by the music of the speech, and the only thing they can imagine doing with a great man is crowning him.

**Host B:** And that is, I think, the deepest criticism of Brutus's approach. He spoke to the crowd as if they were senators. As if they would follow an argument about the republic. And they heard a powerful man speaking well, and wanted to make him king.

**Host A:** And Wills has a very sharp phrase for it. He says, Brutus reads his cold and studied text. Cold and studied. It is prose, and it is clever, and it does not warm anyone.

**Host B:** There is a debate among critics about why Shakespeare gives Brutus prose. Prose in Shakespeare is often for comedy, or for low characters, or for madness. Here it is the noblest Roman. Some critics think it shows Brutus being plain and honest, refusing the tricks of verse. Others, like Wills, think it is the opposite. That it is artificial, over designed, a lecture rather than a speech.

**Host A:** I think it can be both. He is trying to be plain, and the plainness itself is a performance.

**Host B:** And then he makes his last mistake. He leaves.

**Brutus:** Good countrymen, let me depart alone, And, for my sake, stay here with Antony: Do grace to Caesar's corpse, and grace his speech Tending to Caesar's glories; which Mark Antony, By our permission, is allow'd to make. I do entreat you, not a man depart, Save I alone, till Antony have spoke.

**Host A:** Not a man depart, save I alone, till Antony have spoke. He orders the crowd to stay and listen to the man who is about to destroy him.

**Host B:** It is almost unbearable to watch. It is honourable. It is generous. It is suicidal.

**Host A:** And now Antony climbs into the pulpit. And the crowd is hostile. One citizen says, this Caesar was a tyrant. Another says, we are blessed that Rome is rid of him. Another says, it would be best if he speaks no harm of Brutus here.

**Host B:** So Antony starts from a position of total weakness. The crowd is on Brutus's side. He has been given permission to speak only on condition that he does not blame the conspirators. And he has to turn them, within a few minutes, into a mob that will burn the city.

**Host A:** And he does it in verse. And the first lines are the most famous opening in English.

**Antony:** Friends, Romans, countrymen, lend me your ears; I come to bury Caesar, not to praise him. The evil that men do lives after them; The good is oft interred with their bones; So let it be with Caesar.

**Host B:** Friends, Romans, countrymen. Compare Brutus. Romans, countrymen, and lovers. Brutus starts with Romans, the political identity. Antony starts with friends. The personal.

**Host A:** That is a lovely contrast. And then, I come to bury Caesar, not to praise him. He disarms them. He promises not to do what he is about to do for twenty minutes.

**Host B:** That figure, saying you will not do something while you do it, the Greeks called paralipsis. Pretending to pass over something in order to draw attention to it. And Antony is the master of it in this speech. I will not read the will. I am no orator. I do not mean to stir you up.

**Host A:** And then the line that will be repeated until it becomes poison.

**Antony:** The noble Brutus Hath told you Caesar was ambitious: If it were so, it was a grievous fault, And grievously hath Caesar answer'd it. Here, under leave of Brutus and the rest, For Brutus is an honourable man; So are they all, all honourable men, Come I to speak in Caesar's funeral.

**Host B:** If it were so. Three words, and the whole of Brutus's speech is in doubt. Brutus said Caesar was ambitious. Antony says, if it were so.

**Host A:** And then, Brutus is an honourable man. The first time, it sounds sincere.

**Antony:** He was my friend, faithful and just to me: But Brutus says he was ambitious; And Brutus is an honourable man. He hath brought many captives home to Rome Whose ransoms did the general coffers fill: Did this in Caesar seem ambitious? When that the poor have cried, Caesar hath wept: Ambition should be made of sterner stuff: Yet Brutus says he was ambitious; And Brutus is an honourable man.

**Host B:** And now look at what Antony is doing that Brutus never did. He is giving evidence. Caesar brought captives home and put the ransom money into the public treasury. Caesar wept when the poor cried. Concrete actions. Things the crowd can picture.

**Host A:** Brutus gave them balanced abstractions. Antony gives them facts. Selected, slanted facts. But facts.

**Antony:** You all did see that on the Lupercal I thrice presented him a kingly crown, Which he did thrice refuse: was this ambition? Yet Brutus says he was ambitious; And, sure, he is an honourable man.

**Host B:** And here is the masterstroke. The crown. You all saw it, he says. You all saw Caesar refuse the crown three times. Was this ambition?

**Host A:** But remember how Casca described it. Every time gentler than other. He would fain have had it. Antony gives them only the refusals and leaves out the gentleness. He takes the same event Casca told us about and tells it the other way.

**Host B:** And by now, notice what has happened to the phrase. And, sure, he is an honourable man. The word sure has crept in. It is getting heavier each time.

**Host A:** And then he stops. He weeps. He says he cannot go on.

**Antony:** You all did love him once, not without cause: What cause withholds you then, to mourn for him? O judgment! thou art fled to brutish beasts, And men have lost their reason. Bear with me; My heart is in the coffin there with Caesar, And I must pause till it come back to me.

**Host B:** O judgment, thou art fled to brutish beasts. Brutish. And it is hard not to hear Brutus in that word.

**Host A:** Is that deliberate?

**Host B:** Critics have noticed it, and it is very hard to hear the line without the echo. Whether Shakespeare meant it or not, the pun is sitting there. Judgment has fled to brutish beasts. Reason has gone over to Brutus's side, and become an animal.

**Host A:** And then the pause. My heart is in the coffin there with Caesar, and I must pause till it come back to me.

**Host B:** And in the pause, Shakespeare does something extraordinary. He lets us hear the crowd changing its mind in real time.

**Citizen:** Methinks there is much reason in his sayings.

**Citizen:** If thou consider rightly of the matter, Caesar has had great wrong.

**Citizen:** Has he, masters? I fear there will a worse come in his place.

**Citizen:** Mark'd ye his words? He would not take the crown; Therefore 'tis certain he was not ambitious.

**Citizen:** Poor soul! his eyes are red as fire with weeping.

**Citizen:** There's not a nobler man in Rome than Antony.

**Host A:** Therefore it is certain he was not ambitious. Because he refused the crown. A conclusion built on one detail Antony has just fed them.

**Host B:** And there is not a nobler man in Rome than Antony. Ten minutes ago, they wanted to crown Brutus. Now Antony is the noblest man in Rome. The word noble has migrated.

**Host A:** This is where Wills's reading is so useful. He shows that Antony first uses irony to make the crowd doubt the conspirators, and then establishes his own credibility, his ethos as the classical rhetoricians called it, as Caesar's loyal friend. And only then, once they trust him, does he move to emotion.

**Host B:** And now he brings out his second weapon. The will.

**Antony:** But here's a parchment with the seal of Caesar; I found it in his closet, 'tis his will: Let but the commons hear this testament, Which, pardon me, I do not mean to read, And they would go and kiss dead Caesar's wounds And dip their napkins in his sacred blood, Yea, beg a hair of him for memory.

**Host A:** Which, pardon me, I do not mean to read. Paralipsis again. He waves the document and refuses to read it.

**Host B:** And notice what he imagines them doing if they heard it. Kissing the wounds. Dipping napkins in his sacred blood. Begging for a hair to keep as a relic. That is exactly Decius's flattering interpretation of Calpurnia's dream, tinctures, stains, relics. The dream keeps coming true.

**Host A:** And David Kaula's reading, Caesar as a martyr whose relics people will treasure. Antony is literally creating the cult of Caesar, standing over the body.

**Host B:** And then he says something that is an outrageous lie and also a truth.

**Antony:** Have patience, gentle friends, I must not read it; It is not meet you know how Caesar loved you. You are not wood, you are not stones, but men; And, being men, bearing the will of Caesar, It will inflame you, it will make you mad.

**Host A:** You are not wood, you are not stones, but men. Remember the very first scene. The tribune Marullus called the commoners blocks, stones, worse than senseless things. Antony says the opposite. You are men. You have feelings. He flatters them as human beings.

**Host B:** That is a lovely echo. The tribune insulted the crowd, and was silenced. Antony honours the crowd, and is given Rome.

**Host A:** And the crowd is now begging for the will. And he says, I fear I wrong the honourable men whose daggers have stabbed Caesar. And the crowd shouts, they were traitors, honourable men! They were villains, murderers!

**Host B:** Honourable men. The crowd itself now spits out the phrase. Antony never said a single word against Brutus. The crowd said it for him.

**Host A:** That is exactly the genius of it. He never breaks the condition Brutus set. He never blames the conspirators. He lets the crowd do it.

**Host B:** And then the most theatrical move of all. He comes down from the pulpit. He asks their permission. Shall I descend? And they make a ring around the body. And he is no longer above them, lecturing. He is among them.

**Host A:** Wills calls this a staged counterpart to Brutus's visual rhetoric. Brutus had the conspirators bathe their arms in blood, a visual sacrament. Antony answers with his own image. The body, among the people.

**Antony:** If you have tears, prepare to shed them now. You all do know this mantle: I remember The first time ever Caesar put it on; 'Twas on a summer's evening, in his tent, That day he overcame the Nervii: Look, in this place ran Cassius' dagger through: See what a rent the envious Casca made: Through this the well-beloved Brutus stabb'd.

**Host B:** The first time Caesar put on this cloak, a summer evening in his tent, the day he defeated the Nervii, a fierce Gallic tribe. A private, tender, intimate memory. And then, look, in this place ran Cassius' dagger.

**Host A:** He was not there. He cannot possibly know which hole was made by which dagger. He invents it.

**Host B:** He invents it, and it does not matter. Because what the crowd sees is a cloak with holes in it and a name attached to each hole. And then the cruellest, most beautiful passage.

**Antony:** And as he pluck'd his cursed steel away, Mark how the blood of Caesar follow'd it, As rushing out of doors, to be resolved If Brutus so unkindly knock'd, or no; For Brutus, as you know, was Caesar's angel: Judge, O you gods, how dearly Caesar loved him! This was the most unkindest cut of all.

**Host A:** The blood rushes out of the wound to see if it was really Brutus who knocked. Like someone running to the door, unable to believe who is there.

**Host B:** And unkindly. In Shakespeare's English, unkind meant unnatural, against one's own kind, against kin. So Brutus did not just stab Caesar. He committed an unnatural act against the man who loved him like a son.

**Host A:** The most unkindest cut of all. A double superlative, which is not grammatical, and which is exactly right, because no ordinary grammar can carry the feeling.

**Antony:** For when the noble Caesar saw him stab, Ingratitude, more strong than traitors' arms, Quite vanquish'd him: then burst his mighty heart; And, in his mantle muffling up his face, Even at the base of Pompey's statua, Which all the while ran blood, great Caesar fell. O, what a fall was there, my countrymen! Then I, and you, and all of us fell down, Whilst bloody treason flourish'd over us.

**Host B:** In his mantle muffling up his face. That is straight from Plutarch. Caesar covered his face with his toga when he saw Brutus.

**Host A:** And then, I, and you, and all of us fell down. He makes the crowd part of the murder. Not spectators. Victims. You fell too.

**Host B:** And then the final reveal.

**Antony:** Kind souls, what, weep you when you but behold Our Caesar's vesture wounded? Look you here, Here is himself, marr'd, as you see, with traitors.

**Host A:** And he pulls the cloak away and shows them the body.

**Host B:** And the crowd breaks.

**Citizens:** Revenge! About! Seek! Burn! Fire! Kill! Slay! Let not a traitor live!

**Host A:** And this is where Gail Kern Paster's argument comes in. Paster says that Antony tries to, in her words, recuperate Caesar's body for his own political uses, by redefining what his blood means. Brutus said the blood was a sacrifice, a purging. Antony says the blood is a crime, a murder, an act of ingratitude. Same blood. Opposite meaning.

**Host B:** And Maurice Charney adds that Antony's blood and hunting imagery stresses, in his words, butchery rather than the sacrifice Brutus hoped for. Brutus wanted to be a sacrificer, not a butcher. Antony has just told all of Rome he was a butcher.

**Host A:** And then Antony does something almost comic in its audacity. The crowd is about to run off and burn things. And he stops them.

**Antony:** Good friends, sweet friends, let me not stir you up To such a sudden flood of mutiny. They that have done this deed are honourable: What private griefs they have, alas, I know not, That made them do it: they are wise and honourable, And will, no doubt, with reasons answer you.

**Antony:** I come not, friends, to steal away your hearts: I am no orator, as Brutus is; But, as you know me all, a plain blunt man, That love my friend.

**Host B:** I am no orator, as Brutus is. A plain blunt man. Spoken by the greatest orator in the play, at the climax of the greatest speech in the play.

**Host A:** It is the oldest trick in the rhetorical book. The speaker who claims he cannot speak. But it works perfectly, because the crowd has just heard Brutus's polished prose, and Antony now positions himself as the honest man against the smooth talker.

**Antony:** For I have neither wit, nor words, nor worth, Action, nor utterance, nor the power of speech, To stir men's blood: I only speak right on; I tell you that which you yourselves do know; Show you sweet Caesar's wounds, poor poor dumb mouths, And bid them speak for me.

**Host B:** Poor poor dumb mouths. And there is the image from his soliloquy over the body. The wounds as mouths begging him to speak for them. Now he has done it.

**Antony:** But were I Brutus, And Brutus Antony, there were an Antony Would ruffle up your spirits and put a tongue In every wound of Caesar that should move The stones of Rome to rise and mutiny.

**Host A:** Put a tongue in every wound of Caesar that should move the stones of Rome to rise and mutiny. He is describing exactly what he has just done, and pretending he cannot do it.

**Host B:** And then the crowd is running off to burn Brutus's house, and he calls them back again. You have forgotten the will. And he finally reads it.

**Antony:** Here is the will, and under Caesar's seal. To every Roman citizen he gives, To every several man, seventy-five drachmas.

**Antony:** Moreover, he hath left you all his walks, His private arbours and new-planted orchards, On this side Tiber; he hath left them you, And to your heirs for ever, common pleasures, To walk abroad, and recreate yourselves. Here was a Caesar! when comes such another?

**Host A:** Money, and public parks. And here was a Caesar, when comes such another.

**Host B:** And that is the end. They take the body to burn it in the holy places and use the brands to set the conspirators' houses on fire. Pluck down benches, pluck down forms, windows, anything.

**Host A:** Which is exactly what Plutarch describes. Burning the body on benches and tables torn from the forum.

**Host B:** And then Antony, alone.

**Antony:** Now let it work. Mischief, thou art afoot, Take thou what course thou wilt!

**Host A:** Mischief, thou art afoot. And a servant arrives and says that Octavius, Caesar's heir, has come to Rome, and that Brutus and Cassius have ridden like madmen through the gates of the city.

**Host B:** And Antony says, fortune is merry, and in this mood will give us anything. And goes off to meet Octavius.

**Host A:** So let us step back. What has this scene taught us?

**Host B:** For me, the most important lesson is the one Wills puts at the centre of his book. That this is a play about rhetoric, and rhetoric is not decoration. It decides who governs Rome. Brutus spoke the truth as he saw it, and lost. Antony manipulated a crowd, and won. And the play does not tell us how to feel about that.

**Host A:** I think there is something even more uncomfortable underneath. Brutus thought his reasons would be enough. That if he explained clearly, the people would understand. That is a very modern faith. The faith of the expert, the policy paper, the well reasoned argument.

**Host B:** And Antony knows that people do not change their minds because of arguments. They change their minds because of stories, and objects, and money, and the feeling of being included. A cloak with holes in it. A will with their names in it. You fell too.

**Host A:** But I want to be fair to Brutus. His speech is not dishonest. It is the speech of a man who believes the public will act rationally if treated as rational. Antony's speech is, in a deep way, contemptuous of the crowd. He knows exactly which buttons to press, and he presses them, and then he walks away saying, let it work.

**Host B:** That is a very good point. Antony flatters the crowd as men, not stones, and then treats them exactly like stones he can move.

**Host A:** And the actual cost of his speech is about to be shown to us, in the very next scene. In the shortest and most terrifying scene in the play.

**Host B:** The poet Cinna.

**Host A:** Something to listen for. Count how many times Antony says honourable in this speech. And notice who is saying the word by the end. It starts in Antony's mouth, sincere. It ends in the crowd's mouth, as an insult. That journey, a single word losing its meaning in front of us, is one of the great things in the play.
