# Julius Caesar 5. Mob and Death List

**Host A:** A street in Rome, the same afternoon as the funeral. Smoke in the air. The crowd that Antony has just set loose is running through the city with torches. And a man comes out of his house, uneasy, not quite sure why he is going out at all.

**Cinna:** I dreamt to-night that I did feast with Caesar, And things unlucky charge my fantasy: I have no will to wander forth of doors, Yet something leads me forth.

**Host B:** His name is Cinna. He is a poet. And this tiny scene, barely forty lines long, is one of the most frightening things Shakespeare ever wrote.

**Host A:** Before we hear it, the historical background, because it is real. There was a poet called Gaius Helvius Cinna. He was a friend of Catullus, and wrote an ambitious, learned poem called Zmyrna, which Catullus praised, and which is now lost. And at Caesar's funeral, in March forty four before Christ, he was lynched by the crowd, who mistook him for Lucius Cornelius Cinna, a politician who had openly sided with the assassins. Shakespeare makes that Cinna one of the conspirators.

**Host B:** Plutarch tells the story. And Plutarch also records that the poet had dreamed, the night before, that Caesar invited him to supper. Which is exactly the dream Shakespeare gives him. I dreamt tonight that I did feast with Caesar.

**Host A:** Another dream. Calpurnia dreamed of Caesar's statue spouting blood. Brutus lives in a phantasma, a hideous dream. And now a poet dreams of supper with a dead man and walks out of his door to his death.

**Host B:** And the play is full of men who do not want to go out and go anyway. Caesar did not want to go to the Senate. Cinna has no will to wander forth of doors, yet something leads him forth. Fate, or the pull of a crowd.

**Host A:** And then the citizens surround him.

**Citizen:** What is your name?

**Citizen:** Whither are you going?

**Citizen:** Where do you dwell?

**Citizen:** Are you a married man or a bachelor?

**Host B:** Four questions, from four men, all at once. And then they add instructions.

**Citizen:** Answer every man directly.

**Citizen:** Ay, and briefly.

**Citizen:** Ay, and wisely.

**Citizen:** Ay, and truly, you were best.

**Host A:** Directly, briefly, wisely, truly. This is an interrogation. And there is something almost bureaucratic about it. A checklist.

**Host B:** And I find this moment uncanny in a particular way. Listen to the pattern. Directly, briefly, wisely, truly. Four adverbs, balanced. It is a parody of exactly the kind of rhetoric Brutus used in the Forum. Brutus's balanced pairs, tears for his love, joy for his fortune, honour for his valour, death for his ambition. And here is the crowd, an hour later, using the same structure to interrogate a stranger.

**Host A:** The crowd has learned rhetoric from both speeches. Brutus's form and Antony's fury.

**Host B:** And Cinna, being a poet, does something fatal. He makes a joke.

**Cinna:** What is my name? Whither am I going? Where do I dwell? Am I a married man or a bachelor? Then, to answer every man directly and briefly, wisely and truly: wisely I say, I am a bachelor.

**Host A:** Wisely I say, I am a bachelor. Meaning, it is wise not to marry.

**Host B:** And one of the citizens takes offence. That's as much as to say they are fools that marry, you'll bear me a bang for that. He is going to hit him for the joke.

**Host A:** So the poet's wit, the thing that makes him a poet, is already a danger. In a mob, a joke is an insult.

**Host B:** And then the questions continue. Where is he going? To Caesar's funeral. As a friend or an enemy? As a friend. Where does he live? By the Capitol. And then the fatal one.

**Citizen:** Your name, sir, truly.

**Cinna:** Truly, my name is Cinna.

**Citizen:** Tear him to pieces; he's a conspirator.

**Cinna:** I am Cinna the poet, I am Cinna the poet.

**Citizen:** Tear him for his bad verses, tear him for his bad verses.

**Cinna:** I am not Cinna the conspirator.

**Citizen:** It is no matter, his name's Cinna; pluck but his name out of his heart, and turn him going.

**Host A:** Tear him for his bad verses.

**Host B:** That line gets a laugh in almost every production. And then the laugh dies in the audience's throat. Because they realise what they are laughing at.

**Host A:** It is the most terrifying line in the play because it reveals that the crowd does not care whether he is guilty. They have a name, and they want a body. When it turns out he is the wrong man, they find another reason. Bad verses. Anything will do.

**Host B:** Pluck but his name out of his heart. Rip the name out of his chest. The man does not matter. Only the name.

**Host A:** And that connects directly to everything we have been saying about names in this play. R. A. Foakes's phrase, an obsession with names. Cassius weighing the names of Brutus and Caesar like coins. Brutus loving the name of honour. Caesar speaking of himself as Caesar. And here, the end point of all that. A man killed for a name.

**Host B:** And this is where René Girard's reading of the play becomes most powerful. Girard's whole theory is about the scapegoat. When a community is in crisis, full of rivalry and violence, it relieves the pressure by focusing all of it on one victim. And the victim does not need to be guilty. He only needs to be available, and to carry some mark that makes him stand out. Here the mark is a name.

**Host A:** So the crowd that Antony created needs a victim. The conspirators have fled. And a poet called Cinna walks out of his door.

**Host B:** And then they move on. Come, brands, fire brands, to Brutus's house, to Cassius's, burn all. Some to Decius's house, some to Casca's, some to Ligarius's. And they are gone. The scene ends without anyone looking back at the body.

**Host A:** Productions have often cut this scene. It is short, it seems like a digression, and it is very hard to stage. And that is exactly why Orson Welles's production in 1937 was such a landmark.

**Host B:** Welles, at twenty one, staged the play at his Mercury Theatre in New York in modern uniforms that evoked Mussolini's Italy and Hitler's Germany. He called the production Caesar. And he restored the Cinna scene. In his staging, the poet was surrounded by shadowy figures, like secret police, and dragged away into the darkness.

**Host A:** And it stopped the show. The audience understood. This was what happened in Europe in the nineteen thirties. A man stopped in the street, asked his name, and taken away because the name was wrong.

**Host B:** And eighty years later, when the Public Theater in New York staged the play in Central Park in 2017, the director Oskar Eustis echoed Welles's staging of this scene. In his version, the poet was surrounded by soldiers in riot gear, insisting, I'm Cinna the poet.

**Host A:** So the scene keeps being rediscovered whenever an age is frightened of mobs, or of states that behave like mobs.

**Host B:** And I think there is one more layer. Cinna is a poet. The only poet in the play. And he is killed by a crowd that has just been moved by a speech. The power of words that Antony used to make Rome weep is now turned against a man whose only crime is to make words himself.

**Host A:** There is something almost like Shakespeare's self portrait in it. A writer, in the street, at the mercy of an audience.

**Host B:** And it is not the only time Shakespeare shows a crowd turning on a man of letters. In Henry the Sixth, Part Two, written several years earlier, the rebel Jack Cade and his followers seize a clerk from the town of Chatham. His crime is that he can read and write. They find out he can sign his own name, and Cade orders him hanged with his pen and inkhorn around his neck. And it is in that same play that one of Cade's men says the famous line, the first thing we do, let's kill all the lawyers.

**Host A:** So Shakespeare kept coming back to this. The crowd that hates learning. The man killed for being literate, or for being a poet.

**Host B:** And yet he is not simply a snob about crowds. Remember the cobbler at the start of this play, who ran rings round the tribunes. The commoners in Shakespeare can be witty, decent, and right. What frightens him is the moment when a crowd stops being a group of people and becomes a single thing, with one appetite.

**Host A:** And it is the speakers who make that happen. The crowd in the Forum was a group of individuals, arguing with each other, some for Brutus, some for Antony. By the end of Antony's speech, they are one voice. Revenge, burn, fire, kill. And that single voice is what kills Cinna.

**Host B:** And then, with no transition at all, the next scene. A house in Rome. Three men at a table. And the very first line.

**Antony:** These many, then, shall die; their names are prick'd.

**Host A:** Their names are pricked. Marked with a pin or a pen on a list.

**Host B:** So straight after a mob kills a man for his name, the new rulers of Rome sit down and kill men by their names, on paper. The mob does it with fire. The state does it with ink.

**Host A:** And the three men are Mark Antony, Octavius Caesar, Julius Caesar's adopted heir, and Lepidus, a general. Historically, these three formed what became known as the Second Triumvirate, in November forty three before Christ, more than a year and a half after Caesar's death. Shakespeare collapses all that time into the space between two scenes.

**Host B:** And historically, what they did is called the proscriptions. They drew up lists of enemies of the state. Anyone on the list could be killed by anyone, and their property was confiscated. Hundreds of senators and wealthy citizens died. It was partly revenge and partly a way of raising money.

**Host A:** And listen to how casually they trade lives.

**Octavius:** Your brother too must die; consent you, Lepidus?

**Lepidus:** I do consent.

**Octavius:** Prick him down, Antony.

**Lepidus:** Upon condition Publius shall not live, Who is your sister's son, Mark Antony.

**Antony:** He shall not live; look, with a spot I damn him.

**Host B:** With a spot I damn him. A spot. A dot of ink.

**Host A:** Lepidus gives up his brother. Antony gives up his nephew. Exchanged, like trading pieces in a game.

**Host B:** And this is Shakespeare adapting history. In Plutarch, the relative Antony gives up is his uncle on his mother's side, Lucius Caesar. And Lepidus is permitted to put his own brother, Paulus, to death. Shakespeare keeps the brother and turns the uncle into a nephew, a sister's son, which is somehow worse. A young man. Family.

**Host A:** And this is the same Antony who, an hour ago, wept over Caesar's body and said, my heart is in the coffin there with Caesar. Here is his heart, at a desk.

**Host B:** And then the line that is almost unbelievable.

**Antony:** But, Lepidus, go you to Caesar's house; Fetch the will hither, and we shall determine How to cut off some charge in legacies.

**Host A:** The will. The will he read to the crowd. The seventy five drachmas for every citizen. The parks and orchards on this side of the Tiber.

**Host B:** And now he wants to find ways to cut back the bequests. The promise that turned the crowd is already being trimmed.

**Host A:** Garry Wills, in Rome and Rhetoric, says there are no villains in this play. But he also describes the triumvirs as coldly efficient. And this is the scene where you see it. No speeches. No grief. A list, a will, and an errand boy.

**Host B:** And the errand boy is Lepidus. As soon as he is out of the room, Antony turns on him.

**Antony:** This is a slight unmeritable man, Meet to be sent on errands: is it fit, The three-fold world divided, he should stand One of the three to share it?

**Antony:** And though we lay these honours on this man, To ease ourselves of divers slanderous loads, He shall but bear them as the ass bears gold, To groan and sweat under the business, Either led or driven, as we point the way; And having brought our treasure where we will, Then take we down his load, and turn him off, Like to the empty ass, to shake his ears, And graze in commons.

**Host A:** He shall bear them as the ass bears gold. Lepidus will carry the blame for the proscriptions, and then be turned out to pasture.

**Host B:** He is going to use Lepidus as a scapegoat too. To ease ourselves of divers slanderous loads. To take the blame for the killings off us.

**Host A:** And Octavius, who historically was barely twenty at this point, says, very coolly, he's a tried and valiant soldier.

**Antony:** So is my horse, Octavius; and for that I do appoint him store of provender: It is a creature that I teach to fight, To wind, to stop, to run directly on, His corporal motion govern'd by my spirit.

**Host B:** So is my horse. That is the line. Lepidus is a brave soldier, and so is my horse.

**Host A:** And at the end of this speech Antony says, do not talk of him but as a property.

**Host B:** A property. Which in Shakespeare's theatre also meant a stage prop. An object that actors use. So Lepidus is a prop in Antony's play.

**Host A:** And, to finish the thought, Antony's view of Lepidus is exactly how he treated the crowd. Something to be led or driven. A creature to be taught to run directly on. His corporal motion governed by my spirit.

**Host B:** So the man who told the crowd, you are not wood, you are not stones, but men, now describes his own ally as a donkey and a horse.

**Host A:** And there is a long shadow over this scene, if you know what comes later. Shakespeare would write Antony and Cleopatra several years after this play. And in that play, Lepidus appears again, and is mocked again, drunk at a feast, and then quietly deposed by Octavius. Exactly as Antony predicts here. Turned off to graze.

**Host B:** And Antony, in that later play, is himself destroyed by Octavius.

**Host A:** Which is why Octavius's last line in this scene is so sinister. He says, we are at the stake, and bayed about with many enemies, and some that smile have in their hearts, I fear, millions of mischiefs.

**Host B:** Some that smile. He could be talking about Brutus and Cassius. He could also be talking about the man sitting across the table from him.

**Host A:** That is a wonderful reading. And it connects to something Wills noticed about the structure of the play. He reads it in terms of Plutarch's Parallel Lives, where every great man is set against another. And Wills sees two parallel pairs. Cassius starts the conspiracy, but is overshadowed by Brutus. And Antony starts the counter revolution, but is overshadowed by Octavius.

**Host B:** So Antony is the Cassius of the second half. The clever, passionate man who does the work, and is eventually eclipsed by a colder, more single minded partner.

**Host A:** And the German critic Hermann Ulrici, in the nineteenth century, read the triumvirate as representing what he called an oligarchical principle, which won because it had the right of the immediate present on its side.

**Host B:** The right of the immediate present. That is a very cold phrase. History belongs to whoever is ready to act now, without scruple.

**Host A:** And M. W. MacCallum, in 1910, saw Octavius as embodying what he called the spirit of Empire. The force that prevails at the end. Not Brutus's republic, not Antony's passion, but Octavius's cold, impersonal power.

**Host B:** And remember, Octavius is Julius Caesar's adopted son and heir. He takes the name Caesar. Eventually he becomes Augustus, the first emperor. So in this scene, we are watching Caesar come back. Not the man. The name.

**Host A:** The spirit of Caesar, which Brutus wanted to kill without dismembering the man. They killed the man, and here is the spirit, at a table, pricking names.

**Host B:** And I want to add one more piece of history, because the play itself tells us about it a little later. When Brutus and Cassius hear the news from Rome, they learn that the triumvirs have put to death, by Messala's count, a hundred senators, or by Brutus's count, seventy. And one of them is Cicero.

**Host A:** Cicero. The man the conspirators refused to include. Brutus said, name him not, he will never follow anything that other men begin. And he was left out of the plot, and he was killed anyway.

**Host B:** Historically, Cicero had attacked Antony in a series of brilliant speeches, the Philippics. And when he was killed in December forty three, Plutarch says Antony had his head and his hands displayed in the Forum, on the speakers' platform. The hands that had written the speeches.

**Host A:** So the great orator, whose one line in this play warned that men construe things after their fashion, dies on a list drawn up by the greatest orator in the play.

**Host B:** And there is a terrible symmetry. Antony won Rome with a speech. Then he killed the man who had made the best speeches against him.

**Host A:** So what does this short stretch of the play tell us? The mob scene and the list scene, side by side?

**Host B:** I think it tells us that the assassination did exactly what Brutus feared Caesar would do. It unleashed tyranny. Not one king, but three, and a mob. And it is worse than anything Caesar did in the play. Caesar refused a crown three times and spoke grandly about the northern star. These men kill their own families with a dot of ink.

**Host A:** And the play does it without comment. No one in these scenes says, how terrible. We are left to see it.

**Host B:** And Stephen Greenblatt's recent reading in Tyrant, his book on Shakespeare's politics, makes exactly this point. Greenblatt argues that the conspiracy spectacularly backfires. The very act that was meant to save the republic destroys it. And these are the scenes where you watch it being destroyed.

**Host A:** But I want to push back slightly. Because the play is not simply saying, therefore Brutus was wrong and Caesar should have lived. It is also saying something about Antony. The grieving friend of the Forum is a cynical politician the moment the doors close. The man who made the crowd weep for Caesar's generosity is cutting the legacies. So the play is not on Antony's side either.

**Host B:** That is right. Nobody comes out of these two scenes well. The mob is murderous. The triumvirs are cold. The only innocent person is a poet, and he is dead.

**Host A:** Which is perhaps why these scenes have mattered so much to modern directors. Welles in 1937. The Public Theater in 2017. Because they show the real cost of political violence. Not to the great men. To the people with the wrong names.

**Host B:** Something to listen for, if you go back to the play. Count the scenes in which someone's name decides what happens to them. Brutus because of his ancestor. Caesar because of his. Cinna. The names pricked on Antony's list. And Octavius, who will take the name Caesar and keep it. By the end of the play, the most powerful thing in Rome is a name.

**Host A:** Next, a tent in a military camp, near the city of Sardis. Brutus and Cassius, who killed their friend for a principle, quarrel over money. And a letter brings news of Portia.

**Host B:** Thank you for listening to Books on Pods.
