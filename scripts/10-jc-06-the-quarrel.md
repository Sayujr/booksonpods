# Julius Caesar 6. The Quarrel

**Host A:** Months have passed, though the play hardly tells us how many. Brutus and Cassius have fled Rome and raised armies in the east. They are camped near Sardis, in Asia Minor, preparing for war with Antony and Octavius. And they have not seen each other for a while. And Brutus is waiting outside his tent for Cassius to arrive. And he asks one of his officers, Lucilius, how Cassius received him.

**Host B:** And Lucilius says, politely enough, with courtesy and respect, but not with the old familiarity. Not with such free and friendly conference as he used to.

**Brutus:** Thou hast described A hot friend cooling: ever note, Lucilius, When love begins to sicken and decay, It useth an enforced ceremony. There are no tricks in plain and simple faith.

**Host A:** When love begins to sicken and decay, it useth an enforced ceremony. That is one of the truest lines about friendship Shakespeare ever wrote.

**Host B:** When a friendship is dying, it gets more formal. More polite. The warmth goes and the manners stay.

**Host A:** And then Cassius arrives with his army, and his first words are an accusation.

**Cassius:** Most noble brother, you have done me wrong.

**Brutus:** Judge me, you gods! wrong I mine enemies? And, if not so, how should I wrong a brother?

**Host B:** And Brutus immediately does something very characteristic. He says, not here. Not in front of the armies. The soldiers should see nothing but love between us. Come into my tent, and say what you have to say there.

**Host A:** Appearances again. Always the appearance. In the orchard, he wanted the murder to appear necessary and not envious. Now he wants the quarrel to appear like love.

**Host B:** And then they go into the tent. And what follows is the scene that, for at least two centuries, was the most admired scene in the play. Samuel Johnson, who found the play as a whole somewhat cold and unaffecting, said that the contention and reconciliation of Brutus and Cassius is universally celebrated.

**Host A:** And it was celebrated from the very beginning. Leonard Digges, a younger contemporary of Shakespeare, wrote a poem published in 1640 in which he described how audiences were thrilled by this scene, and contrasted it with the learned Roman tragedies of Ben Jonson, which by comparison left them cold. For Digges, this quarrel proved that Shakespeare's genius came from nature, not labour.

**Host B:** And Lewis Theobald, the eighteenth century editor, compared it with famous quarrels between great men in ancient Greek tragedy and in later English plays, and called Shakespeare's version incomparably the best.

**Host A:** And John Dryden, in one of his prologues, wrote of Shakespeare's godlike Romans raging, and confessed he was overawed by it.

**Host B:** So this is a scene that every generation of critics loved. And when you hear it, you understand why. It is not about politics at all. It is two old friends, exhausted and frightened, tearing at each other.

**Host A:** It starts with a bribe. Cassius is angry because Brutus has publicly condemned one of Cassius's officers, Lucius Pella, for taking bribes from the local people. And Cassius wrote letters defending him, and Brutus ignored them.

**Cassius:** In such a time as this it is not meet That every nice offence should bear his comment.

**Host B:** In wartime, says Cassius, you cannot be fussy about every small offence.

**Brutus:** Let me tell you, Cassius, you yourself Are much condemn'd to have an itching palm; To sell and mart your offices for gold To undeservers.

**Cassius:** I an itching palm! You know that you are Brutus that speak this, Or, by the gods, this speech were else your last.

**Host A:** You know that you are Brutus that speak this. Again, the name. If you were anyone but Brutus, I would kill you for that.

**Host B:** And then Brutus brings out the deepest weapon he has. The murder itself.

**Brutus:** Remember March, the ides of March remember: Did not great Julius bleed for justice' sake? What villain touch'd his body, that did stab, And not for justice? What, shall one of us That struck the foremost man of all this world But for supporting robbers, shall we now Contaminate our fingers with base bribes.

**Host A:** Did not great Julius bleed for justice' sake? That is remarkable. Great Julius. He gives the man he murdered his grandest title.

**Host B:** And he says something new about why Caesar died. But for supporting robbers. That is a charge we never heard before. In the orchard he admitted there was no case against Caesar as he was. Now, months later, Caesar supported robbers. The story has changed.

**Host A:** The past being rewritten, to justify the present.

**Host B:** And then the line that every actor playing Brutus loves.

**Brutus:** I had rather be a dog, and bay the moon, Than such a Roman.

**Host A:** Rather a dog howling at the moon than a corrupt Roman.

**Host B:** And Cassius flares back, I am a soldier, older in practice, abler than yourself to make conditions. And it becomes almost childish.

**Brutus:** Go to; you are not, Cassius.

**Cassius:** I am.

**Brutus:** I say you are not.

**Host A:** I am. You are not. I am. That is two of the most powerful men in the world arguing like schoolboys.

**Host B:** Which is the genius of the scene. We see them as they really are, without the speeches. Tired, frightened, proud men.

**Host A:** And Brutus is crueller than Cassius. Much crueller. He tells Cassius he will use him for his mirth, for his laughter, when he is waspish. And Cassius says, is it come to this?

**Host B:** And then Cassius says something revealing, about Caesar.

**Cassius:** When Caesar lived, he durst not thus have moved me.

**Brutus:** Peace, peace! you durst not so have tempted him.

**Host A:** When Caesar was alive, he would not have dared to treat me like this. And Brutus says, you would not have dared to provoke Caesar like this.

**Host B:** So the dead man is in the tent with them. Every argument comes back to him.

**Host A:** And then Brutus's own great speech of self righteousness.

**Brutus:** There is no terror, Cassius, in your threats, For I am arm'd so strong in honesty That they pass by me as the idle wind, Which I respect not. I did send to you For certain sums of gold, which you denied me: For I can raise no money by vile means: By heaven, I had rather coin my heart, And drop my blood for drachmas, than to wring From the hard hands of peasants their vile trash By any indirection: I did send To you for gold to pay my legions, Which you denied me.

**Host B:** Listen to the logic. I cannot raise money by vile means. I would rather coin my own heart than squeeze the peasants. And therefore, Cassius, why did you not send me the money that you squeezed from the peasants?

**Host A:** He is too pure to take bribes himself. But he wants the proceeds of Cassius's bribes to pay his own soldiers.

**Host B:** It is one of the most devastating moments of self exposure in Shakespeare. And Brutus does not see it at all.

**Host A:** And that is exactly what so many critics have said about him. Marvin Vawter's phrase, unable to see himself. Hazlitt's overweening confidence. Brutus believes he is armed so strong in honesty, and he is not even aware of the contradiction in his own sentence.

**Host B:** And then Cassius breaks. And the most moving part of the scene begins.

**Cassius:** I did not: he was but a fool that brought My answer back. Brutus hath rived my heart: A friend should bear his friend's infirmities, But Brutus makes mine greater than they are.

**Host A:** A friend should bear his friend's infirmities. That is the line people quote.

**Host B:** And Brutus is still cold. I do not, till you practise them on me. And Cassius says, you love me not. And Brutus says, I do not like your faults.

**Cassius:** A friendly eye could never see such faults.

**Brutus:** A flatterer's would not, though they do appear As huge as high Olympus.

**Host A:** A friendly eye could never see such faults. A flatterer's would not.

**Host B:** And that exchange is a perfect little argument about friendship. Is a true friend someone who overlooks your faults, or someone who tells you about them? And the scholar Garry Wills, in Rome and Rhetoric, points out that this whole scene draws on Cicero's famous dialogue on friendship, De Amicitia. Which discusses precisely this. Whether a friend should flatter, or speak frankly.

**Host A:** Cicero again. The man who was left out of the conspiracy, and who has just been killed on Antony's list, is present in this scene as a book.

**Host B:** And then Cassius does something extraordinary.

**Cassius:** Come, Antony, and young Octavius, come, Revenge yourselves alone on Cassius, For Cassius is aweary of the world; Hated by one he loves; braved by his brother; Cheque'd like a bondman; all his faults observed, Set in a note-book, learn'd, and conn'd by rote, To cast into my teeth. O, I could weep My spirit from mine eyes!

**Cassius:** There is my dagger, And here my naked breast; within, a heart Dearer than Plutus' mine, richer than gold: If that thou be'st a Roman, take it forth; I, that denied thee gold, will give my heart: Strike, as thou didst at Caesar; for, I know, When thou didst hate him worst, thou lovedst him better Than ever thou lovedst Cassius.

**Host A:** Strike, as thou didst at Caesar. For I know, when thou didst hate him worst, thou lovedst him better than ever thou lovedst Cassius.

**Host B:** That is heartbreaking. Cassius is jealous of a dead man. He knows that Brutus loved Caesar more than him, even while killing him.

**Host A:** And Cassius, the lean and hungry plotter, the man Caesar said thinks too much, turns out to be the most emotional man in the play. The one who needs to be loved.

**Host B:** And I think this is one of Shakespeare's great surprises in the play. In the first act, Cassius seemed cold, envious, manipulative. Here he is weeping, offering his breast to Brutus's dagger. And Brutus, the philosopher, the man of honour, is the cold one.

**Host A:** And Geoffrey Miles has a beautiful idea about this. Miles traced the Stoic traditions in the play, and argued that the play's emotion works more powerfully precisely because it is repressed. These men are trying so hard not to feel, that when feeling breaks through, it is overwhelming.

**Host B:** And it breaks through in Brutus too, finally.

**Brutus:** Sheathe your dagger: Be angry when you will, it shall have scope; Do what you will, dishonour shall be humour. O Cassius, you are yoked with a lamb That carries anger as the flint bears fire; Who, much enforced, shows a hasty spark, And straight is cold again.

**Host A:** I carry anger the way a flint carries fire. You strike it, a spark comes out, and then it is cold again.

**Host B:** And then Brutus admits it. When I spoke that, I was ill tempered too. And they take hands.

**Brutus:** And my heart too.

**Host A:** Give me your hand. And my heart too.

**Host B:** And then Cassius says something so human. Have you not love enough to bear with me, when that rash humour which my mother gave me makes me forgetful? And Brutus says, yes, and from now on, when you are too sharp with me, I will think it is your mother scolding, and let it pass.

**Host A:** Two murderers, joking about their mothers.

**Host B:** And then the strangest interruption in the play. Someone forces his way into the tent. A poet.

**Poet:** For shame, you generals! what do you mean? Love, and be friends, as two such men should be; For I have seen more years, I'm sure, than ye.

**Cassius:** Ha, ha! how vilely doth this cynic rhyme!

**Brutus:** Get you hence, sirrah; saucy fellow, hence!

**Host A:** How vilely doth this cynic rhyme.

**Host B:** This comes straight from Plutarch. In Plutarch, the man who bursts in is called Marcus Favonius, who modelled himself on the philosopher Cato and behaved like a Cynic, one of the ancient philosophers who despised manners and spoke bluntly. And Plutarch says he burst in quoting Homer, telling them to make up, because he was older than both of them. And Cassius laughed at him.

**Host A:** And Shakespeare turns him into a poet.

**Host B:** And I think that is a significant choice. Because this is the second poet in the play. The first was Cinna, torn apart by the mob for his name and his bad verses. And now a second poet comes in, with a perfectly good message, love and be friends, and he is mocked for rhyming and thrown out.

**Brutus:** What should the wars do with these jigging fools? Companion, hence!

**Host A:** What should the wars do with these jigging fools.

**Host B:** Jigging means rhyming, with a bouncy rhythm. And a jig was also the comic dance that ended a play at the Globe. Thomas Platter, who saw Julius Caesar in 1599, says that at the end of the play the actors danced gracefully together, as was their custom.

**Host A:** So Brutus is saying, what does war want with entertainers. In a play, in a theatre, that will end with a dance.

**Host B:** Poets do not fare well in this play. One is killed, one is thrown out. Words are for power here, not for truth.

**Host A:** And then, as soon as they are alone again, Brutus says something that changes everything about what we have just seen.

**Cassius:** I did not think you could have been so angry.

**Brutus:** O Cassius, I am sick of many griefs.

**Cassius:** Of your philosophy you make no use, If you give place to accidental evils.

**Brutus:** No man bears sorrow better. Portia is dead.

**Host B:** No man bears sorrow better. Portia is dead.

**Host A:** He has been carrying this the entire time. The whole quarrel. The cruelty, the coldness, the itching palm, the dog baying at the moon. All of it with his wife newly dead.

**Host B:** And Cassius is devastated.

**Cassius:** How 'scaped I killing when I cross'd you so? O insupportable and touching loss! Upon what sickness?

**Brutus:** Impatient of my absence, And grief that young Octavius with Mark Antony Have made themselves so strong: for with her death That tidings came; with this she fell distract, And, her attendants absent, swallow'd fire.

**Host A:** Swallowed fire. Plutarch says she swallowed hot coals.

**Host B:** And Cassius says, how did I escape being killed, when I crossed you like that? He understands now. The anger was grief.

**Host A:** And this is where Coppélia Kahn's reading of Portia comes full circle. Portia wounded her thigh to prove she was as constant as a Roman man. She wanted to share her husband's secret. And she died of the strain of the secret, alone, while he was away. The Roman ideal of constancy killed her.

**Host B:** And then Brutus says, speak no more of her. Give me a bowl of wine. In this I bury all unkindness, Cassius. And Cassius says, I cannot drink too much of Brutus' love.

**Host A:** And then Messala arrives, with news. And here is the most puzzling moment in the whole play.

**Host B:** Messala reports that Octavius, Antony and Lepidus have put to death a hundred senators through their proscriptions. Brutus says his letters say seventy, and that Cicero is one of them.

**Host A:** And then Messala asks, very gently, whether Brutus has had letters from his wife.

**Messala:** Had you your letters from your wife, my lord?

**Brutus:** No, Messala.

**Messala:** Nor nothing in your letters writ of her?

**Brutus:** Nothing, Messala.

**Host B:** Nothing, Messala. But he has just told Cassius that she is dead.

**Messala:** Then like a Roman bear the truth I tell: For certain she is dead, and by strange manner.

**Brutus:** Why, farewell, Portia. We must die, Messala: With meditating that she must die once, I have the patience to endure it now.

**Messala:** Even so great men great losses should endure.

**Host A:** Even so great men great losses should endure.

**Host B:** So what is happening? There are two main explanations, and both are fascinating. The first is a theory about the text. That Shakespeare wrote two versions of this scene. In one, Brutus tells Cassius about Portia. In the other, Messala tells Brutus. And the printers of the First Folio in 1623 accidentally included both. So we are seeing a draft that should have been crossed out.

**Host A:** And the second explanation?

**Host B:** That it is deliberate. Brutus has told his friend in private, as a friend. And now, in front of a subordinate, he performs Stoic composure. He pretends not to know, so that he can receive the news like a perfect Roman, in public. And Messala gives him exactly the applause he wants. Even so great men great losses should endure.

**Host A:** And Cassius's reply is crucial. I have as much of this in art as you, but yet my nature could not bear it so.

**Host B:** In art. Meaning in theory. I know the philosophy as well as you do, Cassius says, but my nature could not bear it like that.

**Host A:** Which do you believe?

**Host B:** I think the play works either way, but I find the second reading devastating. Because if it is deliberate, then Brutus has turned his wife's death into a performance of his own virtue. Twice. Once privately to Cassius, with no man bears sorrow better. And once publicly to Messala, with we must die, Messala. And that is exactly what the play has been showing us about him all along. A man who cannot stop being Brutus. Who cannot grieve without being admired for how he grieves.

**Host A:** I am more inclined to the revision theory, honestly. Because the scene is so careful elsewhere. But I agree that the effect on stage, if you play both, is chilling. And it fits Miles's idea of repressed emotion. The feeling is so strong it has to be performed twice, and neither performance quite contains it.

**Host B:** And then, immediately, they turn to strategy. Well, to our work alive, says Brutus. And he proposes marching to Philippi to meet the enemy.

**Host A:** And Cassius says no. And Cassius is right. It is better, he says, that the enemy should seek us. Let them march, waste their supplies, tire their soldiers, while we rest. Sound military sense.

**Host B:** And Brutus overrules him. For the fifth time in the play. The oath, Cicero, sparing Antony, letting Antony speak, and now Philippi. And he does it with one of the most beautiful speeches he has.

**Brutus:** There is a tide in the affairs of men, Which, taken at the flood, leads on to fortune; Omitted, all the voyage of their life Is bound in shallows and in miseries. On such a full sea are we now afloat; And we must take the current when it serves, Or lose our ventures.

**Host A:** There is a tide in the affairs of men. Taken at the flood, it leads to fortune. Missed, and your whole life is stuck in the shallows.

**Host B:** It is one of the most quoted passages in the play. People put it on posters about seizing opportunity. And in the play, it is the speech that leads Brutus's army to destruction.

**Host A:** The most beautiful argument for seizing the moment, used to seize the wrong one.

**Host B:** And Cassius gives in. Then, with your will, go on. And look at what Brutus says just before the tide speech. Good reasons must, of force, give place to better. He calls his own reasons better without making the case.

**Host A:** And then they part for the night, and Cassius says, never come such division between our souls. Let it not, Brutus. And Brutus says, every thing is well.

**Host B:** Every thing is well. With his wife dead, Cicero dead, a hundred senators dead, and a battle tomorrow he should not be fighting.

**Host A:** And then, the quietest and loveliest moment in the play. Brutus alone with his servants. He tells two soldiers, Varro and Claudius, to sleep in his tent. He finds a book he thought he had lost. It was in his own pocket. And he says to Lucius, his servant boy, bear with me, good boy, I am much forgetful.

**Host B:** The great Roman, apologising to a child for being forgetful.

**Host A:** And he asks the boy to play some music. And the boy is so tired.

**Lucius:** I have slept, my lord, already.

**Brutus:** It was well done; and thou shalt sleep again; I will not hold thee long: if I do live, I will be good to thee.

**Host B:** If I do live, I will be good to thee.

**Host A:** And the boy plays, and falls asleep over his instrument.

**Brutus:** This is a sleepy tune. O murderous slumber, Lay'st thou thy leaden mace upon my boy, That plays thee music? Gentle knave, good night; I will not do thee so much wrong to wake thee: If thou dost nod, thou break'st thy instrument; I'll take it from thee; and, good boy, good night.

**Host B:** He takes the instrument out of the sleeping boy's hands so it will not break. That is the gentlest thing anyone does in the whole play.

**Host A:** And even here, a dark word. O murderous slumber. He cannot even describe sleep without the word murder.

**Host B:** And he opens his book. Is not the leaf turned down where I left reading? And the candle flickers.

**Brutus:** How ill this taper burns! Ha! who comes here? I think it is the weakness of mine eyes That shapes this monstrous apparition. It comes upon me. Art thou any thing? Art thou some god, some angel, or some devil, That makest my blood cold and my hair to stare? Speak to me what thou art.

**Ghost:** Thy evil spirit, Brutus.

**Brutus:** Why comest thou?

**Ghost:** To tell thee thou shalt see me at Philippi.

**Host A:** Thy evil spirit, Brutus.

**Host B:** And here is a crucial detail from the source. In Plutarch, Brutus sees a strange and terrible figure in his tent at night, which says, I am thy evil spirit, Brutus, thou shalt see me by the city of Philippi. And Plutarch never says it is Caesar. It is Brutus's own evil genius, his own dark spirit.

**Host A:** And Shakespeare's stage direction says, enter the Ghost of Caesar.

**Host B:** Exactly. Shakespeare turns Brutus's evil spirit into Caesar's ghost. But he keeps the words. Thy evil spirit, Brutus. So the ghost is both. Caesar, and Brutus's own spirit. The man he killed has become part of him.

**Host A:** Which is J. E. Phillips's idea of Caesar's spirit as the nemesis against which Brutus's efforts, however highly motivated, are of no avail. And it is also Brutus's own wish, come back to haunt him. In the orchard, he said, we all stand up against the spirit of Caesar. Now the spirit stands up in his tent.

**Host B:** And then the ghost is gone. And Brutus wakes everyone up. Lucius, still half asleep, says, the strings, my lord, are false. He thinks he is still playing. And Brutus asks each of them, did you cry out? Did you see anything? And each one says, nothing, my lord.

**Host A:** Nothing, my lord. Nobody else saw it.

**Host B:** So we are left not knowing whether it was a ghost, or the weakness of Brutus's eyes, as he said himself, or his own guilt. The critic Fortin's point about the play being about the limits of knowledge applies here perfectly. Even the supernatural is uncertain.

**Host A:** So what does this long night tell us about Brutus and Cassius?

**Host B:** For me, it is the scene where the two men become human. In the first acts, Cassius was the envious schemer and Brutus the noble idealist. Here, Cassius is wounded, loving, jealous of a dead man, and right about strategy. Brutus is cruel, self righteous, secretly grieving, tender with a child, and wrong about strategy.

**Host A:** And I think the scene shows us something about why the conspiracy failed. Not just Antony's speech. The two leaders cannot trust each other. They argue about money, about honour, about who loved Caesar more. They have killed for a principle, and the principle cannot hold them together.

**Host B:** When love begins to sicken and decay, it useth an enforced ceremony. Brutus said it about Cassius at the start of the scene. By the end, it is about all of them, and about Rome.

**Host A:** Something to listen for. Count the times in this act Brutus says he is well, or that all is well, when it is not. Every thing is well. With meditating that she must die once, I have the patience to endure it now. And ask yourself whether you believe him.

**Host B:** Next, Philippi. Two armies, two parleys, a birthday, a misread signal, and the noblest Roman of them all.

**Host A:** Thank you for listening to Books on Pods.
