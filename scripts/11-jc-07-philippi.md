# Julius Caesar 7. Philippi

**Host A:** The plains of Philippi, in northern Greece. Two armies facing each other. On one side, Brutus and Cassius. On the other, Antony and the young Octavius, Caesar's heir. And before the battle, by an old custom, the generals meet in the open ground between the armies to talk.

**Host B:** And Octavius is pleased. Antony said the enemy would stay in the hills. And they have come down to meet us. Which is exactly what Cassius warned against in the tent, and Brutus overruled him.

**Host A:** The tide in the affairs of men. Taken at the flood. And here they are.

**Host B:** And before we get to the parley, a note on history and compression, because Shakespeare has done something dramatic. Historically, there were two battles at Philippi, both in October of forty two before Christ, about three weeks apart. Cassius died after the first. Brutus after the second. Shakespeare merges them into a single day.

**Host A:** And one more historical footnote I love. The young Roman poet Horace fought at Philippi, on Brutus's side, as an officer. And years later, in one of his odes, he joked that he ran away from the battle and left his shield behind. One of the greatest poets in Latin survived Philippi by running.

**Host B:** Which is a nice counterweight to all the Roman talk of dying nobly. Horace chose living.

**Host A:** Now the parley. And it is an exchange of insults, and it is all about words.

**Brutus:** Words before blows: is it so, countrymen?

**Octavius:** Not that we love words better, as you do.

**Brutus:** Good words are better than bad strokes, Octavius.

**Antony:** In your bad strokes, Brutus, you give good words: Witness the hole you made in Caesar's heart, Crying 'Long live! hail, Caesar!'

**Host B:** In your bad strokes, you give good words. You stabbed him while shouting long live Caesar.

**Host A:** And then Cassius gets a shot at Antony, and it is a great insult.

**Cassius:** Antony, The posture of your blows are yet unknown; But for your words, they rob the Hybla bees, And leave them honeyless.

**Host B:** Hybla was a place in Sicily famous for honey. Your words are so sweet you have stolen all the honey from the bees.

**Antony:** Not stingless too.

**Brutus:** O, yes, and soundless too; For you have stol'n their buzzing, Antony, And very wisely threat before you sting.

**Host A:** You have stolen their buzzing too, and you threaten before you sting. Brutus is saying Antony is all noise.

**Host B:** And then Antony gives a version of the murder that we have not heard before. And it is brutal.

**Antony:** Villains, you did not so, when your vile daggers Hack'd one another in the sides of Caesar: You show'd your teeth like apes, and fawn'd like hounds, And bow'd like bondmen, kissing Caesar's feet; Whilst damned Casca, like a cur, behind Struck Caesar on the neck. O you flatterers!

**Host A:** Hacked one another in the sides of Caesar. Apes, hounds, bondmen, a cur. Every image is an animal.

**Host B:** And critics who have traced the play's imagery find the animals all the way through. Caesar as a lion, a hart, a deer. The conspirators as apes and dogs and serpents. And here, at the end, Antony turns all of them into beasts.

**Host A:** And Cassius turns on Brutus, right in front of the enemy.

**Cassius:** Flatterers! Now, Brutus, thank yourself: This tongue had not offended so to-day, If Cassius might have ruled.

**Host B:** If Cassius might have ruled. If I had had my way, and we had killed Antony along with Caesar, this tongue would not be insulting us today.

**Host A:** The disagreement from the orchard, still alive at Philippi.

**Host B:** And then Octavius speaks, and the young man's voice is chilling in its simplicity.

**Octavius:** Look; I draw a sword against conspirators; When think you that the sword goes up again? Never, till Caesar's three and thirty wounds Be well avenged; or till another Caesar Have added slaughter to the sword of traitors.

**Host A:** Caesar's three and thirty wounds. In the historical sources, Plutarch and Suetonius, Caesar received twenty three wounds. Shakespeare's text says thirty three. The eighteenth century editor Lewis Theobald changed it to three and twenty, to match the histories. Most modern editors leave it, and assume Shakespeare simply chose the bigger number.

**Host B:** And or till another Caesar. Octavius calls himself Caesar. He has taken the name. He is the second Caesar, the spirit returned in the flesh.

**Host A:** And Brutus says, Caesar, thou canst not die by traitors' hands, unless thou bring'st them with thee. Meaning, the only traitors here are on your side.

**Host B:** And Octavius's reply. So I hope. I was not born to die on Brutus' sword. Short, cold, final. He does not argue. He does not make speeches.

**Host A:** And that is the thing about Octavius in this play. He barely speaks. He never makes a great speech. And he wins.

**Host B:** M. W. MacCallum, writing in 1910, saw Octavius as embodying what he called the spirit of Empire. The force that prevails. And Garry Wills, in Rome and Rhetoric, sees a parallel structure. Just as Cassius started the conspiracy and was overshadowed by Brutus, Antony led the counter revolution and will be overshadowed by Octavius.

**Host A:** So the men who talk lose to the man who does not.

**Host B:** And then the armies separate, and Cassius has a quiet word with his officer Messala. And it is one of the most moving speeches in the play.

**Cassius:** Messala, This is my birth-day; as this very day Was Cassius born. Give me thy hand, Messala: Be thou my witness that against my will, As Pompey was, am I compell'd to set Upon one battle all our liberties.

**Host A:** This is my birthday. Which comes straight from Plutarch, who says that Cassius died on his birthday.

**Host B:** And as Pompey was. Pompey the Great, whom Caesar defeated, was also pushed into fighting a battle against his own judgement, and lost. So Cassius sees himself repeating Pompey's mistake. The play opened with the shadow of Pompey, in the tribunes' speech. And here it is again, at the end.

**Cassius:** You know that I held Epicurus strong And his opinion: now I change my mind, And partly credit things that do presage.

**Host A:** I held Epicurus strong. Epicurus was the Greek philosopher who taught that the gods do not interfere in human affairs. So Epicureans did not believe in omens. And Plutarch says the historical Cassius was an Epicurean, who tried to explain away Brutus's vision of the evil spirit as an illusion of a tired mind.

**Host B:** And now he is changing his mind. And he tells Messala about two eagles that perched on their standards on the march from Sardis, eating from the soldiers' hands, and then this morning they were gone, and in their place ravens, crows and kites circling, looking down on the army as if it were sick prey.

**Host A:** The man who, in the storm before the ides, bared his chest to the lightning and laughed at omens, is now reading the birds.

**Host B:** And remember what he said to Brutus in the first act. The fault, dear Brutus, is not in our stars, but in ourselves. Men at some time are masters of their fates. And here, on his birthday, he partly credits things that do presage.

**Host A:** The play gives Cassius the stars he did not believe in.

**Host B:** And then he and Brutus say goodbye. And they discuss what to do if they lose. And this is a difficult passage, because Brutus contradicts himself. He says that according to his philosophy, he finds it cowardly and vile to end one's own life for fear of what might happen. And a few lines later, when Cassius asks whether he would let himself be led through Rome in triumph as a captive, Brutus says no, never. Brutus will not go bound to Rome.

**Host A:** So his Stoic principle and his Roman pride cannot both be satisfied.

**Host B:** Exactly. And it is a crack in the philosophy that runs right through the play. The idea that a free man can always choose death rather than slavery, which Cassius proclaimed back in the storm, is the Roman idea of honour. And the play shows it, honestly, as something that kills a great many people in its final scenes. Portia, Cassius, Titinius, Brutus. It does not ask us to admire that. It asks us to see where the ideal of constancy leads.

**Host A:** And then the farewell, which is beautiful.

**Brutus:** Therefore our everlasting farewell take: For ever, and for ever, farewell, Cassius! If we do meet again, why, we shall smile; If not, why then, this parting was well made.

**Cassius:** For ever, and for ever, farewell, Brutus! If we do meet again, we'll smile indeed; If not, 'tis true this parting was well made.

**Host B:** Cassius repeats Brutus's words back to him, almost exactly. After all the quarrelling, they end in perfect agreement.

**Brutus:** O, that a man might know The end of this day's business ere it come! But it sufficeth that the day will end, And then the end is known.

**Host A:** O that a man might know the end of this day's business before it comes. That is the whole play in one line. Every character has been trying to read the future. Omens, dreams, letters, soothsayers. And nobody can.

**Host B:** And the critic Fortin's reading of the play as being about the limits of knowledge, in the spirit of Montaigne, could have this line as its epigraph.

**Host A:** And now the battle. And Shakespeare stages it in fragments. Short scenes, alarms, messages. And the crucial events are all about seeing and misreading.

**Host B:** Brutus attacks Octavius's wing and wins. But he attacks too early, and his soldiers start looting, and Cassius's side is left exposed, and Antony's forces surround Cassius. So Cassius has lost his part of the battle, while Brutus has won his.

**Host A:** And Cassius retreats to a hill, and sees tents burning in the distance, and some troops approaching. And he does not know whether they are friends or enemies. So he sends his friend Titinius to find out.

**Cassius:** Titinius, if thou lovest me, Mount thou my horse, and hide thy spurs in him, Till he have brought thee up to yonder troops, And here again; that I may rest assured Whether yond troops are friend or enemy.

**Host B:** And then he asks his servant Pindarus to climb higher and watch, because, he says, my sight was ever thick. My eyesight was always poor.

**Host A:** Remember Caesar's deaf ear. Now Cassius's poor eyes. These great men keep failing in their senses.

**Host B:** And then he says the line about his birthday again.

**Cassius:** This day I breathed first: time is come round, And where I did begin, there shall I end; My life is run his compass.

**Host A:** And Pindarus, watching, reports what he thinks he sees. Titinius surrounded by horsemen. They are taking him. He is captured. And the men are shouting for joy.

**Host B:** And in fact, as we learn a few minutes later, Titinius has reached Brutus's victorious troops, who are cheering and putting a wreath of victory on his head. The shouts are for joy, but not for the reason Pindarus thinks.

**Host A:** And Cassius, believing that his best friend has been captured because of him, decides to die. He asks Pindarus to hold the sword. The same sword, he says, that ran through Caesar.

**Cassius:** Caesar, thou art revenged, Even with the sword that kill'd thee.

**Host B:** Caesar, thou art revenged. And Maurice Charney, who traced the imagery of blood through the play, saw in Cassius's death what he called the reciprocity of blood for blood. The sword that shed Caesar's blood sheds Cassius's.

**Host A:** And then Pindarus runs away into the wilderness, never to be seen again. A freed slave, freed by his master's death.

**Host B:** And Titinius comes back, with the wreath, and with Messala, carrying good news. And finds the body.

**Titinius:** Why didst thou send me forth, brave Cassius? Did I not meet thy friends? and did not they Put on my brows this wreath of victory, And bid me give it thee? Didst thou not hear their shouts? Alas, thou hast misconstrued every thing!

**Host A:** Alas, thou hast misconstrued every thing.

**Host B:** And we are back where we began. Cicero, in the storm. Men may construe things after their fashion, clean from the purpose of the things themselves. Cassius has misconstrued everything. And so, in one way or another, has everyone.

**Host A:** And Messala, before he goes to fetch Brutus, says the lines we quoted in the long episode, and they belong here.

**Messala:** Mistrust of good success hath done this deed. O hateful error, melancholy's child, Why dost thou show to the apt thoughts of men The things that are not?

**Host B:** The things that are not. The tragedy of the play is a series of men acting on things that are not. A tyrant who had not yet happened. A people's voice that was a forged letter. A ritual of purification that was a slaughter. A captured friend who was a cheered one.

**Host A:** And Titinius places the wreath on Cassius's head, and kills himself with Cassius's sword. And then Brutus arrives, with young Cato, Portia's brother, and others. And he sees the bodies.

**Brutus:** O Julius Caesar, thou art mighty yet! Thy spirit walks abroad and turns our swords In our own proper entrails.

**Host B:** There it is. The spirit of Caesar. Brutus said, in the orchard, we all stand up against the spirit of Caesar. And now he admits the spirit has won. It turns their own swords against them.

**Host A:** And this is J. E. Phillips's nemesis. And Edward Dowden's justification of the title. The play bears the name of Julius Caesar with strict propriety, because Caesar dead is more powerful than Caesar living.

**Host B:** And then Brutus gives Cassius his epitaph.

**Brutus:** Are yet two Romans living such as these? The last of all the Romans, fare thee well! It is impossible that ever Rome Should breed thy fellow. Friends, I owe more tears To this dead man than you shall see me pay. I shall find time, Cassius, I shall find time.

**Host A:** The last of all the Romans. For Cassius. The envious schemer of Act One.

**Host B:** And I owe more tears to this dead man than you shall see me pay. Even now, he will not weep in public. I shall find time.

**Host A:** He never does find time. That is the tragedy in four words.

**Host B:** And then the second battle. And there is a strange little scene that brings the play's obsession with names to its climax. Young Cato, Portia's brother, son of the great Stoic, runs into the fighting shouting his name.

**Cato:** I will proclaim my name about the field: I am the son of Marcus Cato, ho! A foe to tyrants, and my country's friend.

**Brutus:** And I am Brutus, Marcus Brutus, I; Brutus, my country's friend; know me for Brutus!

**Host A:** Know me for Brutus. He rushes into battle shouting his own name.

**Host B:** And then, an extraordinary moment. Brutus's officer Lucilius is captured, and pretends to be Brutus, to protect him. He says, I am Brutus, kill Brutus and be honoured in his death. And the soldiers bring him to Antony, thinking they have captured Brutus.

**Host A:** So the name Brutus now belongs to whoever says it. Lucilius steals the name to save the man.

**Host B:** And Antony recognises him, and is moved, and says, this is not Brutus, friend, but, I assure you, a prize no less in worth. And he orders them to treat Lucilius kindly. Antony, of all people, honouring loyalty.

**Host A:** And then the last scene. Brutus, with a few exhausted friends, sitting on a rock. The battle is lost. And he asks them, one after another, to hold his sword.

**Host B:** Clitus refuses. Dardanius refuses. And then Volumnius, and Brutus says something tender.

**Brutus:** Good Volumnius, Thou know'st that we two went to school together: Even for that our love of old, I prithee, Hold thou my sword-hilts, whilst I run on it.

**Volumnius:** That's not an office for a friend, my lord.

**Host A:** That's not an office for a friend. Not the job of a friend.

**Host B:** And he tells Volumnius that Caesar's ghost has appeared to him twice. Once at Sardis, and once last night, here at Philippi. I know my hour is come.

**Host A:** Twice. We only saw it once. The ghost has been visiting him off stage.

**Host B:** And then his farewell.

**Brutus:** Countrymen, My heart doth joy that yet in all my life I found no man but he was true to me. I shall have glory by this losing day More than Octavius and Mark Antony By this vile conquest shall attain unto.

**Host A:** I found no man but he was true to me.

**Host B:** That is the most painful line in the scene. Because it is true about his friends. And it is the exact opposite of what Brutus was to Caesar. Caesar found a man who was not true to him. Brutus himself.

**Host A:** And I shall have glory by this losing day. Even now, he is thinking about how he will be remembered. The name of honour.

**Brutus:** So fare you well at once; for Brutus' tongue Hath almost ended his life's history: Night hangs upon mine eyes; my bones would rest, That have but labour'd to attain this hour.

**Host B:** Brutus' tongue hath almost ended his life's history. Third person, to the very end. Just like Caesar. Et tu, Brute, then fall, Caesar. Brutus' tongue hath almost ended.

**Host A:** And then Strato, his servant, agrees to hold the sword, if Brutus will give him his hand first. And Brutus runs onto it.

**Brutus:** Caesar, now be still: I kill'd not thee with half so good a will.

**Host B:** Caesar, now be still. As if Caesar has been restless all this time, and now can finally rest.

**Host A:** I killed not thee with half so good a will. I kill myself more willingly than I killed you. Which is a strange last confession. It suggests that even Brutus, at the end, knew his heart was never fully in the murder.

**Host B:** And it rhymes with the orchard. There, he said he had not slept since Cassius first whetted him against Caesar. He killed against the grain of his own nature. He dies with it.

**Host A:** And then the victors arrive. Octavius, Antony, Messala, and Lucilius, now a prisoner. And Strato says something that Brutus would have loved.

**Strato:** The conquerors can but make a fire of him; For Brutus only overcame himself, And no man else hath honour by his death.

**Host B:** Brutus only overcame himself. Only Brutus could defeat Brutus.

**Host A:** And then Antony speaks the eulogy.

**Antony:** This was the noblest Roman of them all: All the conspirators save only he Did that they did in envy of great Caesar; He only, in a general honest thought And common good to all, made one of them. His life was gentle, and the elements So mix'd in him that Nature might stand up And say to all the world 'This was a man!'

**Host B:** And we must ask the question we asked in the long episode. Is Antony right?

**Host A:** The judgement itself comes from Plutarch. Plutarch reports that Antony said Brutus was the only one of the conspirators who acted out of a sense of the honour and justice of the deed, while the rest acted out of envy and malice toward Caesar. So Shakespeare did not invent Antony's verdict. He took it from history and gave it to the most persuasive speaker in the play.

**Host B:** And envy is the key word. René Girard called his book on Shakespeare A Theater of Envy. And Antony's eulogy makes envy the motive of the whole conspiracy, except for one man. Brutus alone was not envious. He alone acted for the general good.

**Host A:** So the noblest Roman is the one without envy. And yet he did the worst thing of all. Which is the deepest irony of the play. Envy made the plot, but it took a man without envy to make it possible.

**Host B:** And the elements so mixed in him. In Renaissance thought, a person was made of four elements, earth, water, air and fire, and a perfect person had them in perfect balance. So Antony says Brutus was perfectly balanced. Nature could say of him, this was a man.

**Host A:** This was a man. Not this was a god. A man.

**Host B:** And then the last word goes not to Antony, but to Octavius.

**Octavius:** According to his virtue let us use him, With all respect and rites of burial. Within my tent his bones to-night shall lie, Most like a soldier, order'd honourably. So call the field to rest; and let's away, To part the glories of this happy day.

**Host A:** To part the glories of this happy day. To share out the glory.

**Host B:** And that is the end. Not a speech about Rome. Not a reflection on tragedy. An administrator, giving orders. Bury him in my tent. Call off the troops. Let us go and divide up the rewards.

**Host A:** The future emperor Augustus has the last word. And it is cold, efficient, and correct. MacCallum's spirit of Empire. Ulrici's right of the immediate present.

**Host B:** And it is this happy day. For the victors. The play ends on the word happy, over the bodies of Brutus and Cassius and Titinius, and Portia, and Cicero, and a hundred senators, and a poet called Cinna.

**Host A:** So, at the end of the play, where are we?

**Host B:** I think the ending does two things at once. It honours Brutus, through Antony's eulogy. And it shows that the honour does not matter. The world belongs to Octavius. The noble man is buried in the victor's tent, as a courtesy.

**Host A:** And I think it also completes the pattern of names. The play began with tribunes tearing scarves off Caesar's statues. It goes through Cassius weighing the names of Brutus and Caesar, Cinna killed for his name, the names pricked on a list, young Cato and Brutus shouting their names in battle, Lucilius pretending to be Brutus. And it ends with Octavius, who has taken the name Caesar, giving orders. The name has won.

**Host B:** And the man who wanted to kill the spirit of Caesar has, in a sense, become one of its servants. His bones lie in Caesar's tent.

**Host A:** That is a lovely, terrible image to end on.

**Host B:** Something to listen for, if you go back to the last act. Count the times characters see or hear things wrongly. Cassius's thick sight. Pindarus misreading the shouts. Brutus attacking too soon. And set it beside Cicero's line in the storm. Men may construe things after their fashion. It is the tragedy's real last word.
