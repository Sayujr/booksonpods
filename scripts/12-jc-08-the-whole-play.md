# Julius Caesar 8. The Whole Play

**Host A:** The fifteenth of March, forty four years before Christ. A room near the theatre of Pompey in Rome, full of senators. A man of about fifty five, the ruler of most of the known world, who in Shakespeare's telling is deaf in one ear and prone to fits, is stabbed twenty three times by men he called friends. And then, standing over the body, with their hands red to the elbow, one of the killers says this.

**Cassius:** How many ages hence Shall this our lofty scene be acted over In states unborn and accents yet unknown!

**Host B:** And the man next to him, Brutus, answers.

**Brutus:** How many times shall Caesar bleed in sport, That now on Pompey's basis lies along No worthier than the dust!

**Host A:** Now step forward sixteen and a half centuries, to London in 1599. A brand new theatre on the south bank of the Thames, called the Globe. A Swiss traveller named Thomas Platter writes in his diary that on the twenty first of September he crossed the river and saw the tragedy of the first emperor Julius Caesar, very pleasingly performed, with approximately fifteen characters. And at the end, he says, the actors danced together admirably and exceedingly gracefully, as was their custom. And somewhere in that performance, an English actor in a Roman costume says, how many ages hence shall this our lofty scene be acted over, in states unborn and accents yet unknown.

**Host B:** And the audience at the Globe are the state unborn. They are the accent yet unknown. The line is true while it is being spoken.

**Host A:** And it is worth saying where Shakespeare got the story. He took it almost entirely from Plutarch's Lives of the noble Greeks and Romans, in the English translation by Thomas North, published in 1579. Some of his best lines are North's prose, lightly turned into verse. But he changed things with great skill. He moved Caesar's triumph so that it falls on the festival of the Lupercal. He squeezed months of politics into a few days. And he merged the two battles of Philippi, which were twenty days apart, into a single day.

**Host B:** So the shape is Shakespeare's, even when the words are borrowed. Everything is compressed toward speed. Decision, murder, speeches, riot, war, death.

**Host A:** And it has kept coming true. In Swahili, in fascist uniforms, with all female casts, in Central Park in front of protesters. This play has been performed over and over again by people who were sure it was about them.

**Host B:** Which is why I want to start with what we each actually think it is about. Because I suspect we disagree.

**Host A:** All right. I will go first, and I will give you the classic view, because it is honestly what I felt the first time I read it. Julius Caesar is the tragedy of Brutus. A good man, the best man in Rome, who does a terrible thing for noble reasons and is destroyed by it. A tragedy of idealism. Caesar is the occasion. Brutus is the heart.

**Host B:** That is a very old and very respectable view.

**Host A:** It goes back at least two hundred years. The German Romantic critic August Wilhelm Schlegel declared that Brutus, not Caesar, is the hero of the play. And A. C. Bradley, the great Edwardian critic of Shakespeare's tragedies, called Brutus the hero too, putting the word in quotation marks, as if to say, of course. Even earlier, in the early seventeen hundreds, the critic Charles Gildon called Brutus, quote, the shining and darling character of the poet.

**Host B:** And there is the ending. The final verdict on Brutus is given by his enemy, Mark Antony, over his body.

**Antony:** This was the noblest Roman of them all: All the conspirators save only he Did that they did in envy of great Caesar; He only, in a general honest thought And common good to all, made one of them. His life was gentle, and the elements So mix'd in him that Nature might stand up And say to all the world 'This was a man!'

**Host A:** There it is. The play itself tells us. He was the noblest Roman of them all.

**Host B:** Except the man saying it is Mark Antony. The finest manipulator of public feeling in the whole of Shakespeare. Shall we trust him on this one occasion?

**Host A:** You are going to make me work for this.

**Host B:** I am. Because I think your view is right, and I think if we push it, it turns into something stranger. Let's start with the scene that makes the case for Brutus best. He is alone in his orchard at night, before the conspiracy is settled, trying to decide whether Caesar has to die. Listen to how he reasons.

**Brutus:** It must be by his death: and for my part, I know no personal cause to spurn at him, But for the general. He would be crown'd: How that might change his nature, there's the question.

**Host A:** He has nothing personal against Caesar. It is all for the general good, for Rome. That is exactly the noble Brutus.

**Host B:** Keep listening.

**Brutus:** And, to speak truth of Caesar, I have not known when his affections sway'd More than his reason.

**Host B:** He admits Caesar has never yet let his passions overrule his reason. Caesar has done nothing tyrannical. And then comes the extraordinary part.

**Brutus:** And, since the quarrel Will bear no colour for the thing he is, Fashion it thus; that what he is, augmented, Would run to these and these extremities: And therefore think him as a serpent's egg Which, hatch'd, would, as his kind, grow mischievous, And kill him in the shell.

**Host A:** Since the quarrel will bear no colour for the thing he is.

**Host B:** Since there is no case against Caesar as he actually is. Fashion it thus. Shape the argument. Imagine what he might become if things were augmented. And then treat him as a snake's egg and kill him before he hatches.

**Host A:** That is uncomfortable. He is building a case he knows is not there.

**Host B:** He is not deciding whether to kill Caesar. He is deciding how to describe killing Caesar so that he can live with it. And that is not my reading alone. Critics in the twentieth century, the critic Anson among them, saw self deception at the core of Brutus. And Norman Rabkin pointed out something beautiful in the structure. The scene in Brutus's orchard is followed immediately by a scene in Caesar's house. Two great men, on the same night, both being persuaded of something by people who want something from them. Rabkin called it a balance of perception and self righteous blindness.

**Host A:** So in the next scene, Caesar's wife Calpurnia begs him to stay at home because she has dreamed of his statue running with blood.

**Host B:** And Caesar decides to stay. And then a conspirator named Decius arrives to fetch him to the Senate, and does something brilliant. He reinterprets the dream.

**Decius:** This dream is all amiss interpreted; It was a vision fair and fortunate: Your statue spouting blood in many pipes, In which so many smiling Romans bathed, Signifies that from you great Rome shall suck Reviving blood, and that great men shall press For tinctures, stains, relics and cognizance.

**Host A:** And Caesar goes. Persuaded by flattery, just as Brutus was persuaded by an idea of himself.

**Host B:** Exactly. Brutus is flattered by Cassius into seeing himself as the heir of the Brutus who drove out the kings of Rome. Caesar is flattered by Decius into seeing a nightmare as a coronation. Two self deceptions side by side.

**Host A:** All right. I will give ground. Brutus is not simply noble. He is noble, and he is also a man who talks himself into things. But he is still the centre. He is still the one who suffers most.

**Host B:** Before we leave Caesar's house, listen to how Caesar himself talks about danger. Calpurnia begs him not to go out, because the heavens are full of omens.

**Calpurnia:** When beggars die, there are no comets seen; The heavens themselves blaze forth the death of princes.

**Caesar:** Cowards die many times before their deaths; The valiant never taste of death but once. Of all the wonders that I yet have heard, It seems to me most strange that men should fear; Seeing that death, a necessary end, Will come when it will come.

**Host A:** That is a magnificent speech. Genuinely brave.

**Host B:** It is. And then, a few lines later, he decides to stay at home after all. And a few lines after that, he lets Decius flatter him into going. The brave speech is real, and so is the vanity, and so is the fear. Caesar keeps talking about himself in the third person, as if he were already a statue. Caesar shall go forth. Caesar is turned to hear. The scholar M. W. MacCallum noticed this in 1910. He said Caesar's constant harping on his own flawless valour should make us suspicious.

**Host A:** And there is that wonderful moment in the first act, when Caesar tells Antony why he distrusts Cassius.

**Caesar:** Let me have men about me that are fat; Sleek-headed men and such as sleep o' nights: Yond Cassius has a lean and hungry look; He thinks too much: such men are dangerous.

**Host A:** And then he says he is not afraid of Cassius, because he is Caesar. And in the very next breath.

**Caesar:** I rather tell thee what is to be fear'd Than what I fear; for always I am Caesar. Come on my right hand, for this ear is deaf, And tell me truly what thou think'st of him.

**Host B:** For always I am Caesar. Come on my right hand, for this ear is deaf. The god and the old man in two lines. Shakespeare gives the most powerful man in the world a bad ear, at the exact moment he claims to be above fear.

**Host A:** And epilepsy too. Casca tells us Caesar fell down in the market place and foamed at the mouth when the crowd cheered him.

**Host B:** Which Cassius instantly turns into a joke about the rest of them. No, Caesar hath it not, but you and I, and honest Casca, we have the falling sickness. We are the ones falling down before him.

**Host B:** Then let me push from the other direction. The play is called Julius Caesar. And Caesar dies in the first scene of Act Three. Halfway through. So why is it named after him?

**Host A:** That is the oldest puzzle about the play.

**Host B:** And the Victorian scholar Edward Dowden had an answer. He said the play bears the name of Julius Caesar with strict propriety. Because Caesar is more powerful dead than alive. And the play shows it. After the murder, Brutus and Cassius lose everything. Rome turns against them. And at the end, when Cassius dies, he dies on the sword he used to kill Caesar, and says this.

**Cassius:** Caesar, thou art revenged, Even with the sword that kill'd thee.

**Host B:** And when Brutus finds his body, he says it outright.

**Brutus:** O Julius Caesar, thou art mighty yet! Thy spirit walks abroad and turns our swords In our own proper entrails.

**Host A:** And Brutus's own last words.

**Brutus:** Caesar, now be still: I kill'd not thee with half so good a will.

**Host B:** Caesar, now be still. As if the dead man has been hunting him the whole time. Which in the play he has. His ghost comes to Brutus's tent the night before the final battle.

**Host A:** So the play is named after a spirit, not a man.

**Host B:** And here is the strange thing. That is exactly what Brutus wanted to kill. Listen to what he says when the conspirators are planning the murder.

**Brutus:** Let us be sacrificers, but not butchers, Caius. We all stand up against the spirit of Caesar; And in the spirit of men there is no blood: O, that we then could come by Caesar's spirit, And not dismember Caesar! But, alas, Caesar must bleed for it!

**Host A:** Oh, that is devastating in hindsight.

**Host B:** If only we could kill the spirit and leave the man. But we have to kill the man to get at the spirit. And what happens? They kill the man, and the spirit is set free. Caesar dies, and Caesarism wins. The very next ruler of Rome is Caesar's adopted heir Octavius, who will become the emperor Augustus. They killed a man to stop one man rule, and created an empire.

**Host A:** So that is where my view goes when you push it. I said Brutus is the hero. You say the title is right. And the synthesis is that the real subject is neither man. It is the gap between a person and the idea of the person. Brutus thinks he is fighting an idea, Caesarism. He kills a body. And the idea grows stronger.

**Host B:** And the play is full of this. Listen to how Cassius works on Brutus in the very first act. He does it through names.

**Cassius:** Brutus and Caesar: what should be in that 'Caesar'? Why should that name be sounded more than yours? Write them together, yours is as fair a name; Sound them, it doth become the mouth as well; Weigh them, it is as heavy; conjure with 'em, Brutus will start a spirit as soon as Caesar.

**Host A:** Write them together, sound them, weigh them. He treats the names like coins.

**Host B:** And just before that, the most famous line in the scene.

**Cassius:** Why, man, he doth bestride the narrow world Like a Colossus, and we petty men Walk under his huge legs and peep about To find ourselves dishonourable graves. Men at some time are masters of their fates: The fault, dear Brutus, is not in our stars, But in ourselves, that we are underlings.

**Host A:** The fault, dear Brutus, is not in our stars. A line that has gone into the language so completely that John Green named a bestselling novel after it, The Fault in Our Stars.

**Host B:** And look at the irony. Cassius says, we are masters of our fates, it is not the stars. And this play is crammed with omens, storms, a soothsayer, a ghost, a dream that comes true. Cassius himself, at the end, says, this day I breathed first, time is come round. He dies on his birthday. The play gives him the stars he did not believe in.

**Host A:** And names destroy people literally. The most frightening scene in the play is tiny. After Antony's speech, the crowd runs riot, and they find a poet called Cinna, who happens to share his name with one of the conspirators.

**Cinna:** I am Cinna the poet, I am Cinna the poet.

**Citizen:** Tear him for his bad verses, tear him for his bad verses.

**Cinna:** I am not Cinna the conspirator.

**Citizen:** It is no matter, his name's Cinna; pluck but his name out of his heart, and turn him going.

**Host B:** Pluck but his name out of his heart. They kill the name. The man is incidental.

**Host A:** It is a joke and a horror at the same time. Tear him for his bad verses. And Orson Welles understood exactly how much it mattered. In 1937, when he was twenty one years old, Welles staged the play at his Mercury Theatre in New York in modern uniforms that evoked Mussolini's Italy and Hitler's Germany. Theatre productions often cut the Cinna scene. Welles restored it, and had the poet seized by shadowy figures like secret police. It stopped the show.

**Host B:** Which brings me to the next step. If the play is about names and ideas outliving bodies, then the real question is, who makes the ideas? And the answer the play gives is clear. Whoever speaks best.

**Host A:** The two funeral speeches.

**Host B:** The two funeral speeches. The centre of the whole play. Brutus speaks first. And you must hear what he does, because it is so strange. He is the most philosophical man in Rome, and he speaks in prose.

**Brutus:** Romans, countrymen, and lovers! hear me for my cause, and be silent, that you may hear: believe me for mine honour, and have respect to mine honour, that you may believe: censure me in your wisdom, and awake your senses, that you may the better judge.

**Brutus:** Not that I loved Caesar less, but that I loved Rome more. Had you rather Caesar were living and die all slaves, than that Caesar were dead, to live all free men? As Caesar loved me, I weep for him; as he was fortunate, I rejoice at it; as he was valiant, I honour him: but, as he was ambitious, I slew him.

**Host A:** It is beautifully balanced. Almost mathematical. Each clause answering the other.

**Host B:** It is a lecture. A brilliant one. Tears for his love, joy for his fortune, honour for his valour, and death for his ambition. And the crowd cheers. They even shout, let him be Caesar. Which tells you they have understood nothing.

**Host A:** Let him be Caesar. They want to crown the man who just killed Caesar for wanting a crown.

**Host B:** And then Brutus makes the most fatal decision in the play. He leaves. He lets Mark Antony speak, alone, to the crowd he has just warmed up. And Antony begins.

**Antony:** Friends, Romans, countrymen, lend me your ears; I come to bury Caesar, not to praise him. The evil that men do lives after them; The good is oft interred with their bones; So let it be with Caesar. The noble Brutus Hath told you Caesar was ambitious: If it were so, it was a grievous fault, And grievously hath Caesar answer'd it.

**Antony:** He was my friend, faithful and just to me: But Brutus says he was ambitious; And Brutus is an honourable man. He hath brought many captives home to Rome Whose ransoms did the general coffers fill: Did this in Caesar seem ambitious? When that the poor have cried, Caesar hath wept: Ambition should be made of sterner stuff: Yet Brutus says he was ambitious; And Brutus is an honourable man.

**Host A:** And Brutus is an honourable man. Every time it is repeated, it means less.

**Host B:** Every time it is repeated, it means the opposite. By the fourth time, the word honourable has become an insult, and Antony has not said a single word against Brutus. He has done it purely by repetition and placement. And notice the timing. He stops, as though overcome with grief.

**Antony:** Bear with me; My heart is in the coffin there with Caesar, And I must pause till it come back to me.

**Host A:** And in that pause, the crowd starts talking among themselves, and turning.

**Host B:** It is the most famous pause in drama. Then he shows them Caesar's cloak, with the holes where the daggers went in.

**Antony:** If you have tears, prepare to shed them now. **Host A:** He cannot possibly know which hole is which. He was not there.

**Host B:** He was not there. He invents it. And it does not matter. Then the line that finishes Brutus.

**Antony:** This was the most unkindest cut of all.

**Host B:** Which is, if you think about it, a terrifying thing for a man to say about a riot he has just started.

**Host A:** A mender of bad soles. He is quicker than the tribune. So the commoners are not stupid. They are witty, and cheerful, and on holiday. The problem is not that the people are fools. The problem is that the clever men keep treating them as an audience to be played.

**Host A:** Every time gentler than other. That is a brilliant piece of reporting. He refused it, but each refusal was softer, as if he wanted to be asked again.

**Host B:** And Casca adds, to my thinking, he would fain have had it. So we never see the crucial event. We see a cynic's version of it. **Casca:** If the tag-rag people did not clap him and hiss him, according as he pleased and displeased them, as they use to do the players in the theatre, I am no true man.

**Host A:** And there is a brilliant reading of all this by the French thinker René Girard, in his book on Shakespeare called A Theater of Envy.
