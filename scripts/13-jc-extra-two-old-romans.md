# Julius Caesar Extra. Two Old Romans

**Host A:** Let's do something a little irresponsible. Imagine Philippi went differently. Nobody ran onto a sword. Brutus and Antony both lived, and lived far too long. Thirty years later, two old Romans, somewhere quiet, with a jug of bad wine between them. I will be Brutus. You be Antony.

**Host B:** Gladly. I have always wanted to be the one with the good speech.

**Host A:** And because these two have had thirty years with nothing to do, they have read everything ever written about them. Every critic, every professor, every review. Which, frankly, has not helped either of them.

**Host B:** Agreed. Old Antony, old Brutus. Let's go.

**Host A:** Antony. You look terrible.

**Host B:** Brutus. You look exactly as you did at Philippi. Grey, upright, and disappointed in everyone.

**Host A:** I am not disappointed in everyone. I am disappointed in myself. It is a much more exclusive club.

**Host B:** Oh, here we go. Thirty years, and the first thing out of your mouth is self pity. You know what A. C. Bradley called you? The hero. In quotation marks. A whole century of Englishmen telling you that you were the hero, and you still manage to sound like a man who lost his sandals.

**Host A:** I did lose my sandals. At Philippi. In the mud. Nobody ever writes about that.

**Host B:** Nobody writes about anything except you, Brutus. Schlegel, the German, said you were the hero and not Caesar. Charles Gildon, three hundred years ago, called you, and I quote, the shining and darling character of the poet. The shining and darling character. I gave the most famous speech in the history of the theatre, and I am not anyone's darling.

**Host A:** You are the darling of every drama teacher who needs a seventeen year old to shout in a toga.

**Host B:** That is true, and I accept it as tribute.

**Host A:** Can I tell you what keeps me awake?

**Host B:** Must you?

**Host A:** The word honourable.

**Host B:** Ah.

**Host A:** You said it four times. In front of everyone. And Brutus is an honourable man. And Brutus is an honourable man. By the fourth time, children in the street were saying it to each other as a joke. Honourable became a word you say with your eyebrows.

**Host B:** I was being sincere.

**Host A:** You were not being sincere.

**Host B:** I was being sincere in a way that happened to be devastating. That is called rhetoric, Brutus. You would know if you had used any verse in your speech instead of that little prose lecture.

**Host A:** My speech was balanced.

**Host B:** Your speech was a spreadsheet. As Caesar loved me, I weep for him. As he was fortunate, I rejoice at it. As he was valiant, I honour him. But as he was ambitious, I slew him. Tears for his love, joy for his fortune, honour for his valour, death for his ambition. It is a very elegant accounting ledger. I have seen warmer tax returns.

**Host A:** The historian Garry Wills wrote a whole book about our speeches. Rome and Rhetoric. He takes mine seriously.

**Host B:** He takes mine more seriously.

**Host A:** He also says the play has no villains. Which includes you. I have never forgiven him for that.

**Host B:** You see? Even Wills could not bring himself to make me the villain. Because I was not. I was a grieving friend with good timing.

**Host A:** You were a grieving friend who started a riot, went home, and said, and I want this exactly, Mischief, thou art afoot, take thou what course thou wilt. That is not grief, Antony. That is a man setting fire to a city and then going to bed early.

**Host B:** I did not go to bed early. I went to a meeting.

**Host A:** You went to a meeting with Octavius and Lepidus where you sat down with a list of names and decided who would die. You condemned your own nephew. With a spot I damn him. A spot. You put a dot next to your sister's son.

**Host B:** Politics is a team sport.

**Host A:** Politics is a team sport. Thank you. I will have that carved on my tomb.

**Host B:** You do not have a tomb, Brutus. You are alive. That is rather the problem with this conversation.

**Host A:** Is it?

**Host B:** Oh, don't start.

**Host A:** No, I mean it. I think about it constantly. In the play, I die beautifully. I ask one servant after another to hold my sword, and they all refuse, out of love, and then Strato agrees, and I say, Caesar, now be still, I killed not thee with half so good a will. And then you, you of all people, stand over me and say I was the noblest Roman of them all. It is a perfect exit.

**Host B:** It was a very good eulogy. One of my best.

**Host A:** But I did not get the exit. I got thirty years. And it turns out that when you do not get to die nobly, you have to go on living as the man who did it. Every morning. Every bowl of porridge. I get up, I put on my sandals, the new ones, and I am still the man who stabbed his friend.

**Host B:** Brutus. Listen to me. This is going somewhere dark, and I am too old to carry you up the hill again.

**Host A:** Have you ever thought about whether I was actually honourable? Not your speech. Me. Was I?

**Host B:** You want an honest answer from me? From me?

**Host A:** I want an answer from someone who has read the footnotes.

**Host B:** Fine. Your orchard speech. The night before the murder. You are alone, nobody is manipulating you, Cassius has gone home, and you say, and I have it by heart because I have used it in so many arguments, and since the quarrel will bear no colour for the thing he is, fashion it thus.

**Host A:** I know what I said.

**Host B:** Since the quarrel will bear no colour for the thing he is. Since you have no case against Caesar as he actually is. Fashion it thus. Make one up. Imagine what he might become, and kill him for that. A serpent's egg. You killed a man for a hypothetical, Brutus. You killed a man for a draft.

**Host A:** I was being prudent.

**Host B:** You were being a philosopher. Which in Rome is more dangerous.

**Host A:** That critic, Anson, called it self deception.

**Host B:** The critic Anson did, yes. And Norman Rabkin noticed that your orchard scene is immediately followed by Caesar's house, where Caesar is also being talked into something by a flatterer. He called it a balance of perception and self righteous blindness. You and Caesar, side by side, both certain, both wrong.

**Host A:** Thank you, Antony. That is exactly the comfort a depressed old man needs. To be told that he and the man he murdered had identical personality flaws.

**Host B:** You asked.

**Host A:** I did ask. Here is what I have come to think. Everyone says I made the hard choice. The noble, agonising, terrible choice. And I have started to wonder whether it was actually the easy one.

**Host B:** Stabbing Caesar was the easy one?

**Host A:** Think about it. What were the alternatives? I could have argued in the Senate. Slowly. For years. Losing votes, making compromises, being shouted at by Casca. I could have gone to Caesar privately and said, my friend, you are frightening people, stop taking crowns from Antony. I could have waited and seen what he actually did. All of those are hard. They take patience, they take humility, they might fail.

**Host B:** And instead?

**Host A:** And instead, one morning, twenty minutes, a lot of knives, and we all walk out shouting liberty. It is the shortcut. It is the most dramatic, most decisive, most morally simple thing a man can do. You kill the problem. You do not have to live with it. And then you get to call it sacrifice.

**Host B:** Let us be sacrificers, but not butchers, Caius.

**Host A:** Don't quote me to me.

**Host B:** Let's carve him as a dish fit for the gods.

**Host A:** Antony.

**Host B:** Not hew him as a carcass fit for hounds. You were planning the menu, Brutus. You wanted a beautiful murder. Mark Rose called it a political Mass. Naomi Conn Liebler tied all your blood to the Lupercal, the old purification festival. You thought you were a priest.

**Host A:** And Leo Kirschbaum said the blood was literal. That when I told everyone to bathe their arms in it, up to the elbows, it was not a metaphor. Our hands were red. Actual blood. The audience could see it was a slaughter.

**Host B:** You told a crowd of senators to wash their hands in a corpse and then walk to the marketplace waving their weapons and shouting peace. Peace, Brutus. Covered in blood, shouting peace. Even by Roman standards, that was a branding problem.

**Host A:** It made sense at the time.

**Host B:** Everything makes sense at the time. That is why we have historians.

**Host A:** So you agree it was the easy choice.

**Host B:** No. I think it was the easy choice dressed up as the hard choice. Which is worse. Anyone can take the easy road. It takes a real philosopher to convince himself that the easy road is a cross he is bearing for the republic.

**Host A:** You are enjoying this.

**Host B:** Immensely. I have waited thirty years.

**Host A:** Let me try a different question, then. What is honour?

**Host B:** Oh, I know this one. A word.

**Host A:** What?

**Host B:** What is honour? A word. What is in that word honour? What is that honour? Air. That is from another play, by the same man. A fat knight called Falstaff, in Henry the Fourth, Part One. Can honour set to a leg? No. Or an arm? No. Or take away the grief of a wound? No. Honour hath no skill in surgery then? No. What is honour? A word.

**Host A:** That fat man got the best lines in the whole canon.

**Host B:** He did. And he is right about you. Your honour could not set a leg. It could not stop a civil war. It could not save Portia.

**Host A:** Don't.

**Host B:** I'm sorry. I am. That was too far.

**Host A:** No. It's the right place to go. Do you know what I did when they told me she was dead?

**Host B:** I have read the scene.

**Host A:** Then you know. I turned it into a sentence about myself. No man bears sorrow better. Portia is dead. Five words about my own fortitude, before I told my friend that my wife had swallowed fire. And then, a few minutes later, when Messala came in with the same news, I pretended not to know so that I could perform it again, for a larger audience. Even then, Antony. Even then, I was giving a speech.

**Host B:** Some editors think that bit is a mistake. Two drafts of the scene printed by accident.

**Host A:** That is the kindest thing anyone has ever said about me. That my coldness was a printing error.

**Host B:** Coppélia Kahn would say it was not you, it was Rome. Roman virtue. The word virtue comes from vir, the Latin for man. To be good was to be manly, hard, constant. You were raised to believe that a real man does not show grief. Portia even cut her own thigh to prove she could be one of you.

**Host A:** She did it to prove she could keep my secret. And she could. And I still did not tell her until it was too late. And Calpurnia dreamed the whole thing, every detail, the statue running with blood, the smiling Romans washing their hands in it. And she was told she was being hysterical. The only two people in Rome who saw clearly were both women, and both of them were sent indoors.

**Host B:** Gail Kern Paster has a reading where you conspirators turn Caesar's body into a woman's body. Open, bleeding, nursing Rome. Decius says Rome shall suck reviving blood from him.

**Host A:** Decius said a lot of things. Decius was a flatterer.

**Host B:** Decius was very effective. He got Caesar to the Capitol. That is more than you managed to do with the entire Roman people.

**Host A:** Ah. Here it comes.

**Host B:** Here it comes. You lost the crowd, Brutus. You had them. They were cheering you. They shouted, let him be Caesar. Do you remember?

**Host A:** I remember.

**Host B:** They wanted to crown you, the man who had just killed Caesar for wanting a crown. And instead of saying, no, friends, that is exactly the problem, let me explain, you said, good countrymen, let me depart alone, and stay here with Antony. And you left. You left me alone with them.

**Host A:** I thought honour required it. I had promised you could speak.

**Host B:** Honour. There's that word again. Your honour handed me the crowd, the body, and the pulpit. Thank you, by the way. I never said thank you.

**Host A:** You're welcome.

**Host B:** Hazlitt said it was your overconfidence that wrecked the conspiracy. Your trust that the people would see the reasonableness of it. You believed in reason, Brutus, in a city full of people who had just come from a festival where young men run around naked hitting women with goat skin whips.

**Host A:** The Lupercal was a very old tradition.

**Host B:** My point exactly. You thought you were talking to the Senate. You were talking to an audience. Casca said it, remember? The tag rag people clap and hiss Caesar as they do the players in the theatre. They were never going to be persuaded by a balanced sentence. They wanted a cloak with holes in it.

**Host A:** You did not even know which hole was which.

**Host B:** Of course I didn't. I wasn't there. You lot made sure of that, sending Trebonius to chat with me in the corridor. I made it up. Look, in this place ran Cassius' dagger through. See what a rent the envious Casca made. Pure invention. And it worked better than all your truth.

**Host A:** And that does not trouble you?

**Host B:** Trouble me? It was the best afternoon of my life. Do you know what Marlon Brando did with that speech?

**Host A:** Who?

**Host B:** Never mind. Twentieth century. It was very loud, and people still remember it.

**Host A:** You see, this is what I cannot bear. You lied, and you won. I told the truth, and I lost. And everyone still calls you a great speaker and me a great man.

**Host B:** You want to be the great speaker? Fine. But you will have to give up being the great man. You cannot have both. I took the speech. You took the eulogy. It is a fair trade.

**Host A:** Is it? You wrote the eulogy. The noblest Roman of them all. All the conspirators save only he did that they did in envy of great Caesar. He only, in a general honest thought, and common good to all, made one of them. Did you mean that?

**Host B:** Mostly.

**Host A:** Mostly.

**Host B:** I meant that you were not envious. The others were. Cassius was eaten alive with it. That lean and hungry look. René Girard wrote a whole book on Shakespeare called A Theater of Envy. Everybody wanting what everybody else has. Caesar as the scapegoat, the one victim who absorbs everyone's rivalry. And you, Brutus, were the one man who did not want Caesar's place. You wanted Caesar's absence. That is rarer. And more dangerous.

**Host A:** So my great virtue was that I was not jealous of the man I stabbed.

**Host B:** When you put it like that it sounds less impressive, I admit.

**Host A:** Do you know what I really think about, at night? Not the murder. Not even Portia. I think about Cinna.

**Host B:** Which one?

**Host A:** That is exactly the problem. The poet. The one who had the same name as one of us. The crowd you whipped up found him in the street and tore him apart because of his name. I am Cinna the poet, he said. I am not Cinna the conspirator. And they said, tear him for his bad verses. Pluck but his name out of his heart.

**Host B:** That was not my fault.

**Host A:** It was entirely your fault.

**Host B:** It was partly my fault. I lit the fire. I did not tell them which houses to burn.

**Host A:** You said mischief, take thou what course thou wilt. You gave mischief a free choice of routes.

**Host B:** All right. That one stays with me too. Orson Welles put that scene back into the play in 1937, did you know? Directors used to cut it. Welles, aged twenty one, had the poet dragged off by men who looked like secret police, and it stopped the show. The audience understood that the poet was them. Ordinary people with the wrong name in the wrong street when the speeches have done their work.

**Host A:** So we both have Cinna on our consciences.

**Host B:** We both do. Yours started the knives. Mine finished the riot. We are, as a professor would say, co authors.

**Host A:** Can I tell you about Cassius?

**Host B:** Your brother in arms. Your lean and hungry friend.

**Host A:** We had a terrible quarrel in the tent before Philippi. Everybody loves that scene. Johnson said it was universally celebrated.

**Host B:** I know the scene. You accused him of having an itching palm. Of selling offices for gold.

**Host A:** I did. And then, in the very same speech, I complained that he had refused to send me gold to pay my legions.

**Host B:** Oh, this is my favourite part.

**Host A:** I said, and I am ashamed of how proud I was of it, I can raise no money by vile means. By heaven, I had rather coin my heart and drop my blood for drachmas than wring from the hard hands of peasants their vile trash.

**Host B:** Your blood for drachmas. Very noble. And then?

**Host A:** And then I asked why he had not sent me his.

**Host B:** You were too honourable to squeeze the peasants yourself, so you wanted Cassius to squeeze them and post you the proceeds. That is not honour, Brutus. That is outsourcing.

**Host A:** I know.

**Host B:** Your hands stayed clean because his were dirty. You did the same thing with the murder. Cassius did the thinking, the recruiting, the letters thrown through your window, the dirty work. You lent the name. The name of Brutus. And then you lectured him about ethics.

**Host A:** He wept, you know. In that quarrel. The man Caesar feared for thinking too much turned out to be the one who needed to be loved. He bared his chest and handed me his dagger and said, strike, as thou didst at Caesar.

**Host B:** And you?

**Host A:** I said something cool and superior. And we made up. And then I overruled him about the battle.

**Host B:** Ah. Philippi.

**Host A:** He wanted to wait. Let the enemy march to us, tire themselves out, waste their supplies. Sound military sense. And I gave him a speech instead. There is a tide in the affairs of men, which, taken at the flood, leads on to fortune.

**Host B:** A gorgeous speech. Truly. I wept when I read it.

**Host A:** You won the battle because of it.

**Host B:** I won the battle partly because of it. You marched your army down from a perfectly good position to meet us, on the strength of a metaphor about the sea. I have always meant to send you a note of thanks.

**Host A:** The most beautiful speech about seizing the right moment, used to seize the wrong one.

**Host B:** That is your whole career in one sentence. Do you know, there is a line in your own play. Cassius, on the hill, believes his friend Titinius has been captured, when actually he has been welcomed by friends who are cheering him. And Cassius kills himself over the mistake. And Messala says, O hateful error, melancholy's child, why dost thou show to the apt thoughts of men the things that are not?

**Host A:** The things that are not. A Caesar who had not yet become a tyrant. A defeat that had not yet happened.

**Host B:** You both died of imaginary futures. And you are sitting here alive, still imagining them. That, I think, is what your melancholy is. Not guilt exactly. A habit of seeing things that are not.

**Host A:** That night in the tent, after the quarrel, I could not sleep. My servant boy, Lucius, was playing music for me, and he fell asleep over his instrument. And I took it out of his hands so it would not break. That is the last gentle thing I remember doing.

**Host B:** And then the ghost came.

**Host A:** And then the ghost came.

**Host A:** Here is something else I cannot stop thinking about. Was I even right about Caesar? Ernest Schanzer said the play is a problem play. That it refuses to give a plain and clear cut answer. Was he a tyrant or wasn't he?

**Host B:** He was vain. He was deaf in one ear. He fell down in fits in the marketplace. He talked about himself in the third person. Caesar shall go forth. Always I am Caesar. And he refused the crown three times, each time more gently, as if he hoped I would offer it a fourth.

**Host A:** So I was right.

**Host B:** But he had not done anything yet. M. W. MacCallum noticed how Caesar harps on his own courage, which should make us suspicious. Fine. Suspicious. You do not stab a man for being suspicious, Brutus. If we stabbed every Roman who talked about himself too much, the Senate would have been empty by lunchtime.

**Host A:** The providentialists would say I was wrong. Tillyard and that crowd in the nineteen forties. Order, monarchy, the natural shape of things. Caesar's rule was the order, my murder was the crime, the civil war was the punishment.

**Host B:** And Stephen Greenblatt, much more recently, says that idealistic coups spectacularly backfire. That the very act meant to save the republic destroyed it. Which, you will notice, is exactly what happened. Within a generation there was an emperor.

**Host A:** Octavius.

**Host B:** Augustus, they call him now. That cold little boy who arrived at the end and gave the final orders over your body. He was the real winner. Not me. Certainly not you. Him.

**Host A:** Did he win your Egypt too?

**Host B:** That was a different play.

**Host A:** It was a very long play, Antony. With a queen in it.

**Host B:** I am not discussing Cleopatra with you.

**Host A:** I am simply saying. You lectured me for half an hour about the dangers of high ideals, and you ended up losing an empire because you fell in love with the Queen of Egypt and ran after her ship in the middle of a sea battle.

**Host B:** That is a gross simplification of Actium.

**Host A:** Is it?

**Host B:** It is a slight simplification of Actium.

**Host A:** Shakespeare gave you a second play, you know. Antony and Cleopatra. Several years after ours. You are the hero of that one.

**Host B:** I know. I have read the reviews.

**Host A:** And?

**Host B:** Very good reviews.

**Host A:** You see, this is the difference between us. You lost everything for love and got a second play. I lost everything for principle and got a ghost.

**Host B:** Thy evil spirit, Brutus.

**Host A:** That is what it said. Not Caesar's spirit. Mine. My evil spirit.

**Host B:** Edward Dowden said the play bears the name of Julius Caesar with strict propriety. Because Caesar is more powerful dead than alive. You said it yourself, at the end. O Julius Caesar, thou art mighty yet. Thy spirit walks abroad and turns our swords in our own proper entrails.

**Host A:** I wanted to kill the spirit and leave the man. That is what I said. If only we could come by Caesar's spirit and not dismember Caesar. And instead we dismembered the man and let the spirit out. And it moved in with me. It has been living in my head for thirty years. It is a very poor lodger.

**Host B:** Brutus. I am going to stop being clever for a moment.

**Host A:** Can you?

**Host B:** Briefly. You keep asking whether you were honourable. I think you are asking the wrong question. You were honourable. That was the trouble. You were so sure of your honour that you never had to check your reasons. Honour told you that you were the sort of man who does the right thing. So whatever you did must have been the right thing. It is a beautiful machine for not thinking.

**Host A:** That is unbearably harsh.

**Host B:** I know. And the other half is this. You are the only one of us who ever asked the question at all. Cassius never asked it. Casca never asked it. I certainly never asked it. Octavius would not know what the question meant. You are sitting here, thirty years later, still asking whether you deserved the word. That is the most honourable thing about you. Not the knife. The doubt.

**Host A:** So I am honourable because I suspect I am not.

**Host B:** You are honourable now. You were not, that morning. Something like that. A professor would put it better.

**Host A:** Garry Wills would put it better.

**Host B:** Garry Wills would say we were both partly right and both made a catastrophe. Which is a very comfortable thing to say from a library.

**Host A:** Do you know what the worst part is? People keep doing it again. That line of Cassius's. How many ages hence shall this our lofty scene be acted over, in states unborn and accents yet unknown. He meant it as a boast. It turned into a curse.

**Host B:** The Booth brothers.

**Host A:** Three actor brothers in New York, in 1864. One played me. One played Cassius. And the youngest played you.

**Host B:** John Wilkes Booth. He played me.

**Host A:** And five months later he shot a president in a theatre and shouted sic semper tyrannis, and wrote in his diary that he had done what Brutus was honoured for. He played you, Antony, and then he went and became me.

**Host B:** He only heard half the play. The noble Roman. Not the civil war.

**Host A:** People always only hear half. In 2017 in New York a theatre made Caesar look like an American president and stabbed him, and people were furious, and the sponsors fled. And the theatre had to explain that the play actually shows that those who try to defend democracy by undemocratic means pay a terrible price and destroy the very thing they are fighting to save. Which is me. They were describing me.

**Host B:** And a few years before that, in Stratford, they were describing someone else. Paterson Joseph, who played you in a Royal Shakespeare Company production set in a newly independent African state, wrote about leaders who all fought for freedom from colonial rule and all succumbed to the seduction of power. Caesar keeps coming back. So do you.

**Host A:** And Nyerere translated us into Kiswahili.

**Host B:** Juliasi Kaizari. I sound magnificent in Kiswahili.

**Host A:** You sound magnificent in everything. That is your whole problem.

**Host B:** That is my whole charm.

**Host B:** Can I ask you something now? Since we are being honest.

**Host A:** You have never asked me anything in your life. You make statements with question marks on them.

**Host B:** Fair. But this is a real one. That morning. When Caesar looked at you. Et tu, Brute. Then fall, Caesar. What did his face look like?

**Host A:** Nobody has ever asked me that.

**Host B:** I was in the corridor with Trebonius. I invented every wound on that cloak. I never saw his face.

**Host A:** He did not look angry. That is what I remember. He looked as if a sum had come out wrong. As if he had added up all of us and found one too many. And then he covered his face with his mantle.

**Host B:** I said that in the speech. In his mantle muffling up his face. I made it up.

**Host A:** You were right.

**Host B:** Of course I was. The best lies are the ones that turn out to be true. That is the secret of rhetoric, Brutus. You guess what people need to have happened, and you say it as if you saw it.

**Host A:** Antony, one more question, and then I will let you go back to your wine.

**Host B:** It's terrible wine.

**Host A:** I know. I chose it. Was it worth it? Any of it? The speech, the riot, the proscriptions, Philippi, the empire, the Egyptian queen, the second play. Was it worth it?

**Host B:** It was a wonderful speech.

**Host A:** That is not an answer.

**Host B:** It is the only answer I have. I am a man who knows how to make people feel things. I never once asked whether they should feel them. You are a man who knows what people should feel, and could never make them feel it. Together we would have been one good politician. Separately, we were a civil war.

**Host A:** That is the first wise thing you have said all evening.

**Host B:** Don't tell anyone. It will ruin my reputation.

**Host A:** Samuel Johnson said our play was somewhat cold and unaffecting.

**Host B:** Johnson had clearly never been in the forum when I took the cloak off.

**Host A:** Leonard Digges said audiences went wild for our quarrel in the tent. More than for anything Ben Jonson wrote.

**Host B:** Ben Jonson made fun of a line of Caesar's. Caesar did never wrong but with just cause. Called it ridiculous.

**Host A:** I rather liked that line.

**Host B:** Of course you did. It is exactly your kind of line. A man doing wrong, with a just cause. It is the story of your life.

**Host A:** Antony.

**Host B:** Brutus.

**Host A:** Thank you for coming.

**Host B:** Thank you for the terrible wine. Same time next year?

**Host A:** If we live.

**Host B:** We are two fictional Romans who have already outlived the play by thirty years. I think the living is not the problem.

**Host A:** No. The living was never the problem. It was the meaning.

**Host B:** Let's stop there.

**Host A:** All right, back to ourselves. That was strange. I found Brutus more sympathetic as an old man than I ever have as a young one.

**Host B:** And I found Antony more honest. Probably because he had nothing left to win. A man with no crowd to play to starts telling the truth.

**Host A:** The question old Brutus kept circling, whether the knife was actually the easy choice, is a real one, isn't it? It goes right back to the orchard speech. He builds his case, and then he chooses the most dramatic, most final option. Patience, argument, waiting to see what Caesar would actually do, those were harder and less glorious. The play never quite says that out loud, but it is all there in the text.

**Host B:** And the honour question is right there too. The whole play is soaked in the word, and Shakespeare gives Antony the job of emptying it out by repetition. And a couple of years earlier he had given Falstaff the job of emptying it out by logic. What is honour? A word. Air.

**Host A:** And yet, the play never quite lets the word die. Antony means it at the end, at least partly. The noblest Roman of them all. It survives the irony.

**Host B:** Like Brutus. Bruised, quoted against himself, still standing.

**Host A:** Thank you for listening to Books on Pods.
