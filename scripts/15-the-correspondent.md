# The Correspondent. Virginia Evans, and the Life in Letters

**Host B (the letter writer):** Can I start with a confession? When I was a kid, I had pen pals. Not one. A lot of them. At one point I think I had fourteen. A boy three hundred miles away at the other end of my state, a girl on the far side of the country, a friend of a friend who had moved abroad. And I would come home from school and the first thing I did, before food, before anything, was check the post.

**Host A (the skeptic):** Fourteen. I think I have written, in my entire life, maybe five letters. And most of those were thank you notes my mother made me write.

**Host B (the letter writer):** Which is why I want to talk about this with you. Because I think you have missed something, and I am going to spend the next forty minutes trying to make you feel it.

**Host A (the skeptic):** That is a lot of pressure for a thank you note writer.

**Host B (the letter writer):** Let me tell you what it was like. You would get a letter, say on a Tuesday. You would not open it straight away. You would carry it around. You would look at the handwriting on the envelope, the stamp, the postmark. And then you would find a quiet place, and open it, and read it once fast, greedily, and then again, slowly.

**Host A (the skeptic):** Twice?

**Host B (the letter writer):** At least twice. Sometimes three times. And then you would put it on your desk, and for days it would sit there, and you would be composing the answer in your head. On the bus, in the shower, in boring lessons.

**Host A (the skeptic):** Okay. That part I can almost picture.

**Host B (the letter writer):** And the objects. You have to understand the objects. The girl on the other side of the country used thin blue airmail paper, so thin you could see the writing from the back. The boy at the other end of my state wrote in green ink, always green, I never found out why. One friend drew little cartoons in the margins. One always put a joke in the postscript, so you learned to read the P S first, like dessert.

**Host A (the skeptic):** Postscripts were a thing?

**Host B (the letter writer):** Postscripts were everything. Some people saved the most important thing for the P S, as if they had not dared to put it in the letter itself. And people sent things. A photograph. A pressed leaf from a tree outside their window. A newspaper cutting with an article they thought I would like. A ticket stub from a concert. Once, a single feather, with no explanation.

**Host A (the skeptic):** A feather.

**Host B (the letter writer):** I still have it. And I still do not know what it meant. Which is part of the point. A letter is a physical thing that has travelled from someone's hands to yours. You are holding something they held.

**Host A (the skeptic):** What happened to them? The fourteen.

**Host B (the letter writer):** That is the sad part. They faded, one by one. Someone would take a little longer to reply. Then longer. Then a letter would come that was shorter than usual, and you could feel it in the shortness. And then one day you realise you have not heard for three months, and you write a cheerful little letter pretending nothing has changed, and nothing comes back.

**Host A (the skeptic):** Ghosted by post.

**Host B (the letter writer):** Ghosted by post, long before the word existed. And it hurt, honestly, more than people think. Because you had shared so much on paper. But I think that is part of the education too. The letters teach you that every correspondence ends. And that you should say the important things while the other person is still writing back.

**Host B (the letter writer):** And then there was the form of the reply. Which is the thing I really want you to understand. A good reply was not just news. It was a conversation stretched across two weeks. So you would go through their letter point by point. You asked about my sister. She is fine, she has broken her arm, here is how. You said you had been reading a book I recommended, and you hated the ending. I want to argue with you about that. You asked what I thought about your new teacher. Here is what I think.

**Host A (the skeptic):** Like answering an exam paper.

**Host B (the letter writer):** Like answering an exam paper you actually cared about. And then, after you had answered all their points, you would tell them what had happened to you since your last letter. The interval. Everything that had happened in those two weeks. And here is the strange part. You would be telling the same events to several people. Because I had fourteen of these going.

**Host A (the skeptic):** So you were telling the story of your fortnight fourteen times.

**Host B (the letter writer):** Not fourteen identical times. That is the point. Each person had picked up different things from my last letter. One friend always asked about my grandmother, so in his letter I would tell him about her, and in telling him, I would notice things about her I had not noticed. Another friend loved arguments, so to her I would tell the same week as a series of things I disagreed with. Another was going through a hard time, so to him I would tell the gentle version.

**Host A (the skeptic):** That sounds exhausting.

**Host B (the letter writer):** It was wonderful. Because think about what it was doing. Several times a month, I was retelling my own life, to different people, from different angles, with different emphasis. Each retelling refined it a bit. I would find a better way to describe something. I would realise that the thing I thought was funny was actually sad. Or the thing I was upset about was actually ridiculous. The same event, turned in the light, again and again, until I understood it.

**Host A (the skeptic):** So the letters were a way of thinking.

**Host B (the letter writer):** They were the best way of thinking I have ever had. Someone once said that the unexamined life is not worth living. Well, the epistolary life is the most examined life there is. You cannot help examining it, because you have to explain it to someone you love, in writing, at a distance, and they are going to write back and ask you what you meant.

**Host A (the skeptic):** That was Socrates, by the way. The unexamined life. He did not write anything down at all.

**Host B (the letter writer):** Which might explain a lot about Socrates.

**Host A (the skeptic):** All right. I want to push back. Because I hear you, and it sounds lovely, but I am a bit suspicious.

**Host B (the letter writer):** Of course you are.

**Host A (the skeptic):** You say you were retelling your life fourteen times and refining it. Isn't that just what we now call curating? You are not examining your life. You are polishing it. Choosing the best angle for each audience. That is exactly what people complain about with social media. Everyone presents a version of themselves.

**Host B (the letter writer):** That is a serious objection. And I want to take it properly.

**Host A (the skeptic):** Please.

**Host B (the letter writer):** Here is the difference, I think. Social media is a broadcast. You polish one version for everyone at once, and the response is instant, and it is mostly a number. Likes, views. A letter is addressed to one person. You are not performing for a crowd. You are trying to be understood by someone specific, who knows you, who remembers what you said last time, and who will notice if you are being false.

**Host A (the skeptic):** They will notice?

**Host B (the letter writer):** Oh, they will notice. I had a pen pal who, every single time I wrote that I was fine, would write back, you said you were fine three times in that letter, so what is actually wrong? You cannot hide from a good correspondent. The delay works against hiding. They have days to read your letter. They read between the lines.

**Host A (the skeptic):** Hm.

**Host B (the letter writer):** And there is the other thing. Time. Nothing in a letter happens in real time. By the time you write about something, it has already happened. Days ago, maybe weeks. You are not reacting. You are reflecting. And by the time they read it, more time has passed. So what you get is not the event. It is the event, filtered through you, filtered through time, and then received by someone else at another moment in their life.

**Host A (the skeptic):** Which sounds less like a conversation and more like a series of essays.

**Host B (the letter writer):** It is both. That is the magic. It is an essay addressed to a friend.

**Host A (the skeptic):** I am going to concede a little. I can see that writing to one person who knows you is different from posting to everyone. But I still think there is something artificial about it. When I talk to someone, I can hear their voice, see their face, they interrupt me. A letter is a monologue. You get to say everything you want and no one can stop you.

**Host B (the letter writer):** Which is exactly why it is so revealing. People say things in letters they would never say aloud. Because no one is interrupting them. Because there is a distance. Because they can take their time and find the words.

**Host A (the skeptic):** Or because they can hide behind the words.

**Host B (the letter writer):** Both. And that tension, hiding and revealing at once, is exactly why letters became one of the great forms of the novel.

**Host A (the skeptic):** Okay. Now you have moved from your childhood to literature. I sense a lecture.

**Host B (the letter writer):** A short one. Well. A medium one.

**Host A (the skeptic):** Go on.

**Host B (the letter writer):** The epistolary novel. A novel told in letters. And it is very old. The scholar Janet Gurkin Altman, in a book called Epistolarity, from 1982, traces the letter as an art form all the way from the Roman poet Ovid to Saul Bellow. Ovid wrote a collection called the Heroides, imaginary letters from the heroines of myth to the men who had left them. Penelope writing to Ulysses, asking when he is coming home.

**Host A (the skeptic):** So the first letters in literature are from women who have been abandoned.

**Host B (the letter writer):** Which tells you something about the form. A letter is written across an absence. You only write to someone who is not there.

**Host A (the skeptic):** That is actually very good.

**Host B (the letter writer):** But the novel in letters really takes off in eighteenth century England, with a printer called Samuel Richardson. In 1740 he publishes Pamela. A young servant girl, writing to her parents about the master of the house, who is trying to seduce her. It begins like this.

**Pamela:** Dear Father and Mother, I have great trouble, and some comfort, to acquaint you with.

**Host A (the skeptic):** Great trouble, and some comfort. That is a good opening line.

**Host B (the letter writer):** And it was a sensation. People read it aloud in groups. And Richardson had a phrase for what he was doing. He called it writing to the moment. Because Pamela writes while things are still happening. She does not know how her story ends. She writes in fear, in hope, in confusion, and the reader is right there with her.

**Host A (the skeptic):** Now hang on. If she is writing while it is happening, then my objection comes back. Who stops in the middle of being chased around a house by a nobleman to write a long letter to their parents?

**Host B (the letter writer):** You are in good company. Henry Fielding thought exactly the same thing. In 1741, a year after Pamela, he published a parody called Shamela. In which the heroine writes letters at absurd moments, describing things happening to her in the present tense as she writes. Fielding thought the whole form was ridiculous, and that Pamela was not innocent but a calculating schemer.

**Host A (the skeptic):** I think I like Fielding.

**Host B (the letter writer):** Everyone likes Fielding. But Richardson kept going. In 1748 he published Clarissa, which is one of the longest novels in the English language. Close to a million words. Hundreds of letters between multiple writers. And it is a tragedy, about a young woman pursued and destroyed by a man called Lovelace. And because we read Lovelace's letters too, we see inside the villain's head at the same time as we see inside the victim's.

**Host A (the skeptic):** A million words of letters.

**Host B (the letter writer):** And Samuel Johnson, who loved Richardson, gave the most famous warning about him. He told Boswell, if you were to read Richardson for the story, your impatience would be so much fretted that you would hang yourself. But you must read him for the sentiment, and consider the story as only giving occasion to the sentiment.

**Host A (the skeptic):** That is a backhanded compliment if ever I heard one.

**Host B (the letter writer):** But it is also exactly right about the form. In an epistolary novel, the plot is slow. Because everything has to be written down, sent, received, answered. What you get instead is the inside. The feeling. The reflection. The story is only the occasion for the letters.

**Host A (the skeptic):** So you are saying the slowness is the point.

**Host B (the letter writer):** The slowness is the point. And then the French took the form and turned it into a weapon. Pierre Choderlos de Laclos, in 1782, Les Liaisons dangereuses. Dangerous Liaisons. Two aristocrats, the Marquise de Merteuil and the Vicomte de Valmont, who use letters to seduce, deceive and destroy people. And the brilliance of it is that we, the readers, see every letter. We know who is lying to whom. Every character only knows the letters they receive. We know them all.

**Host A (the skeptic):** So the reader becomes the only person who sees the whole picture.

**Host B (the letter writer):** Exactly. That is one of the great structural powers of the form. The reader as the ultimate eavesdropper. Reading everyone's private post.

**Host A (the skeptic):** Which is a bit creepy, when you put it like that.

**Host B (the letter writer):** It is meant to be. Laclos makes you complicit. You enjoy the cruelty because you are reading it in private, as they wrote it in private.

**Host A (the skeptic):** All right, I am interested now. What else?

**Host B (the letter writer):** Goethe. The Sorrows of Young Werther, 1774. A young man writes letters to his friend about falling hopelessly in love with a woman who is engaged to someone else. And we only ever see Werther's letters. Never the replies.

**Host A (the skeptic):** So it is one side of a conversation.

**Host B (the letter writer):** One side. And that silence is unbearable. You keep wanting to know what his friend is saying back, whether anyone is trying to help him. And the absence of the replies becomes part of the tragedy. It made Werther a European sensation. Young men dressed like him, in a blue coat and a yellow waistcoat.

**Host A (the skeptic):** The first fan merchandise.

**Host B (the letter writer):** More or less. And then there are novels that use letters as a frame. Frankenstein, in 1818, begins with letters from an explorer named Robert Walton to his sister in England, as he sails toward the North Pole. The whole story of Victor Frankenstein and his creature reaches us inside Walton's letters home. Listen to how it opens.

**Walton:** You will rejoice to hear that no disaster has accompanied the commencement of an enterprise which you have regarded with such evil forebodings.

**Host A (the skeptic):** That is wonderfully ironic, given what happens.

**Host B (the letter writer):** And Dracula, in 1897, is built entirely from documents. Letters, diaries, newspaper cuttings, a ship's log, even recordings made on a phonograph and typed up. A whole group of people, each with their own fragment, and the vampire defeated partly by someone collating all the papers into one typewritten file.

**Host A (the skeptic):** So the heroes win by doing the admin.

**Host B (the letter writer):** They win by reading each other's letters. Which is exactly the reader's position. And this is where I think the form becomes genuinely profound. In an ordinary novel, a narrator tells you what happened. In a novel in letters, nobody tells you what happened. You only get versions. Each character's version, at a particular moment, to a particular person.

**Host A (the skeptic):** So the truth is somewhere in between.

**Host B (the letter writer):** The truth is in the gaps. Between the letters, between the writers, between what was meant and what was understood. And that gives you a kind of layering you cannot get any other way. The same event told several times. Told to different people. Told before and after the writer understood it. Told in a version that is softened for one reader and sharpened for another.

**Host A (the skeptic):** Which is exactly what you were doing with your fourteen pen pals.

**Host B (the letter writer):** Exactly what I was doing. The epistolary novel is the epistolary life, written down and read by a stranger.

**Host A (the skeptic):** Give me the actual mechanics, though. What can a novelist do with letters that they cannot do otherwise?

**Host B (the letter writer):** Several things. One is the time lag. In a novel in letters, there is always a gap between something happening and someone hearing about it. So the reader often knows something terrible that a character does not know yet, because the letter carrying the news is still in the post. That is suspense built out of the postal service.

**Host A (the skeptic):** Dramatic irony by mail.

**Host B (the letter writer):** Exactly. Another is the crossed letter. Two people write to each other at the same time, and their letters cross, so each one is answering a letter the other has not received yet. Misunderstandings pile up. Writers have had a lot of fun with that.

**Host A (the skeptic):** I can imagine.

**Host B (the letter writer):** Another is the unreliable narrator, multiplied. In a first person novel, you have one unreliable narrator. In a novel of letters, everyone is a narrator, and everyone is unreliable in a different way, to a different person. And then there is the letter as an object in the plot. Letters get stolen, hidden, burned, intercepted, read by the wrong person. A letter can be evidence. A letter can be a weapon. A letter can arrive too late.

**Host A (the skeptic):** And Altman has a list for all this?

**Host B (the letter writer):** She does. She defines the form through six structures. Mediation, the letter as the thing in between two people. Confidentiality, the privacy of it. Readership, the fact that every letter is shaped by who it is for. Closure, the question of how a story told in letters can ever end, when every letter invites a reply. Discourse, the way letters talk. And narrativity, the way a story gets built out of them.

**Host A (the skeptic):** Closure is an interesting one. How do you end a correspondence?

**Host B (the letter writer):** That is the question every epistolary novel has to solve. Someone stops writing. Someone dies. Someone arrives in person, so the letters are no longer needed. Or the last letter goes unanswered, and the silence is the ending.

**Host A (the skeptic):** Okay. I want to be the devil's advocate again, though.

**Host B (the letter writer):** You have been nothing else.

**Host A (the skeptic):** Isn't the epistolary novel a bit of a museum piece? It peaked in the seventeen hundreds. Nobody writes letters any more. If you wrote a novel now in letters, wouldn't it feel like a costume drama?

**Host B (the letter writer):** That is exactly what people thought. And they were wrong. Altman's whole argument in Epistolarity is that the form never disappeared. It kept coming back, in new shapes. Jane Austen, for example. Her early novella Lady Susan is entirely in letters. And there is a family tradition that the first version of Sense and Sensibility, called Elinor and Marianne, was written in letters too. Scholars argue about it. The critic D. W. Harding thought the story improbable, because it rests on a note made much later by Austen's niece. But most critics accept it.

**Host A (the skeptic):** So Austen learned her craft in letters and then moved away from them.

**Host B (the letter writer):** And she never quite let them go. The most famous moment in Persuasion is a letter. Captain Wentworth, who was rejected by Anne Elliot eight years before, is in a room with her, overhearing her talk about whether women love longer than men. And he cannot speak. So he writes, there and then, and leaves the letter for her.

**Wentworth:** You pierce my soul. I am half agony, half hope. Tell me not that I am too late, that such precious feelings are gone for ever.

**Wentworth:** I have loved none but you.

**Host A (the skeptic):** Oh.

**Host B (the letter writer):** That is a letter written in a room full of people. Writing to the moment, if ever there was one. The critic Mary Favret called it a silent explosion.

**Host A (the skeptic):** And what does Anne do?

**Host B (the letter writer):** She reads it, and she is overwhelmed, and she cannot speak to anyone for a while, because everything has changed and nobody else in the room knows. That is the thing about letters. An entire life can turn over in a room full of people, and only one person knows, because only one person has read the page.

**Host A (the skeptic):** That is true of texts too, though. Someone reads a message across a dinner table and their face changes.

**Host B (the letter writer):** It is true. But Wentworth could not have sent that as a text. It took him the whole conversation he was overhearing to write it. You can hear the pressure building in the sentences. Half agony, half hope. A text would have said, still love you, call me.

**Host A (the skeptic):** A silent explosion. That is lovely. All right, Austen counts. But that is still a long time ago.

**Host B (the letter writer):** Then let us come forward. In 1964, Saul Bellow published Herzog, about a man in the middle of a breakdown who writes letters, constantly, to everyone. His ex wife, his friends, famous philosophers, even dead ones, even the President. And he almost never sends them.

**Host A (the skeptic):** Unsent letters.

**Host B (the letter writer):** Unsent letters. Hold onto that, because it matters later. In 1970, Helene Hanff published 84 Charing Cross Road, which is not a novel but real letters. Twenty years of correspondence between a New York writer who could not afford rare books and a bookseller in London named Frank Doel, at the shop Marks and Company. They never meet. And it is one of the most loved little books of the century.

**Host A (the skeptic):** I have heard of that one.

**Host B (the letter writer):** And here is something about real letters that matters for all of this. They last. I have a shoebox of every letter my pen pals sent me. A few years ago I got it down and read them again. And what I found was not really them. It was me. Because their letters were full of replies to things I had said. You said you were scared about moving schools. You said you thought you might want to be a doctor. You said your dog was ill. My own younger self, preserved in other people's answers.

**Host A (the skeptic):** That is actually quite moving.

**Host B (the letter writer):** It is strange. I had forgotten almost all of it. And there it all was, kept by someone else's handwriting. A letter is a kind of external memory. And not just for individuals. Historians depend on letters. So much of what we know about how people actually felt in the past comes from private letters they never expected anyone else to read.

**Host A (the skeptic):** Which is a little unnerving, if you think about your own letters being read in two hundred years.

**Host B (the letter writer):** That is the risk of writing honestly. And the reward.

**Host A (the skeptic):** Give me an example. A real one.

**Host B (the letter writer):** Abigail Adams, writing to her husband John in March 1776, while he was helping to plan American independence. She told him that in the new code of laws, he should remember the ladies, and be more generous and favourable to them than their ancestors had been. A private letter between a wife and a husband, and it has become one of the most quoted documents of the founding of the United States.

**Host A (the skeptic):** Remember the ladies. In a private letter.

**Host B (the letter writer):** Or Sullivan Ballou, a Union officer in the American Civil War, who wrote to his wife Sarah a week before the first battle of Bull Run in 1861, telling her how much he loved her and that if he did not return, she should know he would always be near her. He was killed in that battle. Millions of people know that letter now, from the Ken Burns documentary about the war. A letter written to one woman, read aloud to a whole country.

**Host A (the skeptic):** So letters often end up reaching people they were never meant for.

**Host B (the letter writer):** Which is exactly the position of the reader of an epistolary novel. You are always reading someone else's post. And the best letters seem to know, somewhere, that a stranger might be listening.

**Host B (the letter writer):** Everyone who loves letters has read it. In 1982, Alice Walker published The Color Purple, which won the Pulitzer Prize. Celie, a young Black woman in the American South, abused and silenced, writes letters to God, because she has no one else to write to. And later, letters to and from her sister Nettie, which have been hidden from her.

**Host A (the skeptic):** Letters to God. That is the ultimate absent reader.

**Host B (the letter writer):** And it is devastating. Because the form tells you how alone she is before the content does. Then 1991, Nick Bantock's Griffin and Sabine, where you actually open little envelopes glued into the book and pull out the letters. In 2004, Marilynne Robinson's Gilead, which won the Pulitzer, is a single long letter from an elderly minister to his young son, to be read after his death.

**Host A (the skeptic):** A letter from the past to the future.

**Host B (the letter writer):** Which every letter is, really. Then there is Lionel Shriver's We Need to Talk About Kevin, letters from a mother to her absent husband about their son. The Guernsey Literary and Potato Peel Pie Society, by Mary Ann Shaffer and Annie Barrows, letters just after the Second World War. Maria Semple's Where'd You Go, Bernadette, made of emails, notes, and documents.

**Host A (the skeptic):** All right. I accept that the form is alive. But that list also proves my point a little. Emails, documents, files. The letter keeps changing into something else.

**Host B (the letter writer):** Yes. And that is the synthesis, I think. The form survives because the thing it is about survives. Absence, distance, the gap between writing and reading, the self you construct for a particular reader. Whether it is a quill, a typewriter or an email, those things do not change.

**Host A (the skeptic):** And then there are real letters, which are their own kind of literature.

**Host B (the letter writer):** Some of the greatest writing in English is in letters. John Keats came up with his most famous idea about poetry, negative capability, the ability to remain in uncertainties, mysteries, doubts, without reaching after fact and reason, in a letter to his brothers in December 1817. Flannery O'Connor's letters, collected as The Habit of Being, are as funny and fierce as her stories. Elizabeth Bishop and Robert Lowell wrote to each other for thirty years, and their letters are collected as Words in Air.

**Host A (the skeptic):** So some writers are at their best in letters, because they are not trying to write literature.

**Host B (the letter writer):** They are just trying to reach one person. And that is the secret. When you try to reach one person, you often reach everyone.

**Host A (the skeptic):** I have to say, you are slowly getting to me.

**Host B (the letter writer):** Good. Because now I want to tell you about a book. And this is the real reason I wanted to do this episode. I have read it. You have not. And I am going to make you read it.

**Host A (the skeptic):** Go on then.

**Host B (the letter writer):** It is called The Correspondent, by Virginia Evans. Published in April 2025. And it went straight onto my favourites shelf, which I have not done with a new novel in a very long time.

**Host A (the skeptic):** High praise. Who is it about?

**Host B (the letter writer):** A woman called Sybil Van Antwerp. She has just turned seventy three. She is a retired lawyer, divorced, living alone in Annapolis. And she writes letters. She has written letters all her life. By hand, on good stationery she orders from England. And the whole novel is her letters, plus a few emails, and some of the letters people write back to her, over about ten years, from 2012 to 2022.

**Host A (the skeptic):** Who does she write to?

**Host B (the letter writer):** That is one of the joys of it. Everyone. Her brother, Felix. Her best friend. Her children. Former colleagues. And then people who have no idea who she is. She writes to authors, to tell them what she thought of their books. Joan Didion. Larry McMurtry. Ann Patchett.

**Host A (the skeptic):** She writes fan mail?

**Host B (the letter writer):** Not exactly fan mail. More like critical mail. She is quite capable of telling a famous author exactly where they went wrong. And she writes to the president of a university who will not let her audit a class she wants to take, which is a wonderful running battle.

**Host A (the skeptic):** She sounds formidable.

**Host B (the letter writer):** She is formidable. She is also, in the words of a lot of reviewers, difficult. The reviewer Jayne, at Dear Author, said readers may have to grit their teeth at her sometimes, that she can be stubborn, intolerant and self righteous. On NPR, Mary Louise Kelly called her a bit cranky and uptight, but said you end up rooting for her.

**Host A (the skeptic):** So not a lovable grandmother.

**Host B (the letter writer):** Not remotely. She is sharp, funny, opinionated, and she has made mistakes, serious ones, that she has not faced. And the letters are how she slowly faces them. Library Journal said each letter is a compelling monologue that carries raw emotion and grief alongside humour and tart observations. Kirkus said it is thoughtful at times and laugh out loud funny at others.

**Host A (the skeptic):** But what actually happens? If it is all letters, is there a plot?

**Host B (the letter writer):** There is a plot, and I am not going to spoil it, because you are going to read it. But I will tell you the shape. There are two threads that pull you through. One is that Sybil is writing letters to someone she never sends them to. Letters with just a date at the top, no name. And you do not know who they are for.

**Host A (the skeptic):** Like Herzog.

**Host B (the letter writer):** Like Herzog. Except instead of a man ranting at the whole world, it is a woman writing to one particular person she cannot or will not send them to. And you spend a lot of the book wondering who it is and why.

**Host A (the skeptic):** That is a good hook.

**Host A (the skeptic):** Practical question, though. If it is all letters, to dozens of people, over ten years, is it hard to follow? Some of these epistolary novels make you keep a chart.

**Host B (the letter writer):** A little, at the very beginning. The Dear Author review said it takes some time to settle in and keep track of who is writing to whom, and I think that is fair. But every letter is clearly headed, with the date and the name, so you are never lost for long. And by about a third of the way in, you know these people. You recognise their voices before you look at the name. Her brother writes one way, her friend another. That is part of the pleasure. You learn to read handwriting, so to speak.

**Host B (the letter writer):** And the other thread is that someone from Sybil's past writes to her, and it forces her to go back to a painful time in her life she has avoided. And the question becomes whether she can forgive, and whether she can be forgiven.

**Host A (the skeptic):** And this is told only in letters.

**Host B (the letter writer):** Only in letters. And here is why I think it is such a perfect example of everything we have been talking about. Because Sybil is doing exactly what I did with my pen pals. She is telling the same life to different people. To her brother, she is one way. To her best friend, another. To Joan Didion, she is an intellectual.
