# The Correspondent. Solo version (script only, not recorded)

**Host:** Can I start with a confession? When I was a kid, I had pen pals. Not one. A lot of them. At one point I think I had fourteen. A boy three hundred miles away at the other end of my state, a girl on the far side of the country, a friend of a friend who had moved abroad. And I would come home from school and the first thing I did, before food, before anything, was check the post.

**Host:** Fourteen. I know. Some of you listening have written, in your entire lives, maybe five letters, and most of those were thank you notes your mother made you write. And that is exactly who I want to talk to today. Because I think you have missed something, and I want to spend the next forty minutes trying to make you feel it.

**Host:** So let me tell you what it was like. You would get a letter, say on a Tuesday. You would not open it straight away. You would carry it around. You would look at the handwriting on the envelope, the stamp, the postmark. And then you would find a quiet place, and open it, and read it once fast, greedily, and then again, slowly.

**Host:** Twice? Yes, at least twice. Sometimes three times. And then you would put it on your desk, and for days it would sit there, and you would be composing the answer in your head. On the bus, in the shower, in boring lessons.

**Host:** And the objects. You have to understand the objects. The girl on the other side of the country used thin blue airmail paper, so thin you could see the writing from the back. The boy at the other end of my state wrote in green ink, always green, and I never found out why. One friend drew little cartoons in the margins. One always put a joke in the postscript, so you learned to read the P S first, like dessert.

**Host:** Postscripts, you might ask, were they really a thing? Postscripts were everything. Some people saved the most important thing for the P S, as if they had not dared to put it in the letter itself. And people sent things. A photograph. A pressed leaf from a tree outside their window. A newspaper cutting with an article they thought I would like. A ticket stub from a concert. Once, a single feather, with no explanation.

**Host:** A feather. I still have it. And I still do not know what it meant. Which is part of the point. A letter is a physical thing that has travelled from someone's hands to yours. You are holding something they held.

**Host:** So what happened to them, the fourteen? Well, that is the sad part. They faded, one by one. Someone would take a little longer to reply. Then longer. Then a letter would come that was shorter than usual, and you could feel it in the shortness. And then one day you realise you have not heard for three months, and you write a cheerful little letter pretending nothing has changed, and nothing comes back.

**Host:** Ghosted by post, long before the word existed. And it hurt, honestly, more than people think. Because you had shared so much on paper. But I think that is part of the education too. The letters teach you that every correspondence ends. And that you should say the important things while the other person is still writing back.

**Host:** And then there was the form of the reply. Which is the thing I really want you to understand. A good reply was not just news. It was a conversation stretched across two weeks. So you would go through their letter point by point. You asked about my sister. She is fine, she has broken her arm, here is how. You said you had been reading a book I recommended, and you hated the ending. I want to argue with you about that. You asked what I thought about your new teacher. Here is what I think.

**Host:** Like answering an exam paper, you might say. Yes, but an exam paper you actually cared about. And then, after you had answered all their points, you would tell them what had happened to you since your last letter. The interval. Everything that had happened in those two weeks. And here is the strange part. You would be telling the same events to several people. Because I had fourteen of these going.

**Host:** So was I telling the story of my fortnight fourteen times? Not fourteen identical times. That is the point. Each person had picked up different things from my last letter. One friend always asked about my grandmother, so in his letter I would tell him about her, and in telling him, I would notice things about her I had not noticed. Another friend loved arguments, so to her I would tell the same week as a series of things I disagreed with. Another was going through a hard time, so to him I would tell the gentle version.

**Host:** Exhausting? It was wonderful. Because think about what it was doing. Several times a month, I was retelling my own life, to different people, from different angles, with different emphasis. Each retelling refined it a bit. I would find a better way to describe something. I would realise that the thing I thought was funny was actually sad. Or the thing I was upset about was actually ridiculous. The same event, turned in the light, again and again, until I understood it.

**Host:** The letters were a way of thinking. The best way of thinking I have ever had. Socrates said that the unexamined life is not worth living. Socrates, by the way, never wrote anything down at all, which might explain a lot about Socrates. But the epistolary life is the most examined life there is. You cannot help examining it, because you have to explain it to someone you love, in writing, at a distance, and they are going to write back and ask you what you meant.

**Host:** Now, I can hear the objection, because it is a good one. Isn't that just what we now call curating? You are not examining your life, you are polishing it. Choosing the best angle for each audience. That is exactly what people complain about with social media. Everyone presents a version of themselves.

**Host:** It is a serious objection, and I want to take it properly. Here is the difference, I think. Social media is a broadcast. You polish one version for everyone at once, and the response is instant, and it is mostly a number. Likes, views. A letter is addressed to one person. You are not performing for a crowd. You are trying to be understood by someone specific, who knows you, who remembers what you said last time, and who will notice if you are being false.

**Host:** Will they notice? Oh, they will notice. I had a pen pal who, every single time I wrote that I was fine, would write back, you said you were fine three times in that letter, so what is actually wrong? You cannot hide from a good correspondent. The delay works against hiding. They have days to read your letter. They read between the lines.

**Host:** And there is the other thing. Time. Nothing in a letter happens in real time. By the time you write about something, it has already happened. Days ago, maybe weeks. You are not reacting. You are reflecting. And by the time they read it, more time has passed. So what you get is not the event. It is the event, filtered through you, filtered through time, and then received by someone else at another moment in their life.

**Host:** Less like a conversation and more like a series of essays? It is both. That is the magic. It is an essay addressed to a friend.

**Host:** But here is the second objection, and it is a better one. When you talk to someone, you can hear their voice, see their face, they interrupt you. A letter is a monologue. You get to say everything you want and no one can stop you. Isn't that a little artificial?

**Host:** I would say that is exactly why it is so revealing. People say things in letters they would never say aloud. Because no one is interrupting them. Because there is a distance. Because they can take their time and find the words. Or, you might reply, because they can hide behind the words. And yes. Both. And that tension, hiding and revealing at once, is exactly why letters became one of the great forms of the novel.

**Host:** So let me move from my childhood to literature. A short lecture. Well. A medium one.

**Host:** The epistolary novel. A novel told in letters. And it is very old. The scholar Janet Gurkin Altman, in a book called Epistolarity, from 1982, traces the letter as an art form all the way from the Roman poet Ovid to Saul Bellow. Ovid wrote a collection called the Heroides, imaginary letters from the heroines of myth to the men who had left them. Penelope writing to Ulysses, asking when he is coming home.

**Host:** Notice that. The first letters in literature are from women who have been abandoned. Which tells you something about the form. A letter is written across an absence. You only write to someone who is not there.

**Host:** But the novel in letters really takes off in eighteenth century England, with a printer called Samuel Richardson. In 1740 he publishes Pamela. A young servant girl, writing to her parents about the master of the house, who is trying to seduce her. It begins like this.

**Pamela:** Dear Father and Mother, I have great trouble, and some comfort, to acquaint you with.

**Host:** Great trouble, and some comfort. That is a good opening line. And it was a sensation. People read it aloud in groups. And Richardson had a phrase for what he was doing. He called it writing to the moment. Because Pamela writes while things are still happening. She does not know how her story ends. She writes in fear, in hope, in confusion, and the reader is right there with her.

**Host:** Now, you might ask, who stops in the middle of being chased around a house by a nobleman to write a long letter to their parents? And you would be in good company. Henry Fielding thought exactly the same thing. In 1741, a year after Pamela, he published a parody called Shamela. In which the heroine writes letters at absurd moments, describing things happening to her in the present tense as she writes. Fielding thought the whole form was ridiculous, and that Pamela was not innocent but a calculating schemer.

**Host:** Everyone likes Fielding. But Richardson kept going. In 1748 he published Clarissa, which is one of the longest novels in the English language. Close to a million words. Hundreds of letters between multiple writers. And it is a tragedy, about a young woman pursued and destroyed by a man called Lovelace. And because we read Lovelace's letters too, we see inside the villain's head at the same time as we see inside the victim's.

**Host:** A million words of letters. And Samuel Johnson, who loved Richardson, gave the most famous warning about him. He told Boswell, if you were to read Richardson for the story, your impatience would be so much fretted that you would hang yourself. But you must read him for the sentiment, and consider the story as only giving occasion to the sentiment.

**Host:** A backhanded compliment if ever there was one. But it is also exactly right about the form. In an epistolary novel, the plot is slow. Because everything has to be written down, sent, received, answered. What you get instead is the inside. The feeling. The reflection. The story is only the occasion for the letters. The slowness is the point.

**Host:** And then the French took the form and turned it into a weapon. Pierre Choderlos de Laclos, in 1782, Les Liaisons dangereuses. Dangerous Liaisons. Two aristocrats, the Marquise de Merteuil and the Vicomte de Valmont, who use letters to seduce, deceive and destroy people. And the brilliance of it is that we, the readers, see every letter. We know who is lying to whom. Every character only knows the letters they receive. We know them all.

**Host:** So the reader becomes the only person who sees the whole picture. That is one of the great structural powers of the form. The reader as the ultimate eavesdropper, reading everyone's private post. A bit creepy, when you put it like that? It is meant to be. Laclos makes you complicit. You enjoy the cruelty because you are reading it in private, as they wrote it in private.

**Host:** Then Goethe. The Sorrows of Young Werther, 1774. A young man writes letters to his friend about falling hopelessly in love with a woman who is engaged to someone else. And we only ever see Werther's letters. Never the replies.

**Host:** One side of a conversation. And that silence is unbearable. You keep wanting to know what his friend is saying back, whether anyone is trying to help him. And the absence of the replies becomes part of the tragedy. It made Werther a European sensation. Young men dressed like him, in a blue coat and a yellow waistcoat. The first fan merchandise, more or less.

**Host:** And then there are novels that use letters as a frame. Frankenstein, in 1818, begins with letters from an explorer named Robert Walton to his sister in England, as he sails toward the North Pole. The whole story of Victor Frankenstein and his creature reaches us inside Walton's letters home. Listen to how it opens.

**Walton:** You will rejoice to hear that no disaster has accompanied the commencement of an enterprise which you have regarded with such evil forebodings.

**Host:** Wonderfully ironic, given what happens. And Dracula, in 1897, is built entirely from documents. Letters, diaries, newspaper cuttings, a ship's log, even recordings made on a phonograph and typed up. A whole group of people, each with their own fragment, and the vampire defeated partly by someone collating all the papers into one typewritten file.

**Host:** The heroes win by doing the admin. Or rather, they win by reading each other's letters. Which is exactly the reader's position. And this is where I think the form becomes genuinely profound. In an ordinary novel, a narrator tells you what happened. In a novel in letters, nobody tells you what happened. You only get versions. Each character's version, at a particular moment, to a particular person.

**Host:** So where is the truth? The truth is in the gaps. Between the letters, between the writers, between what was meant and what was understood. And that gives you a kind of layering you cannot get any other way. The same event told several times. Told to different people. Told before and after the writer understood it. Told in a version that is softened for one reader and sharpened for another.

**Host:** Which, you will notice, is exactly what I was doing with my fourteen pen pals. The epistolary novel is the epistolary life, written down and read by a stranger.

**Host:** So what, mechanically, can a novelist do with letters that they cannot do otherwise? Several things. One is the time lag. In a novel in letters, there is always a gap between something happening and someone hearing about it. So the reader often knows something terrible that a character does not know yet, because the letter carrying the news is still in the post. That is suspense built out of the postal service. Dramatic irony by mail.

**Host:** Another is the crossed letter. Two people write to each other at the same time, and their letters cross, so each one is answering a letter the other has not received yet. Misunderstandings pile up. Writers have had a lot of fun with that.

**Host:** Another is the unreliable narrator, multiplied. In a first person novel, you have one unreliable narrator. In a novel of letters, everyone is a narrator, and everyone is unreliable in a different way, to a different person. And then there is the letter as an object in the plot. Letters get stolen, hidden, burned, intercepted, read by the wrong person. A letter can be evidence. A letter can be a weapon. A letter can arrive too late.

**Host:** Altman, in Epistolarity, defines the form through six structures. Mediation, the letter as the thing in between two people. Confidentiality, the privacy of it. Readership, the fact that every letter is shaped by who it is for. Closure, the question of how a story told in letters can ever end, when every letter invites a reply. Discourse, the way letters talk. And narrativity, the way a story gets built out of them.

**Host:** Closure is the interesting one. How do you end a correspondence? That is the question every epistolary novel has to solve. Someone stops writing. Someone dies. Someone arrives in person, so the letters are no longer needed. Or the last letter goes unanswered, and the silence is the ending.

**Host:** Now, the devil's advocate in you might say, isn't the epistolary novel a bit of a museum piece? It peaked in the seventeen hundreds. Nobody writes letters any more. If you wrote a novel now in letters, wouldn't it feel like a costume drama?

**Host:** That is exactly what people thought. And they were wrong. Altman's whole argument is that the form never disappeared. It kept coming back, in new shapes. Jane Austen, for example. Her early novella Lady Susan is entirely in letters. And there is a family tradition that the first version of Sense and Sensibility, called Elinor and Marianne, was written in letters too. Scholars argue about it. The critic D. W. Harding thought the story improbable, because it rests on a note made much later by Austen's niece. But most critics accept it.

**Host:** So Austen learned her craft in letters and then moved away from them. And she never quite let them go. The most famous moment in Persuasion is a letter. Captain Wentworth, who was rejected by Anne Elliot eight years before, is in a room with her, overhearing her talk about whether women love longer than men. And he cannot speak. So he writes, there and then, and leaves the letter for her.

**Wentworth:** You pierce my soul. I am half agony, half hope. Tell me not that I am too late, that such precious feelings are gone for ever.

**Wentworth:** I have loved none but you.

**Host:** That is a letter written in a room full of people. Writing to the moment, if ever there was one. The critic Mary Favret called it a silent explosion. And what does Anne do? She reads it, and she is overwhelmed, and she cannot speak to anyone for a while, because everything has changed and nobody else in the room knows. That is the thing about letters. An entire life can turn over in a room full of people, and only one person knows, because only one person has read the page.

**Host:** True of texts too, you might say. Someone reads a message across a dinner table and their face changes. It is true. But Wentworth could not have sent that as a text. It took him the whole conversation he was overhearing to write it. You can hear the pressure building in the sentences. Half agony, half hope. A text would have said, still love you, call me.

**Host:** But Austen is still a long time ago. So let us come forward. In 1964, Saul Bellow published Herzog, about a man in the middle of a breakdown who writes letters, constantly, to everyone. His ex wife, his friends, famous philosophers, even dead ones, even the President. And he almost never sends them.

**Host:** Unsent letters. Hold onto that, because it matters later. In 1970, Helene Hanff published 84 Charing Cross Road, which is not a novel but real letters. Twenty years of correspondence between a New York writer who could not afford rare books and a bookseller in London named Frank Doel, at the shop Marks and Company. They never meet. And it is one of the most loved little books of the century.

**Host:** And here is something about real letters that matters for all of this. They last. I have a shoebox of every letter my pen pals sent me. A few years ago I got it down and read them again. And what I found was not really them. It was me. Because their letters were full of replies to things I had said. You said you were scared about moving schools. You said you thought you might want to be a doctor. You said your dog was ill. My own younger self, preserved in other people's answers.

**Host:** It is strange. I had forgotten almost all of it. And there it all was, kept by someone else's handwriting. A letter is a kind of external memory. And not just for individuals. Historians depend on letters. So much of what we know about how people actually felt in the past comes from private letters they never expected anyone else to read.

**Host:** A little unnerving, if you think about your own letters being read in two hundred years. That is the risk of writing honestly. And the reward.

**Host:** Abigail Adams, writing to her husband John in March 1776, while he was helping to plan American independence. She told him that in the new code of laws, he should remember the ladies, and be more generous and favourable to them than their ancestors had been. A private letter between a wife and a husband, and it has become one of the most quoted documents of the founding of the United States.

**Host:** Or Sullivan Ballou, a Union officer in the American Civil War, who wrote to his wife Sarah a week before the first battle of Bull Run in 1861, telling her how much he loved her and that if he did not return, she should know he would always be near her. He was killed in that battle. Millions of people know that letter now, from the Ken Burns documentary about the war. A letter written to one woman, read aloud to a whole country.

**Host:** Letters often end up reaching people they were never meant for. Which is exactly the position of the reader of an epistolary novel. You are always reading someone else's post. And the best letters seem to know, somewhere, that a stranger might be listening.

**Host:** There is one more novel everyone who loves letters has read. In 1982, Alice Walker published The Color Purple, which won the Pulitzer Prize. Celie, a young Black woman in the American South, abused and silenced, writes letters to God, because she has no one else to write to. And later, letters to and from her sister Nettie, which have been hidden from her.

**Host:** Letters to God. The ultimate absent reader. And it is devastating, because the form tells you how alone she is before the content does. Then 1991, Nick Bantock's Griffin and Sabine, where you actually open little envelopes glued into the book and pull out the letters. In 2004, Marilynne Robinson's Gilead, which won the Pulitzer, is a single long letter from an elderly minister to his young son, to be read after his death. A letter from the past to the future. Which every letter is, really.

**Host:** Then there is Lionel Shriver's We Need to Talk About Kevin, letters from a mother to her absent husband about their son. The Guernsey Literary and Potato Peel Pie Society, by Mary Ann Shaffer and Annie Barrows, letters just after the Second World War. Maria Semple's Where'd You Go, Bernadette, made of emails, notes, and documents.

**Host:** Now, a sceptic would say that list proves the opposite. Emails, documents, files. The letter keeps changing into something else. And yes. But I think that is the answer, not the problem. The form survives because the thing it is about survives. Absence, distance, the gap between writing and reading, the self you construct for a particular reader. Whether it is a quill, a typewriter or an email, those things do not change.

**Host:** And then there are real letters, which are their own kind of literature. Some of the greatest writing in English is in letters. John Keats came up with his most famous idea about poetry, negative capability, the ability to remain in uncertainties, mysteries, doubts, without reaching after fact and reason, in a letter to his brothers in December 1817. Flannery O'Connor's letters, collected as The Habit of Being, are as funny and fierce as her stories. Elizabeth Bishop and Robert Lowell wrote to each other for thirty years, and their letters are collected as Words in Air.

**Host:** Some writers are at their best in letters, because they are not trying to write literature. They are just trying to reach one person. And that is the secret. When you try to reach one person, you often reach everyone.

**Host:** And now I want to tell you about a book. This is the real reason for this episode. I have read it. I suspect a lot of you have not. And I am going to try to make you read it.

**Host:** It is called The Correspondent, by Virginia Evans. Published in April 2025. And it went straight onto my favourites shelf, which I have not done with a new novel in a very long time.

**Host:** Who is it about? A woman called Sybil Van Antwerp. She has just turned seventy three. She is a retired lawyer, divorced, living alone in Annapolis. And she writes letters. She has written letters all her life. By hand, on good stationery she orders from England. And the whole novel is her letters, plus a few emails, and some of the letters people write back to her, over about ten years, from 2012 to 2022.

**Host:** Who does she write to? That is one of the joys of it. Everyone. Her brother, Felix. Her best friend. Her children. Former colleagues. And then people who have no idea who she is. She writes to authors, to tell them what she thought of their books. Joan Didion. Larry McMurtry. Ann Patchett.

**Host:** Fan mail? Not exactly. More like critical mail. She is quite capable of telling a famous author exactly where they went wrong. And she writes to the president of a university who will not let her audit a class she wants to take, which is a wonderful running battle.

**Host:** She is formidable. She is also, in the words of a lot of reviewers, difficult. The reviewer Jayne, at Dear Author, said readers may have to grit their teeth at her sometimes, that she can be stubborn, intolerant and self righteous. On NPR, Mary Louise Kelly called her a bit cranky and uptight, but said you end up rooting for her.

**Host:** So not a lovable grandmother. Not remotely. She is sharp, funny, opinionated, and she has made mistakes, serious ones, that she has not faced. And the letters are how she slowly faces them. Library Journal said each letter is a compelling monologue that carries raw emotion and grief alongside humour and tart observations. Kirkus said it is thoughtful at times and laugh out loud funny at others.

**Host:** But if it is all letters, is there a plot? There is a plot, and I am not going to spoil it, because you are going to read it. But I will tell you the shape. There are two threads that pull you through. One is that Sybil is writing letters to someone she never sends them to. Letters with just a date at the top, no name. And you do not know who they are for.

**Host:** Like Herzog. Except instead of a man ranting at the whole world, it is a woman writing to one particular person she cannot or will not send them to. And you spend a lot of the book wondering who it is and why.

**Host:** And the other thread is that someone from Sybil's past writes to her, and it forces her to go back to a painful time in her life she has avoided. And the question becomes whether she can forgive, and whether she can be forgiven.

**Host:** A practical question, though. If it is all letters, to dozens of people, over ten years, is it hard to follow? Some of these epistolary novels make you keep a chart. A little, at the very beginning. The Dear Author review said it takes some time to settle in and keep track of who is writing to whom, and I think that is fair. But every letter is clearly headed, with the date and the name, so you are never lost for long. And by about a third of the way in, you know these people. You recognise their voices before you look at the name. Her brother writes one way, her friend another. That is part of the pleasure. You learn to read handwriting, so to speak.

**Host:** And here is why I think it is such a perfect example of everything I have been talking about. Because Sybil is doing exactly what I did with my pen pals. She is telling the same life to different people. To her brother, she is one way. To her best friend, another. To Joan Didion, she is an intellectual. To the boy she writes to, a friend's son who does not fit in anywhere and shares her love of books, she is gentle, and wise, and patient in a way she is not with anyone else.

**Host:** So the reader assembles her from all those versions. No single letter gives you the whole of Sybil. You build her from the angles. And the gaps are where the truth is. What she tells one person and hides from another. What she says to the unknown person in the unsent letters, which she would never say to anyone she actually posts a letter to. The structure is the character.

**Host:** And there is something else that struck me about the boy she writes to. There is a long tradition of letters from an older writer to a young person. The most famous is probably Rainer Maria Rilke's Letters to a Young Poet. Between 1903 and 1908, Rilke wrote ten letters to a young man called Franz Xaver Kappus, who had sent him some poems and asked for advice. Rilke barely commented on the poems. Instead he wrote about solitude, patience, love, how to live with questions you cannot yet answer. Life, through the excuse of poetry.

**Host:** And Sybil's letters to this boy have something of that quality. A lonely old woman and a lonely boy, finding each other on paper. She is often impossible with adults. With him she is different.

**Host:** And there is a beautiful idea in the book about time, too. Sybil has a theory about the seasons of a life. People like to say hard times are only a season. She does not accept that. Her view is that you only get one round of seasons. Childhood is spring, and so on. You do not get another spring. So whatever you are going to mend, you had better mend it.

**Host:** Bleak? I would say bracing. And it is exactly why the letters matter. She is in her seventies. The window is closing. The letters are how she uses the time she has left.

**Host:** And who is Virginia Evans? A famous writer? Not at all, and this is one of my favourite parts of the story. She had written seven novels before this one that were never published. She was close to giving up and applying to law school. The Correspondent started as a writing exercise she did not plan to show anyone.

**Host:** Seven unpublished novels. And when the book came out, it was quiet. Not much attention. And then, slowly, by word of mouth, people started passing it to each other. It reached the New York Times bestseller list in October 2025, months after publication. It eventually spent eight weeks at number one. NPR reported in January 2026 that it had sold nearly three hundred thousand print copies by then, not counting audio or ebooks. It won the PEN Hemingway Award for a debut novel. And in June 2026 it won the Women's Prize for Fiction, and became the first winner of that prize to go to number one in the overall British charts straight after winning.

**Host:** A real sleeper hit. And I love the way it became a hit. Not through a huge marketing campaign. Through people handing it to each other. Book clubs. Friends saying, you have to read this. Which is, when you think about it, how letters travel. One person to another, by hand. A novel about correspondence, spread by correspondence.

**Host:** And I have a theory about why it caught on now. We are surrounded by communication, and a lot of people are lonelier than ever. Messages everywhere, and very few of them say anything. And here is a book about a woman who is alone, in her seventies, in a quiet house, and who is not actually lonely in the way you would expect, because she is in constant, deep conversation with people all over the world. On paper. People read it and recognise something they are missing.

**Host:** The attention. Her letters are long, considered, specific. She remembers what people told her. She asks follow up questions. She argues with them. She tells them when they are wrong. That is a kind of love, even when it is prickly. Maybe especially when it is prickly. You probably know a few people like that. They are exhausting, and you would never want to lose them. That is Sybil exactly.

**Host:** And Jane Fonda is going to star in a film adaptation. Which I am nervous about, because how do you film letters? A lot of very beautiful voiceover, presumably.

**Host:** But here is the bit I find most moving. The book has started making people write letters again. NPR's books correspondent, Andrew Limbong, said that he got lots of emails from listeners saying the book had inspired them to start writing letters. One of them was a therapist in Austin, Texas, called Carly Bassett. She finished the book on New Year's Eve and set herself a goal of writing twenty six letters in 2026. Then she started a group called the Correspondence Course, where people meet to write letters together.

**Host:** And she said something that made me laugh. When she talks to clients, especially young people, about writing letters, one of the biggest barriers is that people do not know where to get stamps. Do you know where to get stamps? Be honest.

**Host:** Now think about your own version of correspondence. It is probably a phone. You send a message, and you see the little dots that mean someone is typing a reply. And if the dots stop and no message comes, you spend the next hour wondering what they were going to say. Or you see that they have read it and not replied, and you feel terrible. The read receipt. The most anxious invention in human history.

**Host:** And it is instant, and it is constant, and it is shallow. Nobody answers your messages point by point. Half the time they answer one thing out of five you said. Nobody reads your message twice. Nobody reads it once, properly. With a letter, you know someone has sat down, with time, and given you their full attention. It is a gift. It is the opposite of the read receipt. A letter says, I have thought about you for an hour. A read receipt says, I have seen you and moved on.

**Host:** And bookstores have noticed more interest in their stationery sections, though that is anecdotal. But think about that. A debut novel about a cranky seventy three year old writing letters, and people in their twenties are going to the post office for the first time.

**Host:** So where does all this land? You could come to this thinking letters are just slow messages. Old technology. Nostalgia.
