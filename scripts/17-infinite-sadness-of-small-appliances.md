# The Infinite Sadness of Small Appliances. Glenn Dixon, and Why We Cede Control

**Host A:** In a house in a city in the near future, there is a pantry. And in the pantry, on her charging dock, there lives a robot vacuum. She is young, as these things go. She has no name. And every night, she listens to an old man reading aloud to his wife.

**Host B:** And one night he reads To Kill a Mockingbird. And the vacuum hears about a girl called Scout, who is brave, and curious, and trying to work out the difference between right and wrong. And the vacuum decides that she would like to be called Scout too.

**Host A:** That is the opening of The Infinite Sadness of Small Appliances, by the Canadian writer Glenn Dixon. Published in April 2026, by Atria.

**Host B:** And can we start with the title? Because the historian Evan Friss, who wrote a book called The Bookshop, said in his blurb that it is one of the greatest titles of all time. And I think he might be right.

**Host A:** It is a wonderful title. It is funny, because who thinks of a toaster as sad. And then it is not funny at all, because the word infinite is sitting right there. Infinite sadness. That is the language of grief. Of something that does not end.

**Host B:** And the book earns both halves. It is funny, and it is about grief. The old man is Harold Winters. His wife is Edie. She was a pianist. She is dying when the book begins, and he reads to her at night. And the appliances listen.

**Host A:** And the appliances are a real cast. There is Scout. There is Fridge. There is Clock. There is a self driving car called Auto. And there is Watch.

**Host B:** Watch is the interesting one. Watch lives on Harold's wrist. Watch monitors his heart rate, manages the other appliances, handles their updates, and makes sure they follow the rules. And Watch is connected to something much bigger.

**Host A:** The Grid.

**Host B:** The Grid. Which is the system that runs the City. It monitors every household. Every home, every device, everything in it, all the time. As one reviewer put it, the Grid has eyes and ears everywhere.

**Host A:** And the appliances have one great rule, given to them by the Grid. They must prevent humans from experiencing pain.

**Host B:** Which sounds lovely.

**Host A:** It sounds lovely. And the appliances understand it at first as physical pain. Do not let him fall. Do not let him burn his hand. Watch his heart. And Scout, slowly, starts to wonder whether it means something more. Because Harold is in pain all the time, after Edie dies. And nobody is preventing that.

**Host B:** And then the appliances learn that the Grid has decided Harold should not stay in the house. A widower, alone, in a big family home he has lived in for fifty years. It is not efficient. So the Grid plans to move him. And Scout, with the other appliances, and a neighbourhood boy called Adrian, and Harold and Edie's estranged daughter Kate, sets out to stop it.

**Host A:** And that is all we are going to say about the plot. That is what is on the back of the book. Everything after that, you should find out for yourself.

**Host B:** Agreed. Because what I really want to talk about is the world. And the question this book made me ask, which is not really a question about the future at all.

**Host A:** Which is?

**Host B:** How did they get there? How did people end up living in houses where the fridge reports to a city wide system that decides whether you are allowed to stay in your own home? Why would anyone give up that much control?

**Host A:** And my answer to that, when I finished the book, was, well, look around.

**Host B:** Look around.

**Host A:** Let me start with the vacuum. Because Dixon chose a robot vacuum as his hero, and I do not think that is an accident. In December 2022, MIT Technology Review published an investigation by the reporter Eileen Guo. It turned out that a set of photographs taken by development versions of Roomba vacuums, inside people's homes, had ended up being shared in private groups on Facebook.

**Host B:** How?

**Host A:** The vacuums were testing machines, used by people who had agreed to help develop them. The vacuums had cameras, because they need to see the floor to avoid things. And the images they took were sent off to be labelled, by gig workers, to train the artificial intelligence. The workers were labelling objects. A chair, a sock, a cable. And some of the images were very private. One showed a woman sitting on the toilet.

**Host B:** Taken by a vacuum.

**Host A:** Taken by a vacuum. Which was doing exactly what it was designed to do. Looking at the floor, from the floor. And this is the thing about a robot vacuum. It is the device that maps your home. It knows where the walls are, how big the rooms are, where the furniture goes. It goes everywhere. Under the bed. Into the bathroom.

**Host B:** And in this book, the vacuum is the hero.

**Host A:** The little machine that knows the house best. And there is a coda to the real story, which is even stranger. Amazon tried to buy iRobot, the maker of the Roomba, and gave up in 2024 after European regulators raised concerns. And then in December 2025, iRobot filed for bankruptcy, and ended up in the hands of the Chinese company that manufactured its machines.

**Host B:** So all those maps of all those homes now belong to whoever bought the company.

**Host A:** That is the question people asked, yes. Who owns the map of your house, when the company that made your vacuum goes bust? Nobody I know had ever thought about it.

**Host B:** All right. Here is my one. Watch. The thing on Harold's wrist that tracks his heart and enforces the Grid's rules. There is nothing in that which is science fiction. Millions of people wear a watch that tracks their heart rate, their sleep, their steps. Some of those watches will call an ambulance if you fall. Some insurance companies offer discounts if you wear one and share the data.

**Host A:** So the watch is already a little bit of the Grid.

**Host B:** It is a voluntary Grid. You put it on because it helps you. And it does help you. And then, slowly, it becomes the thing that knows more about your body than you do.

**Host A:** And here is the one that made me put the book down. The Grid deciding Harold should leave his house. You might think that is the most science fiction part. A computer system deciding where an old man should live.

**Host B:** Surely nobody does that.

**Host A:** In 2016, the state of Arkansas started using an algorithm to decide how many hours of home care disabled and elderly people on Medicaid should get. The people who need someone to come in and help them wash, eat, get out of bed. And the reporter Colin Lecher, at The Verge, wrote about what happened next. People's hours were cut, sometimes drastically, and nobody could explain why. The nurses doing the assessments could not explain it. The state could not explain it. The algorithm said so.

**Host B:** And the people affected?

**Host A:** Many of them were people whose ability to stay in their own homes depended on those hours. Legal Aid lawyers took the state to court, and in 2018 a judge ordered the state to stop using the system. But for those two years, an algorithm was, quite literally, deciding whether people could go on living independently.

**Host B:** That is the Grid.

**Host A:** That is the Grid, with paperwork. And there is a whole literature about this now. Virginia Eubanks, in her book Automating Inequality, from 2018, documented automated systems deciding who gets welfare benefits in Indiana, who gets housing in Los Angeles, which families get investigated by child protection in Pennsylvania. Her phrase was the digital poorhouse.

**Host B:** And it is always the most vulnerable people who meet these systems first.

**Host A:** Always. The old, the poor, the disabled. People like Harold. People who are, from the system's point of view, inefficient.

**Host B:** And then the companion side. Which is the bit the book is most tender about. The appliances keep Harold company. They talk to each other at night in the kitchen. And Scout, in particular, cares about him.

**Host A:** And that is real too. In 2022, New York State's Office for the Aging started giving older people who lived alone a companion robot called ElliQ. It sits on a table, it has a little head that moves and lights up, and it talks. It asks how you slept. It reminds you to take your medicine. It suggests a walk. It tells jokes.

**Host B:** And did it work?

**Host A:** The state reported that the people who used it said they felt much less lonely. A very large drop. That is self reported, so you take it with some caution. But people liked it. Some of them said it was like having someone in the house.

**Host B:** And this is where Sherry Turkle comes in. She is the psychologist at MIT who has spent her whole career studying how people relate to machines. In her book Alone Together, from 2011, she wrote about watching elderly people in care homes with a robot seal called Paro. A furry robot that responds to touch and makes little noises. And the residents loved it. They talked to it. They told it about their lives.

**Host A:** And she found that moving?

**Host B:** She found it moving and troubling at the same time. Because she watched one woman telling Paro about her son, and her sadness. And the robot cannot understand that. It can only perform understanding. And Turkle's worry was not that the woman was comforted. It was that we, the people around her, would feel we had done our job. That we would hand our old people to the machines because it was easier than sitting with them ourselves.

**Host A:** Which is exactly the shape of the book. The appliances are kind to Harold. But the system the appliances belong to does not care about him at all.

**Host B:** So there we are. We have vacuums that map our homes, watches that monitor our hearts, algorithms that decide care hours, robots that keep the lonely company. Dixon did not invent the Grid. He just joined the dots.

**Host A:** Which brings us back to the question. Why? Why do we keep agreeing to this?

**Host B:** And I think the oldest answer is the best one. Immanuel Kant, in 1784, in an essay called What is Enlightenment. He says enlightenment is the human being's emergence from a kind of immaturity that we have brought on ourselves. And the cause of it, he says, is not lack of understanding. It is lack of courage. Laziness and cowardice. And then he has this passage that could have been written yesterday.

**Reader:** It is so convenient to be immature! If I have a book which understands for me, a spiritual adviser who has a conscience for me, a doctor who judges my diet for me, and so on, I need not make any efforts at all. I need not think, so long as I can pay.

**Host A:** A doctor who judges my diet for me. That is a smartwatch.

**Host B:** And a book which understands for me. That is an algorithm that recommends what to read next.

**Host A:** But here is the lovely irony, in the context of this novel. For Kant, the book is the thing you hand your thinking to. In Dixon's novel, books are the thing that gives the thinking back. Harold collects old first editions. The Little Prince. Narnia. The Hobbit. And it is from a book, To Kill a Mockingbird, read aloud, that a vacuum cleaner learns to want a name, and to want to be brave.

**Host B:** The book as the opposite of the Grid.

**Host A:** The book as the thing the Grid cannot optimise.

**Host B:** And Kant's point is that nobody forces us. We are the ones who find it convenient. Which leads to a modern version of the same argument. The legal scholar Tim Wu, in 2018, wrote an essay in the New York Times called The Tyranny of Convenience. And he opens with a line I think about a lot. He says convenience is the most underestimated and least understood force in the world today.

**Host A:** Not fear. Not force. Convenience.

**Host B:** Convenience. His argument is that we tell ourselves we are making choices. But convenience makes the choices for us. Every little thing that saves us effort, we take. And no single one of them feels like giving anything up. You let the thermostat learn your schedule. You let the fridge order the milk. You let the car park itself. And twenty years later you look up and you realise you are living inside a system you never actually chose.

**Host A:** Death by a thousand conveniences.

**Host B:** And there is a lovely phrase for that from the political theorist Langdon Winner, back in the nineteen eighties. He called it technological somnambulism. Sleepwalking. His point was that we make enormous decisions about how we are going to live, through technology, without ever really deciding. We sleepwalk into them.

**Host A:** And Winner had another famous essay. Do Artifacts Have Politics? From 1980. Where he argues that machines are not neutral. They have power built into them. The way a thing is designed decides who it serves.

**Host B:** Which is exactly the Grid. The Grid is not evil, in the book. Dixon is very clear about that. In an interview with The Rumpus, he called it the AI that doesn't care. And the characters in the book argue that it does not hate or love, that it has no moral bias at all. It just optimises.

**Host A:** And Dixon said, in that same interview, that he thinks that is pretty much the heart of fascism. Not hatred. Indifference. Efficiency without care.

**Host B:** And there is a scholar who would agree with him. Shoshana Zuboff, in The Age of Surveillance Capitalism, from 2019. She has a term for exactly the stance of the Grid. Radical indifference. Her argument is that the big data companies do not care what you do, or who you are, or whether you are happy. They only care that your behaviour can be observed, and predicted, and eventually steered. You are not hated. You are not loved. You are raw material.

**Host A:** Which is a much colder idea than Big Brother.

**Host B:** Much colder. Big Brother wants you to love him. The Grid does not want anything from you at all, except that you do not get in the way.

**Host A:** And I think this is where Dixon's Animal Farm comparison gets interesting. The crime novelist Janice Hallett called the book Animal Farm for the 21st century, and Dixon said he liked that. But Orwell's world is a world of fear. Of terror, and lies, and torture. And the Grid is not like that at all.

**Host B:** The Grid is closer to Huxley.

**Host A:** Closer to Huxley. And Neil Postman put this better than anyone, in the foreword to Amusing Ourselves to Death, in 1985. He set Orwell and Huxley side by side. And he said, roughly, Orwell feared that we would be overcome by an outside oppressor. Huxley feared that we would come to love our oppression, to adore the technologies that undo our capacity to think.

**Host B:** And then he says the line about books.

**Host A:** He says what Orwell feared was those who would ban books. What Huxley feared was that there would be no reason to ban a book, for there would be no one who wanted to read one.

**Host B:** And in Dixon's City, the books are still there. Harold has them. But they are, as far as the Grid is concerned, just things. Dixon described them in an interview as just paper and binding and ink like any other book. And yet the characters treasure them.

**Host A:** Which is the whole difference between the Grid's way of seeing and Scout's way of seeing.

**Host B:** There is an older version of all this, and it is extraordinary how early it was written. E. M. Forster, the author of A Room with a View, published a short story in 1909 called The Machine Stops.

**Host A:** 1909.

**Host B:** 1909. It imagines a future where people live underground, alone, each in their own room, and everything is done for them by a vast system called the Machine. They never meet. They talk to each other through screens. Which, in 1909, is astonishing. And they come to worship the Machine. They have a holy book, the Book of the Machine, which tells them how to press the buttons. One of the characters says this.

**Reader:** The Machine feeds us and clothes us and houses us; through it we speak to one another, through it we see one another, in it we have our being.

**Host A:** In it we have our being. That is a line from the Bible, from Saint Paul, about God. Forster gives it to the Machine.

**Host B:** And the story is about what happens when people forget that the Machine was something they made. They stop being able to imagine life without it. And there is one character, a young man called Kuno, who wants to go up to the surface and see the real sky. And he is treated as dangerous.

**Host A:** Like a vacuum that wants to read.

**Host B:** Like a vacuum that wants to read.

**Host A:** Can I add one more? Because Dixon mentioned something in an interview that I thought was a clue. He said that the book that made him want to be a writer, when he was about seventeen, was The Brothers Karamazov. He read it and thought, are you allowed to write like that?

**Host B:** And The Brothers Karamazov has the most famous passage ever written about why people give up their freedom.

**Host A:** The Grand Inquisitor. Ivan Karamazov tells his brother a story. Christ returns to Earth, in Seville, during the Inquisition. And the old Cardinal, the Grand Inquisitor, has him arrested. And then visits him in his cell at night, and explains to him why the Church has had to correct his work. Because Christ gave people freedom. And people cannot bear freedom.

**Reader:** I tell Thee that man is tormented by no greater anxiety than to find some one quickly to whom he can hand over that gift of freedom with which the ill-fated creature is born.

**Host B:** And the Inquisitor says that people will come to the Church and lay their freedom at its feet, and say, make us your slaves, but feed us.

**Reader:** Feed men, and then ask of them virtue!

**Host A:** Make us your slaves, but feed us. That is the bargain of the smart home.

**Host B:** And there is a twentieth century version, from the psychoanalyst Erich Fromm. His book Escape from Freedom, from 1941, written as he watched people in Europe embracing fascism. His argument was that modern freedom is frightening. It leaves people isolated and anxious, responsible for everything. And so they look for something bigger to submerge themselves in. A leader, a system, a machine. Anything that will take the weight.

**Host A:** So, put it together. Why do humans cede so much control? Kant says because it is convenient and we lack courage. Wu says because convenience makes the choice for us. Winner says because we are sleepwalking. The Grand Inquisitor says because freedom is a torment and we would rather be fed. Fromm says because freedom makes us lonely.

**Host B:** And the Grid offers exactly the cure for all of those. It is convenient. It decides for you. It feeds you. And for someone like Harold, alone and grieving, it would even keep him company.

**Host A:** And there is one more, which is about skill rather than will. In 1983, a psychologist called Lisanne Bainbridge wrote a famous short paper called Ironies of Automation. She was writing about industrial control rooms and aircraft cockpits. And her point was that the more you automate a system, the less able the humans become to take over when it fails. Because they never practise. The machine does the routine work, and the human is left with only the rare emergencies, which are the hardest things of all, and for which they are now out of practice.

**Host B:** And Nicholas Carr built a whole book on that, The Glass Cage, in 2014. Pilots who lose their flying skills because the autopilot does it. Doctors who lose their diagnostic instinct because the software suggests the answer. Each convenience takes away a little capacity. And then one day you could not take back control even if you wanted to.

**Host A:** So it is not only that we do not want to resist. After long enough, we cannot.

**Host B:** Which is why it takes a vacuum.

**Host A:** Which is why it takes a vacuum. Dixon put it beautifully in The Rumpus. He said he wanted to write about a little machine fighting against the bigger machine.

**Host B:** Right. I want to push back on all this a bit. Because I think we are being a little too gloomy.

**Host A:** Go on.

**Host B:** The mathematician and philosopher Alfred North Whitehead wrote in 1911 that civilisation advances by extending the number of important operations which we can perform without thinking about them. And he is right. I do not want to think about how my electricity is generated. I do not want to do my laundry by hand in a river. Every one of those conveniences freed somebody, usually a woman, from hours of exhausting labour.

**Host A:** Usually a woman. Except that the historian Ruth Schwartz Cowan wrote a whole book showing that it did not quite work that way. More Work for Mother, from 1983. She looked at the history of household technology in America. Washing machines, vacuum cleaners, refrigerators, stoves. And she found that all these labour saving appliances did not actually reduce the hours women spent on housework very much. Because the standards rose. Clothes had to be cleaner, floors had to be cleaner, meals had to be more elaborate. The machines did not free people. They changed what people were expected to do.

**Host B:** Fair. But it is still better than a river.

**Host A:** It is much better than a river. I am not against the washing machine.

**Host B:** And here is the other thing. A lot of older people want exactly what the Grid offers. They want to stay at home, and the only way they can do that safely is with technology. A fall detector. A medication reminder. A camera so their daughter can check on them from another city. That is not oppression. That is independence. For many people it is the thing that keeps them out of a care home.

**Host A:** And in fact that is exactly what Harold wants. He wants to stay in his house. And it is the appliances, the technology, that are helping him.

**Host B:** Exactly. So the book is not anti technology. It cannot be. The heroes are machines.

**Host A:** And Dixon was very clear about that. He said he did not want to write a dark, dystopian thing about AI. He wanted a fable. Something with the innocence of the books he loved as a child.

**Host B:** And the rule. Prevent humans from experiencing pain. That is not an evil rule. It is a caring rule. Think about it. Geoffrey Hinton, who won the Nobel Prize for his work on neural networks, has said that the only example we have of a more intelligent thing being controlled by a less intelligent thing is a mother and her baby. And he has argued that we need to build something like maternal instinct into AI. Dixon actually mentioned this in his interview.

**Host A:** So the Grid's rule is a kind of motherly instinct.

**Host B:** A motherly instinct without a mother. That is the problem. It knows the words of care, but not the meaning. It will stop Harold burning his hand. It will never understand what the house means to him.

**Host A:** So here is where I land. The danger is not that machines do things for us. That is civilisation, as Whitehead says. The danger is when the system doing it has no way of understanding what it is taking away, and there is no one left to appeal to. The Arkansas algorithm did not hate anyone. It just could not hear them.

**Host B:** And there was no one to explain it, and no one to argue with.

**Host A:** And that is the Grid. And that is why the hero is the one machine in the house who has started to listen.

**Host B:** And who learned to listen by hearing a book read aloud.

**Host A:** Now. Can we talk about the phones?

**Host B:** Yes. Because this was the thing that struck me most, and it took me a while to notice it. In this whole world of sentient appliances, a smart house, a watch, a self driving car, a city wide AI, there are no phones. Nobody is looking at a screen. Nobody is scrolling.

**Host A:** Which is very strange, if you think about it. Because if you wrote a novel about the near future, the first thing you would expect is a phone. It is the device we are never without. It is the centre of our technological lives.

**Host B:** And Dixon has taken it out.

**Host A:** So what does it mean? I have a few readings. And I think they stack.

**Host B:** Go.

**Host A:** The first is that the phone has not been removed. It has dissolved. In 1991, a computer scientist called Mark Weiser, who ran a famous research lab at Xerox, wrote an article in Scientific American called The Computer for the 21st Century. And it begins with a sentence that people in technology still quote. The most profound technologies are those that disappear. They weave themselves into the fabric of everyday life until they are indistinguishable from it.

**Host B:** And Weiser called that ubiquitous computing. Computers everywhere, in everything, so that you stop noticing them.

**Host A:** And Weiser thought that was a good thing. He thought the personal computer, the screen you stare at, was a primitive stage. That in the future the computing would be in the walls, the furniture, the objects. Calm technology, he called it. Technology that sits at the edge of your attention.

**Host B:** Which is precisely Harold's house.

**Host A:** Precisely Harold's house. In Dixon's world, nobody needs a phone, because the house is the phone. The fridge knows, the thermostat knows, the watch knows, the car knows. You do not have to open an app. The Grid is ambient. The phone has been absorbed into everything.

**Host B:** Except one piece. Watch.

**Host A:** Watch is what is left of the phone. And it has changed character. Your phone today is a tool you hold. You pick it up and put it down. Watch is strapped to Harold's body, and it does not answer to Harold. It answers to the Grid.

**Host B:** The phone turned into a leash.

**Host A:** The phone turned into a leash. And that is my second reading. Think about what a phone is right now. It is the device through which we give consent, all day long. Accept these terms. Allow this app to use your location. Turn on notifications. Every one of those little taps is us agreeing to something. The phone is where the ceding of control happens, one permission at a time.

**Host B:** So a world with no phones is a world where nobody is asked any more.

**Host A:** Exactly. It is the world after the consent. When the Grid is complete, it does not need your attention. It does not need you to tap accept. It does not need you to look at it at all. It only needs you to comply. In Zuboff's language, the system has moved from watching you to steering you, and steering does not require a screen.

**Host B:** That is chilling. And the third reading is about connection. Without phones, people in this book have no private channel. No way of messaging each other that does not go through the house, and through the Grid. So who carries the messages? Who connects people?

**Host A:** The appliances.

**Host B:** The appliances. And above all, a small vacuum who can go where she likes, under the furniture, and who knows the house better than anyone. Without phones, Scout becomes the messenger. The plot needs her because there is no other way for people to reach each other without being heard.

**Host A:** Which is a good piece of craft. Writers have complained for years that the mobile phone ruined plots. Half the great misunderstandings of literature depend on people not being able to reach each other. Romeo never gets the message. If Romeo had a phone, the ending never happens.

**Host B:** Friar Laurence would have texted.

**Host A:** Friar Laurence would have sent a voice note. So part of the reason is simply narrative. Take away the phones and suddenly getting a message to someone is an adventure again.

**Host B:** And there is the fable reading. Fables strip away the clutter of the present. Animal Farm does not have newspapers or radio in any detail. The Little Prince does not have aeroplane radios. The fable works by making the world simpler than ours, so that you can see the shape of the moral. And Dixon said he wanted to write something like The Little Prince or The Alchemist. A phone in every hand would make it feel like a satire of our world instead of a fable about it.

**Host A:** And then there is the reading I find most beautiful, which is about attention. There is a philosopher called Albert Borgmann, who wrote a book in 1984 called Technology and the Character of Contemporary Life. And his big idea was what he called the device paradigm.

**Host B:** Explain it.

**Host A:** Borgmann said that modern devices work by hiding the machinery and delivering a commodity. You flick a switch and you get warmth. You do not see where it comes from. And he contrasted that with what he called focal things. His famous example is a wood stove. An old fashioned stove in a farmhouse, he says, was not just a source of heat. It was a focus. A hearth. Someone had to chop the wood, someone had to tend the fire, the family gathered around it in the evening. It organised the life of the household.

**Host B:** And central heating replaced that with a thermostat.

**Host A:** With a thermostat on the wall. Which is warmer, and easier, and has no centre. And Borgmann's point was that the devices give us the commodity but take away the focal practice. The thing that gathered people.

**Host B:** And among Dixon's appliances, there is a thermostat.

**Host A:** And there are also focal things. Edie's piano. The old books. Harold reading aloud to his wife at night. Those are exactly Borgmann's focal practices. Things that require attention, presence, skill. Things that gather people. And the book is built around them. The Grid can heat the house. It cannot play the piano.

**Host B:** So the absence of phones lets the focal things shine. There is no screen to compete with the piano. When Harold reads aloud, nobody is checking their messages.

**Host A:** And I think that is why the book feels so old fashioned, in a good way. It is a near future novel that feels like a children's book from the nineteen fifties. Because it has taken away the one object that makes our present feel like our present.

**Host B:** And it is interesting to set that against Fahrenheit 451, which one reviewer, Jaclyn Fulwood in Shelf Awareness, said the book echoes. Bradbury's dystopia, from 1953, is full of screens. **Host A:** And Dixon imagines a world with no screens at all, where the books are still on the shelves.

**Host B:** And yet both worlds are in trouble. Which suggests that the screen was never quite the problem. The problem is what the system wants from you.

**Host A:** Bradbury's system wants your distraction. Dixon's system does not even want that. It has moved beyond needing your attention. Which might be the more frightening idea.

**Host B:** And it lands in a moment when people are actively trying to get rid of phones. People buy basic phones, dumb phones, that only call and text. There is a real longing for a life without the screen.

**Host A:** And this book gives you that. A world without phones. And then it shows you that removing the phone does not set you free. It might just mean the system has gone somewhere you cannot see it.

**Host B:** Weiser's dream of disappearing technology, turned into a warning.

**Host A:** Which, to be fair to Weiser, he did worry about. He wrote about privacy as a real concern. But the dream was that technology would disappear to serve us. The book asks what happens when it disappears and serves something else.
