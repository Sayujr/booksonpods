# The Devotion of Suspect X. Drishyam, August 2, and the Tenth of March

**Anoop (guest):** If you go to Kerala, and you stop anyone in the street, a bus conductor, a schoolteacher, a grandmother, and you ask them, what happened on August the second, most of them will tell you the same thing. On August the second, Georgekutty and his family went to a prayer retreat in Thodupuzha. They watched a film. They stayed the night in a lodge. They ate a meal in a restaurant. They came home the next day.

**Host A:** And did they?

**Anoop (guest):** No. Not on that day. That is the whole point. It is the most famous alibi in the history of Malayalam cinema, and it is a lie. A whole state knows the date of an event that never happened.

**Host B:** And you found out this summer that it had happened before.

**Anoop (guest):** I found out this summer that it had happened before. Not on August the second, in a hill town in Kerala. On the tenth of March, in Tokyo.

**Host A:** Welcome to Books on Pods. Today we have a guest, which is new for us. Anoop is a film critic from Kochi, in Kerala, in the south west of India. He has been writing about Malayalam cinema for about twenty years. And he came to us with a story about a film, and a book, and a question nobody seems to want to answer properly. Anoop, welcome.

**Anoop (guest):** Thank you. It is strange to be talking about this to people who have never seen the film.

**Host B:** We have now. We watched it last week, with subtitles. But you are going to have to explain a lot. I will be honest, until a month ago, I did not know there was a film industry in Malayalam.

**Anoop (guest):** Most people outside India do not. So let me start with the basics. India does not have one film industry. It has many, one for each major language. Hindi cinema, based in Mumbai, is the one the world calls Bollywood. But there is also Tamil cinema, Telugu cinema, Kannada cinema, Bengali cinema. And there is Malayalam cinema, which is the cinema of Kerala, a state of about thirty five million people, whose language is Malayalam. People sometimes call it Mollywood, though many of us dislike the word.

**Host A:** And it is small, compared with the others?

**Anoop (guest):** Small in money, big in reputation. Malayalam cinema has always been known for realism, for strong writing, for small stories about ordinary people. It is the industry that other Indian film people watch to see what good writing looks like. But for a long time, it was not where the big money was. A big Malayalam hit, before 2013, might make twenty or thirty crore rupees.

**Host B:** Crore. You will need to explain that.

**Anoop (guest):** Of course. A crore is ten million. Indians count in lakhs, which is a hundred thousand, and crores, which is ten million. So when we say a film made fifty crore, that is five hundred million rupees. At today's rate, roughly six million American dollars. Which sounds small to you. But a Malayalam film might cost three or four crore to make. So fifty crore is a different world.

**Host A:** And Drishyam made fifty crore.

**Anoop (guest):** Drishyam was made for about five crore, and released in December 2013, and it became the first Malayalam film ever to cross fifty crore. It ended at about sixty two crore worldwide. It ran in Kerala theatres for more than a hundred and fifty days. In the United Arab Emirates, where there is a huge Malayali community, it ran for a hundred and twenty five days, which was longer than Titanic had run there. It was the highest grossing Malayalam film for two years.

**Host B:** And the title means?

**Anoop (guest):** Drishyam means a visual, or a scene, something seen. Which is a clever title, as you will see, because the whole film is about the difference between what people see and what happened.

**Host A:** And the star is Mohanlal.

**Anoop (guest):** The star is Mohanlal. And I need to explain Mohanlal, because you cannot understand the film without him. There are two great stars in Malayalam cinema, Mohanlal and Mammootty, and they have dominated it for more than forty years. Mohanlal, people call him Lalettan, which means something like Lal, our elder brother. He has acted in hundreds of films. In 2025 he received the Dadasaheb Phalke Award, which is India's highest honour in cinema.

**Host B:** So it is like casting, I do not know, Tom Hanks?

**Anoop (guest):** In a way. Except that Mohanlal has a particular gift. He is the most natural actor I know. He can play a king or a gangster, but his special thing is the ordinary man. The man next door, a little lazy, a little funny, who turns out to have depths. And by 2013, if I am honest, he was in a difficult patch. A run of films that had not worked, big films, star vehicles, where he was playing the superstar. And Drishyam gave him back the ordinary man. The critic at Rediff said the film helped bring Mohanlal back to form. Which is putting it mildly.

**Host A:** And interestingly, he was not the first choice.

**Anoop (guest):** No. The director, Jeethu Joseph, first offered it to Mammootty, who said no. Then he wrote it for Mohanlal. And it became the role of his later career.

**Host B:** So tell us who he plays. For people who have not seen it.

**Anoop (guest):** He plays Georgekutty. George, with kutty added, which is an affectionate ending, like little George. He lives in a village called Rajakkad, in the hills of Idukki, in eastern Kerala. Tea plantations, mist, small towns. He runs a cable television business. In India, at that time, cable TV in small towns was run by local operators, who sat in a little office with a satellite dish and sent channels down the wires to people's homes.

**Host A:** And he watches films all night.

**Anoop (guest):** That is the key. He left school in the fourth standard, which is roughly the fourth grade, so he has almost no formal education. But he sits in his cable office every night watching films. Malayalam films, Tamil films, Hindi films, English films. Thrillers, crime films, police films. And he has learned everything from them. How the police think. How they question people. What evidence is. How memory works.

**Host B:** So he is a self taught genius.

**Anoop (guest):** A cinema educated genius. The critic Sowmya Rajendran wrote that the film reverses the usual snobbery. Intellectuals look down on popular cinema. And here is a man whose whole intelligence was formed by it.

**Host A:** And he has a family.

**Anoop (guest):** His wife, Rani, played by Meena. Two daughters, Anju, who is a teenager, and Anu, who is small. A happy, comfortable, slightly ordinary family. The first hour of the film is almost nothing but family life. Small jokes, small arguments. You fall in love with them. And that is deliberate, because you are going to need to be on their side.

**Host B:** And then.

**Anoop (guest):** And then. Anju goes on a school nature camp, and a boy secretly films her in the bathroom on his phone. He is Varun, the son of a very senior police officer. His mother is Geetha Prabhakar, the Inspector General of Police. An IG, we call it. One of the highest ranks in the state police. He follows Anju home, and he blackmails her. He tells her to meet him at night, or he will put the video on the internet.

**Host A:** And the mother finds out.

**Anoop (guest):** Rani finds out, that night, when he comes to the house. Georgekutty is away, at his office. Rani begs the boy to delete the video. He refuses, and he makes a disgusting demand of her instead. And Anju, desperate, hits him on the head with an iron rod. Once. And he dies. And Rani and Anju, in panic, bury him in the compost pit behind the house. And the little girl, Anu, is watching from the window.

**Host B:** And Georgekutty comes home in the morning.

**Anoop (guest):** Comes home in the morning, and finds out. And the film becomes something else. He does not call the police. He takes control. And the rest of the film is a battle between a man with a fourth standard education and the entire Kerala police, led by the dead boy's mother.

**Host A:** Okay. And this is where the book comes in.

**Anoop (guest):** This is where the book comes in. But before the book, I have to tell you about the rumours. Because I knew the rumours. Everybody in the industry knew the rumours. I just never checked them.

**Host B:** When did they start?

**Anoop (guest):** Almost immediately. Early 2014. The film was a huge hit, and very quickly the producers in other languages wanted to remake it. That is very common in India. If a film is a hit in Malayalam, someone buys the rights to remake it in Telugu, in Tamil, in Hindi, with their own stars. And Drishyam was remade more than almost any Indian film in history. Telugu in 2014, Kannada in 2014, Tamil in 2015, with Kamal Haasan, Hindi in 2015, with Ajay Devgn. Later Sinhala, in Sri Lanka, and Chinese.

**Host A:** Chinese?

**Anoop (guest):** We will come to the Chinese. It is the best part of the story. But when the Hindi remake was being planned, something happened. A producer called Ekta Kapoor, who runs a very big Mumbai company called Balaji, sent a legal notice to the makers.

**Host B:** Why?

**Anoop (guest):** Because she had bought the Indian film rights to a Japanese novel. The Devotion of Suspect X, by Keigo Higashino. She was planning her own official Hindi film of it. And people in the industry told her team, you should look at this Malayalam film. And her team looked, and said, this is our book.

**Host A:** And what did Jeethu Joseph say?

**Anoop (guest):** In March 2014, he gave an interview to the Press Trust of India. And he said, very clearly, that the story came from a situation someone had described to him in a meeting about twelve years earlier. And he said it has not been inspired by any Japanese or Korean authors.

**Host B:** Japanese or Korean.

**Anoop (guest):** Because there was also a Korean film of the novel, from 2012, called Perfect Number. So he denied both. And he said one more thing, which I think is important. He said, when two films are similar, that does not mean that you have copied or been inspired by it.

**Host A:** And people believed him?

**Anoop (guest):** Mostly, yes. In Kerala, absolutely. We were proud of this film. It was ours. And almost nobody had read the novel. It had been translated into English only in 2011. It was not in Kerala bookshops. The Japanese film had been seen by a handful of film buffs. So the accusation was something people heard about, and dismissed. Jealous Bombay people trying to take credit for a Malayalam success.

**Host B:** Was that your view?

**Anoop (guest):** I am embarrassed to say it was. I wrote a column at the time, defending him. I said similarity is not theft, every thriller has a cover up, and so on.

**Host A:** And then?

**Anoop (guest):** And then the Hindi remake came out in 2015, and at the beginning of it, there is a title card that says the story is an original by Jeethu Joseph. And a few critics got angry. Aniruddha Guha wrote a piece in a news website called The Quint, in August 2015. And he called the claim of originality hogwash. His word. And he praised the film. He said it was one of the smartest adaptations in Indian cinema. He just thought the denial was the false note.

**Host B:** And did the legal notice go anywhere?

**Anoop (guest):** Nowhere that I can find. There is no court judgment. There is no settlement that anyone has reported. Ekta Kapoor's company eventually made their own film of the novel, in 2023. It is called Jaane Jaan. Directed by Sujoy Ghosh, starring Kareena Kapoor. It went straight to Netflix, and it did very well. Eight million views in the first week.

**Host A:** And that was when a lot of Indian viewers found out about the book.

**Anoop (guest):** That was when a lot of people watched Jaane Jaan, and said, wait, this is Drishyam. Social media went mad for a week. And then it was forgotten again.

**Host B:** So what made you finally read it?

**Anoop (guest):** Keigo Higashino died in July this year. He was sixty eight. And there were obituaries everywhere. He was one of the best selling writers in the world. More than a hundred million copies of his books sold. And every obituary mentioned The Devotion of Suspect X, because it was the book that won him the Naoki Prize, which is one of Japan's most important literary prizes. And I read down the list of adaptations. Japan. Korea. China. And India, Jaane Jaan.

**Host A:** India.

**Anoop (guest):** And I thought, I have defended a man for twelve years about a book I have never read. So I bought it. I read it in one night. And I closed it at about four in the morning and I sat there and I thought, I owe Aniruddha Guha an apology.

**Host B:** Okay. So make the case. Tell us the book.

**Anoop (guest):** I will tell you the whole book, including the end. Your listeners should know that. If you want to read it fresh, stop now.

**Host A:** We will not stop.

**Anoop (guest):** Good. So. The Devotion of Suspect X. Published in Japan in 2005. A divorced woman called Yasuko Hanaoka lives in a small apartment in Tokyo with her teenage daughter, Misato. Yasuko works in a shop that sells bento, the Japanese lunch boxes. Next door lives a man called Ishigami. He is a high school mathematics teacher. A genius, in fact. As a student he was a brilliant mathematician, the kind of mind that comes along once in a generation. But life did not work out. He had to look after his parents, he never got the academic career. So he teaches maths to teenagers who do not care, and he goes home and does mathematics alone, every night.

**Host B:** A wasted genius.

**Anoop (guest):** A wasted genius. Who, every morning, goes to the bento shop and buys his lunch from Yasuko. Because he is quietly, completely in love with her. He has never said a word.

**Host A:** And then.

**Anoop (guest):** And then, one evening, Yasuko's ex husband turns up. Togashi. A violent, useless man, who has beaten her before, and who has found where she lives, and wants money. He comes into the apartment. He threatens her. He threatens the daughter. And the daughter, Misato, hits him with a heavy vase. And then Yasuko, in a kind of fury and terror, strangles him with an electrical cord. And he is dead. And the two of them are standing there, a mother and her teenage daughter, with a dead man on the floor.

**Host B:** That is the same.

**Anoop (guest):** Wait. And there is a knock on the door. And it is Ishigami. He heard the noise through the wall. And he says, very calmly, I think you need help. And he takes over. Completely. He tells them what to do, what to say, where to go, and he says, leave the body to me.

**Host A:** The man who was not there.

**Anoop (guest):** The man who was not there takes over everything. Exactly like Georgekutty, who was in his office that night.

**Host B:** And then the police.

**Anoop (guest):** And then the police. A few days later a body is found by the Old Edogawa River, on the edge of Tokyo. A man, stripped naked. The face has been smashed so it cannot be recognised. The fingertips have been burned, so there are no fingerprints. And nearby there is a bicycle, which leads the police to identify the body as Togashi. And they work out the time of death as the evening of the tenth of March.

**Host A:** And the ex wife is the obvious suspect.

**Anoop (guest):** The obvious suspect. Two detectives come to see her. One of them is called Kusanagi. And they ask, where were you on the evening of the tenth? And Yasuko says, my daughter and I went to the cinema. Then we had ramen. Then we went to karaoke. And she can prove it. The ramen shop is in the same building as the cinema. The detectives go to the karaoke box, and a member of staff confirms that the mother and daughter were there. Everything checks out.

**Host B:** The cinema. And the meal.

**Anoop (guest):** The cinema and the meal and a third thing, with witnesses. In Drishyam, the family's story for August the second is a prayer retreat, a film, a lodge, a restaurant. And the police in both stories go round checking each piece. And each piece holds.

**Host A:** And the police are suspicious of how well it holds.

**Anoop (guest):** In both stories. The alibi is too good. The detective feels it. Ordinary people do not have alibis that perfect. And in both stories, the family has been coached. Ishigami has prepared Yasuko and Misato for exactly what the police will ask, and how they should answer, so that the mother and daughter, questioned separately, say the same things in their own natural words. Georgekutty does the same. There is that wonderful scene in Drishyam where he drills his family, again and again, around the dining table. What did we see. What did we eat. Who did we meet. Until it is not a lie any more. It is a memory.

**Host B:** But how does Ishigami coach them, if the police are watching the women?

**Anoop (guest):** This is a detail I love. He never phones from his own apartment. Every evening he walks to a public phone, one where he has a reasonable chance of not being seen, and he calls Yasuko with a telephone card. Night after night. So there is no record of calls between the neighbour and the suspect. Just a lonely man at a phone in the dark, giving instructions.

**Host A:** And the cinema tickets?

**Anoop (guest):** The ticket stubs. This is the other detail. Ishigami tells Yasuko to keep the stubs. But not in a drawer. Inside the film's programme, the pamphlet you buy at the theatre. Because, as he says in the novel, nobody goes out of their way to store ticket stubs, and the police might suspect something if she has them in a drawer. Think about that. He is not only designing the evidence. He is designing the careless way an ordinary person would happen to keep it.

**Host B:** And do the detectives notice anyway?

**Anoop (guest):** They ask about the stubs, and where she kept them. And when it reaches Yukawa, the question in the air is the obvious one. Who holds onto ticket stubs?

**Host A:** And Georgekutty.

**Anoop (guest):** Georgekutty keeps everything. The bus ticket, the film tickets, the lodge bill, the restaurant bill. A small town cable operator who suddenly has perfect paperwork for one particular trip. In both stories, the receipts are the alibi. And for a careful investigator, the neatness of the receipts is the crack.

**Host B:** And then the investigator who sees through it.

**Anoop (guest):** Kusanagi cannot crack it. So he goes to his old university friend, a physicist called Yukawa, who helps the police with difficult cases. The Japanese call him Detective Galileo. He is the hero of a whole series of Higashino's books. And Yukawa discovers that the neighbour, Ishigami, was his classmate at university. A man Yukawa has always thought of as a true genius. And Yukawa realises that Ishigami is behind it, and that the case is a duel between two geniuses.

**Host A:** In Drishyam the investigator is the mother.

**Anoop (guest):** Geetha Prabhakar. The dead boy's mother, who is also the IG. Which is Jeethu's great change, and I will come back to it. She is the one who sees through the family. She understands what Georgekutty has done. But she cannot prove it.

**Host B:** Okay. So far, I hear similar stories. A cover up, an alibi, a clever investigator. Jeethu Joseph would say, every crime thriller has those.

**Anoop (guest):** He would. And until I read the book, I would have said the same. So now let me tell you the trick. Because that is where the proof is.

**Host A:** Go on.

**Anoop (guest):** Remember the date. The police are sure Togashi died on the evening of the tenth of March. The medical examiner says so. And the mother and daughter have an alibi for the tenth. They really did go to the cinema on the tenth. They really did eat ramen and sing karaoke.

**Host B:** Really?

**Anoop (guest):** Really. That is the twist. Their alibi is true. Togashi did not die on the tenth. He died on the ninth. The night before.

**Host A:** So whose body is by the river?

**Anoop (guest):** That is the terrible part. It is not Togashi's body at all. Ishigami got rid of Togashi's real body himself, in pieces. And then, on the tenth, he killed another man. A homeless man, one of the men who lived in cardboard shelters by the river, whom he passed every day on his way to school. A man nobody would miss. He smashed the face, burned the fingertips, stripped the body, and left it by the river, with a bicycle nearby, so that everything would point to Togashi. And so the police are investigating a murder that happened on the tenth. While the women are out at the cinema, making the most solid alibi in the world.

**Host B:** That is monstrous.

**Anoop (guest):** It is monstrous. And it is brilliant. Yukawa realises it because the police keep asking about the tenth, and the tenth only. And he understands that Ishigami has done something no detective would think of. He did not make a false alibi for the true date. He moved the date of the crime, so that a true alibi would cover it.

**Host A:** That reminds me of something you said about Ishigami as a teacher.

**Anoop (guest):** Yes. Earlier in the novel, Ishigami explains how he sets exam questions. He gives his students a question that looks like a geometry problem, but is in fact an algebra problem. The students attack it from the angle they think it is, and they cannot solve it. And Yukawa later uses exactly that idea to understand the case. It exploits the solver's own assumptions. The police think they are solving a problem about an alibi. In fact it is a problem about a body, and a date.

**Host B:** Looks like geometry. Is really algebra.

**Anoop (guest):** And Drishyam plays the same game. It looks like a problem about where the family was. It is really a problem about when.

**Host A:** And Georgekutty?

**Anoop (guest):** Now think about Drishyam. Georgekutty does the mirror image. The family's story is that they were away in Thodupuzha on the crucial dates. So he goes to Thodupuzha alone the day after the killing. He collects the bills, the bus tickets, the film tickets, the lodge receipt. He chats to the people, so they will remember him. And then, the next day, he takes the whole family on exactly the same trip. The same bus, the same film, the same lodge, the same restaurant. So the witnesses really did see the family. They just saw them a day later than the bills say. And people remember faces. They do not remember dates.

**Host B:** So he turns real memories into a false alibi.

**Anoop (guest):** Exactly. Ishigami makes the crime fit the alibi. Georgekutty makes the alibi fit the crime. It is the same trick, turned inside out. In both stories, the whole investigation rests on a single date, and the genius's move is to make the police fight on the wrong day.

**Host A:** That is not a genre convention.

**Anoop (guest):** That is not a genre convention. A cover up is a genre convention. An alibi is a genre convention. But the idea that the police are trapped by the date, and that you defeat them by sliding real events across a single day, and that the alibi involves a cinema outing, a meal, and a third activity, with witnesses who genuinely remember the family, that is not a convention. That is a design. And there is more.

**Host B:** More?

**Anoop (guest):** The body. Remember what Ishigami does. He gives the police a substitute body. A different dead man, who they think is the victim. Now think about the climax of Drishyam. The little girl, Anu, is beaten by a brutal police constable called Sahadevan, until she tells them where the body is. In the compost pit. And the police dig, in front of the whole village and the television cameras. And what do they find?

**Host A:** A cow.

**Anoop (guest):** The carcass of a cow. Georgekutty had moved the body and buried an animal in its place. A substitute. The police dig up the wrong body. And in that moment they are humiliated, and the village turns on them.

**Host B:** So the substitute body is there too. Just changed to an animal.

**Anoop (guest):** Changed to an animal, because a Malayalam family film in 2013 was not going to have its hero murder a homeless man. Jeethu Joseph could not use the darkest part of the novel. So he softened it. And I want to say clearly, the softening is brilliant. It is better for his story. But it is the same move.

**Host A:** And the meaning of the book. Devotion.

**Anoop (guest):** The devotion. That is the title, and that is what the book is really about. Ishigami does all of this because of love. And at the end, when he thinks Yukawa is getting close, he goes to the police and confesses. But he confesses as a stalker. He says, I was obsessed with Yasuko, I followed her, I watched her, I killed her ex husband because he was a threat to her. He makes himself into a monster, so that the women will be safe. He even sends anonymous threatening letters, so that his story as a stalker will look true.

**Host B:** He sacrifices himself.

**Anoop (guest):** He sacrifices everything. His freedom, his name, his reputation. And we learn, near the end, why. Before Yasuko and her daughter moved in next door, Ishigami had decided to end his life. He was about to do it. And there was a knock on his door. It was the two of them, introducing themselves as his new neighbours. And their ordinary kindness, that small knock, gave him a reason to keep living.

**Host A:** Oh.

**Anoop (guest):** And then the ending, which reviewers fought about. Yukawa cannot bear to see his friend destroy himself. So he tells Yasuko the truth. What Ishigami has done. The homeless man. All of it. And she cannot live with it. She goes to the police and confesses. And Ishigami, when he sees her, realises that everything he did was for nothing. And he lets out a terrible cry. That is the last page.

**Host B:** And in Drishyam, Georgekutty also wants to protect them.

**Anoop (guest):** Georgekutty's devotion is to his family. That is Jeethu's biggest change. In the novel, the devoted man is an outsider, a neighbour, a lonely man in love with a woman who does not love him. In Drishyam, the devoted man is the father. The devotion becomes family love. Which is exactly what an Indian popular audience would understand and celebrate. A father who will do anything. And it means the investigator also has to be a parent. So Jeethu makes the detective the dead boy's mother. Parent against parent.

**Host A:** That is a wonderful change.

**Anoop (guest):** It is a wonderful change. The film critic Nandini Ramnath put it best. She said Jeethu Joseph's achievement lies in lifting an intelligent concept and localising it so effectively that the links appear tenuous.

**Host B:** Appear tenuous.

**Anoop (guest):** Appear. That is the right word. They appear tenuous only if you have not read the book.

**Host A:** So let me be the devil's advocate. Isn't it possible he really did get the idea from a conversation? And then, by coincidence, it came out like this?

**Anoop (guest):** I asked myself that, honestly. And here is my answer. One similarity is a coincidence. Two is interesting. But here we have a mother and daughter at home, threatened by a man from outside the family. The daughter strikes the first blow. The protector is not there, arrives after, and takes complete control. He is a genius with no recognition. He coaches the women to answer the police. The alibi is a cinema outing, a meal, and a third activity, with real witnesses and carefully kept receipts. The whole case turns on one date, and the trick is to shift real events across a single day. The investigator is a brilliant mind who sees through it but cannot prove it. And the police are defeated by a substitute body. That is not one similarity. That is the skeleton of the book, bone by bone, in the same order.

**Host B:** The skeleton. That is a good word for a story about a body.

**Anoop (guest):** I am a critic. We like our metaphors.

**Host A:** And there is the Japanese film too. Which he admitted seeing.

**Anoop (guest):** He later admitted there were similarities to the Japanese film. The 2008 one, called Suspect X. It was a huge hit in Japan, nearly five billion yen. But he said his film was not an adaptation and not a copy.

**Host B:** So why do you say he was wrong to deny it? Legally, he seems to have been fine.

**Anoop (guest):** Legally he was probably fine. And this is important, so let me explain the law. In India, there is a famous Supreme Court case from 1978, R. G. Anand against Deluxe Films. A playwright called Anand had written a play called Hum Hindustani, and he said a film called New Delhi had copied it. And the Supreme Court said no. It said that copyright protects the expression of an idea, not the idea itself. You cannot own a theme, or a plot idea. The test is whether an ordinary viewer, watching the second work, gets the unmistakable impression that it is a copy of the first.

**Host A:** And would they?

**Anoop (guest):** With Drishyam? The words are different. The setting is different. The characters are different. The religion, the class, the family structure, the language. An ordinary viewer in Kerala would never think, this is a Japanese novel. So in court, I think Jeethu Joseph would very likely have won. That is probably why the legal notice went nowhere.

**Host B:** So what is the problem?

**Anoop (guest):** The problem is that legally safe is not the same as honest. He did not say, my lawyers tell me I am within my rights. He said, it has not been inspired by any Japanese or Korean authors. And that, having read the book, I simply do not believe.

**Host A:** Why do you think he did it?

**Anoop (guest):** I can only guess, and I want to be fair to him. Partly fear, I think. Once you admit inspiration, you open yourself to a lawsuit, and Ekta Kapoor's company was already sending notices. Partly pride. The film was being celebrated as the great original Malayalam thriller. And partly, I think, there is an old habit in Indian cinema. For decades, Indian films borrowed from Hollywood, from Korean films, from European films, without credit, because nobody would ever notice. The audiences would never see the originals. That world ended with the internet, but the habit did not.

**Host B:** And what would it have cost him to say yes?

**Anoop (guest):** Almost nothing. That is what makes me sad. If he had said in 2014, I read a wonderful Japanese novel, and I asked myself what would happen if this story took place in a Kerala village, and the protector was a father, then nobody would have thought less of the film. We would have thought more. Because then everyone could see what he actually did, which is the transformation. Making devotion into fatherhood. Making the detective into a grieving mother. Turning the homeless man into a cow. Teaching the hero everything from cinema. Those are his inventions, and they are great ones. The denial hides his own best work.

**Host A:** That is an interesting way to put it. The denial hides the real achievement.

**Anoop (guest):** And there is a final irony, which is about money. Let me count it up for you. The novel sold, in Japan, well over three million copies. The official Japanese film made nearly five billion yen, which is something like forty or fifty million dollars at the time. There was the official Korean film in 2012, the official Chinese film in 2017, and the official Hindi film, Jaane Jaan, on Netflix in 2023. All with rights paid to Higashino.

**Host B:** And the unofficial line?

**Anoop (guest):** Drishyam, about sixty two crore. Then the remakes. The Tamil one, Papanasam, about a hundred crore. The Hindi one about a hundred and ten crore worldwide. Then the sequels, which we will come to, which made hundreds of crores more. And then China.

**Host A:** You promised us China.

**Anoop (guest):** In 2017, a Chinese company bought the official remake rights to Drishyam. Not to the novel. To Drishyam. And in 2019 they released a film called Sheep Without a Shepherd, set in Thailand, directed by a Malaysian Chinese filmmaker called Sam Quah. And it made about one hundred and ninety nine million dollars.

**Host B:** Two hundred million dollars.

**Anoop (guest):** Nearly two hundred million dollars. It was the first Indian film ever to be officially remade in mainland China. And it made more money than every official adaptation of Higashino's novel put together. Probably several times over. And as far as I can find, none of that money went to Higashino.

**Host A:** So the Chinese paid India for a Japanese idea.

**Anoop (guest):** The Chinese paid Kerala for a Japanese idea. Which a scholar at Duke University, Krista Van Fleit, has actually given a talk about. She studies how stories move between Asian film industries. And her argument is that we should not only see this as theft, centre and periphery, but also as what she calls the creative power of cultural borrowing. Stories travel across Asia now, from Tokyo to Kerala to Beijing to Seoul, and each industry remakes them into its own world.

**Host B:** Do you accept that?

**Anoop (guest):** I accept it as a description of what happens. Stories have always travelled. Shakespeare took his plots from Italian novellas and never paid anyone. But Shakespeare did not issue a press release saying he had never read Italian. The borrowing is not the problem. The denial is.

**Host A:** Okay. Let us talk about the sequel. Because you told us before we started recording that you had a theory about Drishyam 2.

**Anoop (guest):** I do. Drishyam 2 came out in February 2021, in the middle of the pandemic. And it was a landmark for another reason. It went straight to Amazon Prime Video, in more than two hundred countries, without any cinema release. Because the cinemas in Kerala were closed.

**Host B:** And the industry did not like that.

**Anoop (guest):** The cinema owners were furious. The Kerala Film Chamber, which is the body that represents producers and exhibitors, ruled that films sold directly to streaming could not later be shown in Kerala theatres. Trade people estimated it might have made a hundred or two hundred crore in cinemas. But it went to Amazon, and it was a huge streaming hit. And it was the moment many people outside Kerala discovered Malayalam cinema at all.

**Host A:** And the story?

**Anoop (guest):** It is six years later. The family is still living with what happened. Anju has terrible anxiety. Georgekutty is richer, he owns a cinema now, and he wants to produce a film. And the police have quietly reopened the case. They have planted an undercover officer as the family's neighbour. And a witness comes forward. A man who, on the night, years ago, saw Georgekutty coming out of the police station that was being built. Because, and this is the end of the first film, Georgekutty buried Varun's body under the floor of the new police station. In the foundations.

**Host B:** Under the police station.

**Anoop (guest):** Under the police station. That is the most famous final shot in Malayalam cinema. And in Drishyam 2, the police dig it up. They find the skeleton. Georgekutty is arrested. And then he confesses. He says, I did it, I killed him, to protect his family from the questioning.

**Host A:** That sounds familiar.

**Anoop (guest):** That is what I thought. And then, in court, the twist. The forensic test comes back, and the bones are not Varun's.

**Host B:** Not Varun's.

**Anoop (guest):** Georgekutty had prepared, long before, for the day the body would be found. And through a man who worked at a mortuary, Varun's remains are swapped for the bones of an unidentified body before the test. So when the police test the skeleton, it is the wrong body.

**Host A:** A substitute body.

**Anoop (guest):** A substitute body. An unknown, unclaimed dead person, whom nobody will miss, standing in for the victim. That is the central trick of Higashino's novel. The trick Jeethu could not use in 2013, because his hero could not kill a homeless man. In 2021 he found a way to use it without a murder. The unclaimed body from the mortuary.

**Host B:** So your theory is that Drishyam 2 uses the parts of the novel that Drishyam 1 left out.

**Anoop (guest):** That is my theory. The first film took the structure and the alibi. The second film took the substitute body and the protector's false confession. Between them, they use almost the whole novel.

**Host A:** Is that fair? Has anyone else said this?

**Anoop (guest):** I have not found anyone who has said it in print, and I want to be honest that it is my reading. Nobody accused Drishyam 2 of copying. And a lot of Drishyam 2 is genuinely original. The undercover neighbour. The witness who wants reward money. And the brilliant courtroom device, where it turns out that Georgekutty had told the whole story, years earlier, to a novelist, as an idea for a book. And the novelist has written it, and Georgekutty's lawyers argue that the police have built their case from a novel.

**Host B:** Wait. In the sequel, the defence is that the case comes from a novel.

**Host A:** That is almost funny.

**Anoop (guest):** I laughed out loud when I realised. A film accused of secretly coming from a novel makes a sequel in which the hero escapes by claiming the police case secretly comes from a novel. I do not know if Jeethu meant it as a private joke. I would love to ask him.

**Host B:** And Drishyam 3?

**Anoop (guest):** Briefly, because it is less relevant to the book. Drishyam 3 came out this year, on Mohanlal's birthday, the twenty first of May. It made about two hundred and thirty seven crore worldwide, the biggest of the three. The reviews were mixed. Almost everyone praised Mohanlal, many said the screenplay was the weakest of the three. And there is a Hindi version, with Ajay Devgn, which opened on the second of October and was past four hundred crore within nine days. And there are remakes coming in Korean, in English with an American company, even in Spanish.

**Host A:** So it is a franchise now.

**Anoop (guest):** It is a franchise. A Malayalam family thriller made for five crore has become one of the most remade stories in the world. And the novel underneath it is still, for most of its audience, invisible.

**Host B:** Can we talk about why it works? Because I have to say, watching it, I was completely on Georgekutty's side. And he is covering up a killing.

**Host A:** And making a little girl lie to the police.

**Anoop (guest):** And letting the police beat her. That is the uncomfortable part. And yes, the whole audience is on his side. In the theatre where I saw it, people clapped when the police dug up the cow.

**Host B:** Why?

**Anoop (guest):** There are a few reasons, and they are different in the book and the film. In the film, the first reason is the police. Kerala, like the rest of India, has a long, painful history of police brutality. Custodial deaths, people beaten to death in police stations. There have been famous Indian films about it, like Visaranai in Tamil, which was India's entry for the Oscar, and Jai Bhim. So when Constable Sahadevan beats the family, beats a little girl, every person in the audience knows that this happens. And the film makes him the one true villain.

**Host A:** And the police complained about the film.

**Anoop (guest):** They did. In January 2014, a very senior Kerala police officer, T. P. Senkumar, said publicly that the film gave the wrong message. He said a victim of blackmail should go to the police, not kill the culprit and hide the crime. And he said Mohanlal should have thought twice before committing to such a role.

**Host B:** How did that go down?

**Anoop (guest):** Badly. People mocked it on social media. Another senior officer, R. Sreelekha, said cinema should be seen as art, and that she had enjoyed it. And Jeethu Joseph made the obvious reply. Who would believe they could get justice from the police, when the boy who did it is the son of the police chief?

**Host A:** Which is the real question of the film.

**Anoop (guest):** It is. And there is a recent essay that takes the other side seriously. This June, a retired senior officer, B. Sandhya, who served in the Kerala police crime branch for years, wrote a reflection on Drishyam. And she argued that the law could have handled it. That modern forensic science would have found the truth. And that escaping punishment does not end the suffering. Her phrase was that freedom from punishment can mean lifelong imprisonment of guilt.

**Host B:** Which is exactly what the sequels show.

**Anoop (guest):** Exactly what the sequels show. Georgekutty never sleeps properly again. Anju has panic attacks. The family is in a prison without walls.

**Host A:** Let me bring in some philosophy here, because I think this is the deep question. When we live in a society, we agree to give up our right to punish people ourselves. We hand it to the state. The police, the courts. That is the basic deal of civil society. Thomas Hobbes, in the seventeenth century, said that without that deal, life would be a war of everyone against everyone.

**Host B:** Nasty, brutish and short.

**Host A:** Nasty, brutish and short. But John Locke, a little later, added a condition. He said we hand our power to the state so that there will be a common judge, someone impartial between us. And if there is no impartial judge, then we are back in what he called the state of nature, where each person has to defend themselves.

**Anoop (guest):** That is Drishyam exactly. The judge is not impartial. The person investigating the killing is the dead boy's mother. The police are her men. So Georgekutty, in Locke's terms, is back in the state of nature. He has to be his own law.

**Host B:** So the film is a thought experiment about what happens when the social contract breaks down.

**Anoop (guest):** For a small family, yes. And there is an Indian thinker who makes this even sharper. Partha Chatterjee, a political scientist from Kolkata. He argued that in India, what Western theory calls civil society, citizens with rights, dealing with the state through law and courts, really only describes a small, educated, urban class. Most Indians, he said, live in what he called political society. They deal with the state not through rights, but through networks, negotiation, local solidarity, favours, crowds.

**Host A:** And Georgekutty.

**Anoop (guest):** Georgekutty is a perfect citizen of political society. He has a fourth standard education. He does not trust the law. He does not have a lawyer. What he has is the village. The tea shop, the friends, the neighbours who like him, the crowd that turns on the police when they dig up the cow. His protection is not his rights. It is that people know him and love him.

**Host B:** So civil society is a kind of myth, for people like him.

**Anoop (guest):** A myth for some, a reality for others. The film shows you a man for whom the promise of civil society is not real, and who survives by the other kind.

**Host A:** And there is something even darker, which the German critic Walter Benjamin wrote about in 1921, in an essay called Critique of Violence. He noticed something odd. That the figure of the great criminal, even when his aims are repellent, arouses the secret admiration of the public. And his explanation was that it is not the crime we admire. It is that the criminal shows that violence outside the law is still possible. The state claims a monopoly on violence, and the great criminal breaks the monopoly. And something in us cheers.

**Host B:** That is uncomfortable.

**Host A:** It is meant to be. Why do we cheer when Georgekutty beats the police? Partly because he is a good man protecting his child. But partly, Benjamin would say, because he has shown that the state is not all powerful. That a small man can beat it.

**Anoop (guest):** And Malayalis love an underdog. The cable operator against the IG.

**Host B:** And the storytelling helps. Because we know who did it from the start.

**Anoop (guest):** That is a very important point, and it applies to both the book and the film. Most detective stories are whodunnits. You do not know who did it until the end. But this kind of story is what critics call an inverted detective story. You see the crime at the start. The question is not who, but whether they will get away with it, and how.

**Host A:** Like Columbo.

**Anoop (guest):** Like Columbo. And when you watch a crime from the inside, you start to share the criminal's anxiety. Alfred Hitchcock understood this perfectly. In Psycho, there is a scene where Norman Bates pushes a car with a body in it into a swamp. And the car starts to sink, and then it stops, half under. And Hitchcock told the director François Truffaut that the audience, watching that, were thinking, I hope it goes all the way down. Even though they know there is a body in it.

**Host B:** We all want the car to sink.

**Anoop (guest):** We all want the car to sink. Drishyam is two hours of wanting the car to sink.

**Host A:** And the scholars of television have a name for this. Margrethe Bruun Vaage, who writes about antiheroes, Tony Soprano, Walter White, calls it fictional relief. Fiction lets us take a holiday from our usual morality. We can root for someone we would condemn in real life, because it is not real. And then we go back to being good citizens.

**Anoop (guest):** But I think Drishyam is different from those. Walter White is a villain we enjoy. Georgekutty is a hero we approve of. The film does not let you feel guilty. Except, a little, for the parents.

**Host B:** The Prabhakars.

**Anoop (guest):** The end of the first film is very quiet. Geetha and her husband come to Georgekutty, and they apologise for their son. And they say, we just want to know what happened to our boy. And Georgekutty tells them, in a roundabout way, a story about a man who would do anything for his family. He never confesses. But they understand. And they have to live with not knowing where their son is.

**Host A:** That is the cost.

**Anoop (guest):** That is the cost. And the novel is much more honest about the cost than the film. Because in the novel, an innocent man dies. A homeless man, with no name, killed so that the police will have a body. The novel never lets you forget him. The film, by turning him into a cow, takes that cost away. And I think that is why the film is easier to love, and the book is harder to forget.

**Host B:** That is a really interesting difference. The book is about the price of devotion. The film is about the reward.

**Anoop (guest):** Yes. In the book, devotion destroys everyone. Ishigami, Yasuko, the homeless man. The ending is a cry of despair. In the film, devotion saves the family. The ending is a smile in the police station.

**Host A:** And there is a critique of the film's devotion too, isn't there? The family honour part.

**Anoop (guest):** Yes. Sowmya Rajendran, whom I mentioned before, loved the film, but she pointed out something important. The whole crisis is about a video of a girl's body. And the film never really questions the idea that this would be a catastrophe of honour for the family. She argued that a mass medium should question those ideas, not reinforce them. In the novel, the threat is a violent man. In the film, part of the threat is shame.

**Host B:** Can we talk about other stories like this? Because when you describe it, I keep thinking of Antigone.

**Host A:** Oh, yes.

**Anoop (guest):** Tell me.

**Host B:** Antigone, by Sophocles. Fifth century before Christ. Antigone's brother has died fighting against the city, and the king, Creon, has ordered that his body be left unburied, as a traitor. And Antigone buries him anyway, because family duty and the laws of the gods matter more to her than the law of the state. And she is condemned for it.

**Host A:** And the philosopher Hegel read Antigone as the great example of tragedy, because both sides are right. The family is right, and the state is right. Two goods in collision.

**Anoop (guest):** And it is also about a body. Who controls the body, where it is buried. That is Drishyam. A family hiding a body from the state.

**Host B:** And Les Misérables. The policeman Javert, who believes absolutely in the law, chasing Jean Valjean, a good man who once broke it. And in the end Javert cannot bear the discovery that the law and justice are not the same thing.

**Anoop (guest):** That is the whole question. The philosopher Jacques Derrida, in a famous lecture called Force of Law, said that law and justice are never the same. Law can be written down and applied. Justice is something we are always reaching for and never quite arrive at. Every legal judgment is an approximation.

**Host A:** And in cinema, Bong Joon-ho's film Mother, from 2009, which I think is a perfect companion. A Korean mother whose son is accused of murdering a girl, and who will do anything at all to protect him. Anything.

**Anoop (guest):** I know it. It is the dark sister of Drishyam. It asks what Drishyam does not dare to ask. What if the devotion is the crime?

**Host B:** And Patricia Highsmith's Ripley. The charming murderer who keeps getting away with it, and whom readers somehow want to get away with it.

**Anoop (guest):** And Crime and Punishment, which is the opposite. A man who gets away with it legally and is destroyed by his own conscience. Which is what Drishyam 2 and 3 turn into. The sequels are Dostoevsky. The first film is Hitchcock.

**Host A:** Let me ask about the book on its own terms. Because we have been treating it as the source of the film. Is it good?

**Anoop (guest):** It is very good, and it is also not perfect, and the critics were split. When it came out in English in 2011, it was nominated for the Edgar Award, the most important prize in American crime writing. The Los Angeles Times called the ending one of the most clever endings in some time. The Independent called it a postmodern take on the conventions of the detective novel.

**Host B:** And the other side?

**Anoop (guest):** The Guardian said it was cold and mechanical. And the Washington Post reviewer, Patrick Anderson, said that some readers might find the ending ingenious, but he found it simply unfair, a cheat.

**Host A:** A cheat?

**Anoop (guest):** Because Higashino hides the key fact, the homeless man, from the reader. He shows you the homeless men by the river, but never tells you one is missing. And this caused a real argument in Japan too. There is a tradition in Japanese crime fiction called honkaku, which means something like orthodox, or authentic. It is the classic puzzle mystery, where the author plays fair, and gives the reader every clue needed to solve it. And in 2005, a well known mystery writer, Reito Nikaido, argued that Suspect X was not real honkaku, because the clues were hidden unfairly.

**Host B:** And what happened?

**Anoop (guest):** A big public argument among Japanese mystery writers. Most of them disagreed with him. The book won the Honkaku Mystery Award anyway. And Higashino said only that readers should decide for themselves.

**Host A:** So the book was accused of cheating on its clues.

**Anoop (guest):** And the film was accused of hiding its source. There is something poetic in that. A story about hiding things, where everyone who touches it is accused of hiding something.

**Host B:** And Higashino himself. What do you know about him now?

**Anoop (guest):** More than I did. He was an engineer before he was a writer. He wrote more than a hundred books. He was enormously popular in Japan and China, and widely translated. **Host A:** Which is the title, really. Not the puzzle. The devotion.

**Anoop (guest):** The devotion. A lonely mathematician who was saved by a knock on his door, and who decided to give everything back. That is what the book is about. And that is what Jeethu Joseph understood, and turned into a father. And that is why I am not angry with him, really. I am sad. Because he made something wonderful out of something wonderful, and he could not bring himself to say so.

**Host B:** If you could say one thing to him.

**Anoop (guest):** I would say, Jeethu chettan, you did not need to hide the body. It was a beautiful body. You buried it under the police station, and we have spent twelve years digging in the wrong place.
