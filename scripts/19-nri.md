**Arjun (guest):** Everybody knows it, and nobody says it out loud. In most of South Asia, you wash with water after using the toilet. There is a hose, or a little jug, a lota. **Host B:** And Americans find the opposite strange.

**Arjun (guest):** And Americans find the water strange. Until 2020. Because in the early pandemic, when toilet paper vanished from American shops, lots of Americans suddenly bought bidets. And there was a moment when every Indian I knew in New Jersey felt quietly vindicated.

**Host A:** The great bidet awakening.

**Arjun (guest):** The second shock is shoes. In an Indian home, you take your shoes off at the door. Always. The street is dirty, the home is clean, and the shoes belong to the street. **Host B:** That is exactly what your landlady felt.

**Host A:** That is exactly it.

In India, nobody of her class would touch that. That would be someone else's job. A lower caste person's job.

**Host A:** And that is the whole topic, isn't it.

**Arjun (guest):** That is the whole topic. Who cleans. Who is supposed to clean. And what counts as clean.

**Host B:** So here is my question. In the Indian home, you take off your shoes, you wash with water, the floor is spotless. And yet the online picture of India, the one that gets millions of views, is a picture of filth. Rubbish in the streets, people spitting, open drains. How do both things exist at once?

**Arjun (guest):** The best answer I know comes from a political scientist called Sudipta Kaviraj. In 1997 he wrote an essay called Filth and the Public Sphere, about Calcutta. And he pointed out that in Bengali, and in many Indian languages, there is a strong distinction between the inside and the outside. Bhitor and bahir in Bengali. The inside is the home, the family, the realm of purity and care. The outside is everything else. And the outside, in Kaviraj's argument, is not public in the Western sense. It does not belong to everyone. It belongs to no one. It is the space beyond your responsibility.

**Host A:** So you clean the inside by pushing the dirt to the outside.

**Arjun (guest):** Exactly. The cleaning of the home is often literally done by sweeping the dirt out of the door, into the lane. The cleaner the inside, the dirtier the outside. My grandmother's house in Pune was immaculate. And the lane outside was where everything went.

**Host B:** Which is your landlady and the banana leaf.

**Host A:** The banana leaf over the wall. Yes.

**Arjun (guest):** And this is where I think the old idea of guilt and shame is really useful. Can I bring it in?

**Host A:** Please.

**Arjun (guest):** In 1946, an American anthropologist called Ruth Benedict wrote a famous book about Japan, The Chrysanthemum and the Sword. And she made a distinction that became hugely influential, and also hugely controversial. She said some cultures are organised around guilt, and some around shame. Guilt is internal. You do something wrong, and you feel bad inside, whether or not anyone knows. It is a judgement by the self. Shame is external. In her words, shame is a reaction to other people's criticism. And, crucially, she said, it requires an audience.

**Host B:** It requires an audience.

**Arjun (guest):** Shame only works if someone is watching. Or if you imagine someone is watching.

**Host A:** And she was writing about Japan, which is the model of public cleanliness. The coffee can.

**Arjun (guest):** Which is very interesting. Because Japan, in Benedict's scheme, is a shame culture. And Japanese public cleanliness is often explained exactly that way. Everyone is watching. The street has an audience. You felt it yourself. You said you felt watched by the whole street.

**Host B:** I did.

**Arjun (guest):** And Japanese children clean their own classrooms. Every day, at school, the students sweep the floors and wipe the desks. There are no cleaners. So from childhood you learn that public space is yours to look after, and that everyone can see whether you do.

**Host A:** So why does it not work the same way in India? India is also often described as a shame culture. Family honour, what will people say. There is even a phrase for it.

**Arjun (guest):** Log kya kahenge. What will people say. It is perhaps the most powerful sentence in Indian life. It decides who you marry, what you study, what you wear.

**Host A:** So India has shame. Lots of it.

**Arjun (guest):** India has enormous amounts of shame. But here is the point. Shame needs an audience, and the audience that matters in India is a particular audience. Your family. Your caste. Your neighbours. Your community. The people whose opinion decides your marriage prospects. Log kya kahenge means, what will our people say. It does not mean, what will a stranger at the bus stop say.

**Host B:** So the street has no audience.

**Arjun (guest):** The street has no audience that counts. In a crowded Indian city, the street is full of strangers. Thousands of them. And their opinion does not matter, because they are not your people. You will never see them again. Nobody at the bus stop is going to tell your mother. So the shame mechanism, which is very powerful at home, simply does not switch on outside.

**Host A:** And in Tokyo the strangers count.

**Arjun (guest):** In Tokyo, everyone is in some sense part of the same audience. That is a big generalisation, and Japanese scholars have argued with Benedict for seventy years. She never even visited Japan. She wrote the book during the war, from interviews and films and documents. But the core idea, that shame depends on who you think is watching, I think is right.

**Host B:** Which brings us to the internet. Because the internet has made the whole world an audience.

**Arjun (guest):** Yes. And that is where it gets ugly.

**Host A:** Let us talk about the online side. Because this is a huge part of the conversation now. If you spend any time on social media, you will see them. **Arjun (guest):** And a lot of it is not about hygiene at all.

**Arjun (guest):** And that is a very old move. Calling a group of immigrants dirty is one of the oldest tricks there is. In the nineteenth century in America and Britain, it was said about the Irish. Then about the Chinese, in California, with their laundries. Then about Italians, and Jews from Eastern Europe. Each new wave of poor immigrants was described as dirty, smelly, carrying disease. Dirt is the easiest thing to accuse a stranger of, because it makes them disgusting, and disgust is harder to argue with than an opinion.

**Host B:** So is it all racism? Because I want to be fair. Some of those videos are real. The rubbish is real.

**Arjun (guest):** The rubbish is real. I am not going to pretend Indian streets are clean. I live half the year in Pune. They are not. And that is the uncomfortable thing about this conversation. **Host A:** How do you tell them apart?

**Arjun (guest):** I have a rough test. Does the criticism lead somewhere? Does it ask who collects the rubbish, why there are no bins, who pays the sanitation workers, why the drains overflow? **Host B:** And here is the argument that I see constantly, under every one of those videos. **Arjun (guest):** That argument is everywhere. And there is actually a wonderful piece of research that speaks to it. Can I tell you about the diplomats' parking tickets?

**Host A:** Please.

**Arjun (guest):** Two economists, Raymond Fisman and Edward Miguel, published a study in 2007. In New York City, United Nations diplomats had diplomatic immunity. They could park anywhere, block anything, and they could not be forced to pay the fines. So it was a perfect natural experiment. You have people from every country in the world, in the same city, with the same streets, and no penalty for breaking the rules.

**Host B:** And what happened?

**Arjun (guest):** Diplomats from countries with a lot of corruption at home ran up far more unpaid parking tickets than diplomats from countries with little corruption. Norway, Sweden, almost none. So the cultural norms people brought from home really did travel with them. Culture mattered.

**Host A:** So the commenters are right.

**Arjun (guest):** Wait. Because then, in 2002, the rules changed. The authorities got the power to take away the diplomatic number plates of cars with unpaid tickets. Real enforcement. And unpaid violations fell dramatically, by more than ninety per cent, almost overnight. For everybody.

**Host B:** So culture matters, but enforcement matters more.

**Arjun (guest):** Enforcement swamps culture. People bring their habits with them, yes. But when the rule is real and the penalty is real, almost everyone follows it, wherever they come from. Singapore is famous for its fines. Littering, spitting, not flushing a public toilet. And Indians in Singapore follow them for the same reason Singaporeans do.

**Host A:** And there is the other piece of research we love, about litter itself.

**Host B:** The Groningen experiments. In 2008, three researchers in the Netherlands, Kees Keizer and his colleagues, published a set of field experiments in Science. In one, they put advertising flyers on the handlebars of bicycles parked in an alley. In one version, the alley wall was clean. In another, it was covered in graffiti, with a sign saying no graffiti. And they watched how many people threw the flyer on the ground.

**Arjun (guest):** And?

**Host B:** In the clean alley, about a third of people littered. In the graffiti alley, more than two thirds did. Same people, same city, same flyer. The only difference was whether the place looked like a place where rules were followed.

**Arjun (guest):** So dirt teaches dirt. And cleanliness teaches cleanliness.

**Host A:** And that is my answer to the immigrant argument. When Prashant, in your novel, arrives in New Jersey, he is not becoming a different person. He is walking into a clean alley. With enforcement. And with a new audience.

**Arjun (guest):** Yes. And that last part is the most interesting to me. Because when you move abroad, especially when you look different, suddenly the strangers on the street do count. Suddenly you are visible. Everyone is watching the brown man to see if he will drop his wrapper. And you know that if you do, it will not be your shame. It will be your whole country's shame. You become a representative. The audience appears.

**Host B:** Shame comes back.

**Arjun (guest):** Shame comes back, because the audience comes back. That is the chapter of my novel that people in India argue with most. **Host A:** Bada angrez ban raha hai.

**Arjun (guest):** Exactly that. And Prashant realises he has become the N.R.I. he used to laugh at. The Americanah. Shouting at his father about a banana peel.

**Host B:** And was his father wrong?

**Arjun (guest):** That is the question the book refuses to answer. The peel goes on the road. Somebody sweeps the road. Probably a woman from a caste that has swept roads for generations. **Host A:** I want to turn the mirror round now. Because the online picture is not just that India is dirty, it is that the West is clean. And is it?

**Host B:** Not really. Not historically, and not now. Let us start with history. In the summer of 1858, London had what is still called the Great Stink. The Thames was effectively an open sewer, and the smell in the heat was so bad that Parliament, which sits on the river, could barely work. They soaked the curtains in chemicals. And only then did they pay for the great sewer system that the engineer Joseph Bazalgette built. London was not clean because Londoners had good manners. It was clean because it built sewers, after the smell reached the politicians.

**Arjun (guest):** Infrastructure, not virtue.

**Host A:** And then there is America. Can I tell you about the Crying Indian?

**Arjun (guest):** The advert? I know it from the internet, but tell me.

**Host A:** In 1971, an organisation called Keep America Beautiful ran a television advert that became one of the most famous public service adverts in American history. A Native American man paddles a canoe down a river full of rubbish, walks to the side of a highway, and someone throws a bag of litter out of a car window, and it lands at his feet. And the camera closes in on his face, and a single tear runs down his cheek. And the slogan was, People Start Pollution. People Can Stop It.

**Host B:** That is a guilt advert. Pure guilt.

**Host A:** Pure guilt. It is designed to make you feel bad inside. And it worked, in the sense that everyone remembers it. But two things. First, the actor, who called himself Iron Eyes Cody, was not Native American. He was the son of Italian immigrants. And second, Keep America Beautiful was funded by the companies making the litter. Beverage companies, bottlers, can makers. Coca Cola and Pepsi were among them.

**Arjun (guest):** Of course they were.

**Host A:** And historians have argued that the whole point was to make litter a matter of individual guilt, not industrial responsibility. People start pollution. Not, companies make billions of disposable bottles. Not, we should have deposits on bottles. Just, you, personally, should feel bad.

**Host B:** And then there is Texas. In the eighties Texas had a big litter problem on its highways, and they realised that guilt adverts did not work on the people who were littering most, who were mostly young men in pickup trucks. So in 1986 they created a campaign that did not mention guilt or nature at all. It just said, Don't Mess with Texas. Pride. Identity. Toughness. And litter on Texas highways reportedly fell dramatically in the first few years.

**Arjun (guest):** That is very clever. That is not guilt, and it is not even shame. It is honour. Make the clean thing the proud thing.

**Host A:** And the modern West? Paris, in March 2023. The rubbish collectors went on strike against pension reform, and within a couple of weeks there were reportedly something like ten thousand tonnes of uncollected rubbish on the streets of Paris. Piles of bags as high as people, outside cafés, with rats. The most beautiful city in the world. And the pictures went round the world, and nobody said Parisians were a dirty people.

**Arjun (guest):** That is my point exactly. When Paris is dirty, it is a strike. When Delhi is dirty, it is the people.

**Host B:** And New York has spent years trying to get rubbish bags off its pavements, because for decades the city just put black bags out on the sidewalk for collection, in huge walls, every night. San Francisco has had serious problems with human waste on its streets, linked to homelessness and the lack of public toilets. So the West is not clean by nature. It is clean where it pays for cleaning, and dirty where it does not.

**Arjun (guest):** And the reason the online picture is different is the audience. When a Western city is dirty, the people filming it are locals, and they blame the council. When an Indian city is dirty, the people filming it are often outsiders, and they blame the people.

**Host A:** Let us go back to South Asia, and to something that actually worked. Because there is a real success story here, and it is Bangladesh.

**Arjun (guest):** Bangladesh is the story nobody online knows.

**Host A:** In 2003, about forty two per cent of people in Bangladesh defecated in the open. Within about twelve years, it was down to roughly one per cent. One of the biggest public health achievements anywhere in the world. Poorer than India, more crowded, and it did what India struggled to do.

**Host B:** How?

**Host A:** A lot of things. Government commitment, funding, local governments, NGOs like BRAC, women having more say over building latrines, and a toilet becoming a status symbol. But one of the most famous tools was a method called Community Led Total Sanitation. It was developed in Bangladesh in 2000 by Kamal Kar, an Indian development specialist. And it is based, quite deliberately, on shame and disgust.

**Arjun (guest):** The walk of shame.

**Host A:** The walk of shame, people call it. A facilitator comes to a village and leads everyone on a walk through their own village, to find where people have been defecating in the open. They stand around it. They talk about it. Sometimes they do a demonstration with a hair, touching the faeces and then dipping into a glass of water, and ask someone if they would drink it. Flies, they explain, do the same thing, carrying it into your food.

**Host B:** That is shame as public policy.

**Host A:** It is shame turned into a technology. And the critics are fierce. One aid worker in Nepal called the process, simply put, condescending and offensive. Others pointed out that in a caste society, it is often higher caste families shaming lower caste families, which can reinforce discrimination. And it can turn into neighbours policing neighbours.

**Arjun (guest):** And the defenders say?

**Host A:** One researcher, Amanda Glassman, put it bluntly. The ethical questions are real, she said, but it is unethical to let children die too. Because open defecation kills children, through diarrhoea, and it stunts the growth of the ones who survive.

**Arjun (guest):** And here is the thing that I think explains why the same method worked less well in India. There is a book by two researchers, Diane Coffey and Dean Spears, called Where India Goes, from 2017. And they found something astonishing. In rural north India, even in households that had a working latrine, in over forty per cent, at least one person still defecated in the open.

**Host B:** Even with a toilet.

**Arjun (guest):** Even with a toilet. And their explanation was caste. Ideas of ritual purity. In this way of thinking, a latrine pit near the house is polluting, and emptying it is the work of the lowest castes, so people want enormous pits that never need emptying, or they avoid the latrine altogether. It is not ignorance. It is purity. The house must stay pure, so the waste must go outside.

**Host A:** Kaviraj again. The inside and the outside.

**Arjun (guest):** Kaviraj again. And it all comes back to who cleans. In India, cleaning has been pushed for centuries onto Dalit communities, the castes once called untouchable. Manual scavenging, cleaning dry latrines and sewers by hand, is illegal, and still happens. So when the Indian middle class says, people have no civic sense, there is often an unspoken assumption underneath. Somebody else will clean it. Somebody else is supposed to.

**Host B:** Which is your woman in the fur coat in Montclair.

**Arjun (guest):** Picking up her own dog's mess. That was the most revolutionary thing I saw in America. Not the clean streets. A rich person cleaning up after herself.

**Host A:** There is a novel I want to recommend, which you mentioned to me, Arjun.

**Arjun (guest):** Leila, by Prayaag Akbar, from 2017. It was made into a Netflix series. It imagines an Indian city split into walled sectors, each organised around purity, of caste, of religion, of food. The wealthy live inside the walls, and the slums are outside. And it takes the inside and outside idea to its terrifying conclusion. A whole city built on the logic of keeping the dirt out. If you want to see where purity leads, read it.

**Host B:** Let me try to pull this together. We started with a coffee can in Tokyo and a banana leaf in Chennai.

**Host A:** And Americanah.

**Host B:** And Americanah. So what makes a street clean? I think we have found at least four things. Infrastructure. Bins, collection, sewers, paid workers. London built sewers, Paris has garbage trucks, and when they stop, Paris is as dirty as anywhere. Enforcement. The diplomats' parking tickets, Singapore's fines. A norm you carry inside, which is guilt in Benedict's sense, the voice that says I do not do that, even when no one is looking. And an audience you respect, which is shame. The street in Tokyo that watches you.

**Arjun (guest):** And I would add a fifth. Dignity for the people who clean. Because as long as cleaning is somebody else's caste, the street will be somebody else's problem.

**Host A:** And the online shaming?

**Arjun (guest):** I think it mostly fails. And the reason is Benedict's own logic. Shame needs an audience that matters to you. Strangers on the internet, mocking your country, are not an audience that matters. They are an audience you resent. So instead of shame, you get defensiveness. Nationalism. People who would happily criticise their own city in their own kitchen will defend it to the death against a foreigner's comment. The internet does not shame India into cleanliness. It shames India into pride.

**Host B:** And the diaspora? The N.R.I. posting about how dirty home is?

**Arjun (guest):** That is the most interesting case, because the N.R.I. is half inside the audience and half outside. My family listens to me, a little, because I am theirs. But they also roll their eyes, because I am an angrez now. Bada angrez. The Americanah is always in that position. Too foreign to be listened to, too much ours to be ignored.

**Host A:** Which is where Americanah ends, really. Ifemelu goes back to Lagos, joins the club of complaining returnees, is ashamed of them, and of herself. And then she does something else. She stops complaining and starts noticing. She writes about Lagos with love and with clear eyes.

**Arjun (guest):** The Small Redemptions of Lagos. That is the line I wanted in my own book. Not, how dirty home is. Not, how wonderful America is. The small redemptions.

**Host B:** What does that look like, for an N.R.I. in Pune?

**Arjun (guest):** For Prashant, at the end of my novel, it looks very small. He goes back to the lane outside his father's house, the lane where everything has been swept for fifty years. And he does not shout at anybody. He does not post a video. He just starts sweeping it himself, in the mornings, before work. And the neighbours laugh at him. And then, after a few weeks, the man next door starts doing his part too.
