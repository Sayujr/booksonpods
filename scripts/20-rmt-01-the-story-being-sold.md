# Responsive Math Teaching 1. The Story Being Sold

**Host A:** In the early 1960s, a man who had spent years working in advertising sat down at home in Illinois with his twin sons, who were four years old, and taught them algebra.

**Host B:** Four years old.

**Host A:** Four. Not counting. Not shapes. Algebra. Simple equations, with unknowns. And he filmed it. And he showed the film to anyone who would watch, saying, look. If my boys can do this, at four, then everything you believe about what young children are ready for is wrong.

**Host B:** And this is Zig Engelmann.

**Host A:** Siegfried Engelmann. Zig, to everyone who knew him. And he is the hero of the second season of a podcast that has changed American education more than any podcast has a right to. Sold a Story, by Emily Hanford at American Public Media.

**Host B:** The first season, in 2022, was about reading. About how a set of popular methods for teaching children to read, especially the idea that children should guess unfamiliar words from pictures and context, the method critics call three cueing, had spread through American schools despite decades of research suggesting it was the wrong way to teach beginners. And the effect was enormous. States passed laws. Publishers rewrote programs. Teachers who had been trained in one way of teaching reading were told, in effect, that they had been sold a story.

**Host A:** And it is worth saying why maths is harder to tell as a simple story than reading. In reading, there is a code. The letters stand for sounds, and the research on teaching that code directly is very strong. Maths has codes too, the number facts, the procedures. But it also has ideas that are not codes at all. What a fraction is. Why you can not divide by zero. Why the area of a triangle is half the base times the height. And the fight in mathematics has always been about how those two things, the facts and the ideas, relate.

**Host B:** The maths wars.

**Host A:** The maths wars. In 1989 the National Council of Teachers of Mathematics published its Curriculum and Evaluation Standards, which called for more problem solving, more reasoning, more communication, and less emphasis on pencil and paper drill. It was enormously influential. And within a few years there was a backlash. Parents and mathematicians in California formed a group in the mid nineties called Mathematically Correct, and fought, successfully, for more traditional state standards in 1997. Then came the Common Core in 2010, and a wave of viral photographs of homework with strange new methods for subtraction, and parents on social media asking why their children could not just carry the one.

**Host B:** And more recently, California again. In 2023 the state adopted a new mathematics framework after years of fierce public argument, with critics attacking its research claims and its approach to tracking students by ability.

**Host A:** So by the time Sold a Story turned to teaching, there was already a long, angry argument about how to teach mathematics. And the story landed in the middle of it.

**Host B:** And the consequences were real. In the years after the first season, dozens of states passed laws about how reading must be taught. Some of the most popular reading programmes in the country were revised or dropped. A famous reading and writing project at Teachers College, Columbia, was closed. Teachers I know describe it as being told, mid career, that something they believed in and had been trained to do was harmful. Some were relieved. Some were furious. Most were both.

**Host A:** So a podcast with a season about how to teach is not a small thing. It can move legislatures.

**Host A:** And in October 2026, the second season began. It is called What Happened to Teaching. And it goes beyond reading. In the trailer, Hanford describes an approach to teaching that, in her words, has been largely rejected by the education system, despite decades of evidence that it works.

**Host B:** Direct Instruction.

**Host A:** Direct Instruction, with capital letters. Engelmann's method. Which is not the same thing, and this matters, as direct instruction in small letters, or what most researchers now call explicit instruction. Explicit instruction is a broad family. Clear explanations, modelling, worked examples, guided practice, then independent practice. Direct Instruction, capital D, capital I, is a specific set of programmes, with names like DISTAR, Reading Mastery, Connecting Math Concepts. Fully scripted. The teacher reads the words from the page. The children respond in unison on a signal. And nobody moves on until everybody has mastered the step.

**Host B:** It helps to hear what that sounds like. This is not quoted from any programme. It is a short lesson written in the style of one, so you can hear the rhythm.

**Teacher:** Listen. Seven plus one. Everybody, what number comes after seven?

**Children:** Eight.

**Teacher:** Yes. So seven plus one equals?

**Children:** Eight.

**Teacher:** My turn. Seven plus one equals eight. Your turn. Seven plus one equals?

**Children:** Eight.

**Teacher:** Nine plus one. What number comes after nine?

**Children:** Ten.

**Teacher:** So nine plus one equals?

**Children:** Ten.

**Host A:** Hear the pace. A question every few seconds. Every child answering. A rule taught first, the number after, and then applied again and again until it is automatic. If one child hesitates or gets it wrong, the teacher says, my turn, models it, and the whole group does it again. And the programmes were designed by Engelmann and his collaborators so that the sequence of examples leads every child to the same correct generalisation.

**Host B:** It is, in its way, beautiful. Like a drill sergeant crossed with a jazz drummer.

**Host A:** And children in those classrooms are busy, and loud, and very often successful, and they know it.

**Host B:** And the second season tells a story that goes like this. In the 1960s, an outsider discovered a way of teaching that worked spectacularly, especially for poor children. The government tested it in the largest education experiment in American history. It won. And then the education establishment ignored the result, because it did not fit its philosophy.

**Host A:** Which is a powerful story. And this series is our answer to it. Or rather, our attempt to take it seriously enough to answer it properly. Because the approach that the story sets itself against, the one it implies is the villain, is something the research world calls responsive teaching. Teaching that starts from what children are actually thinking. And in mathematics, that tradition has its own decades of evidence, its own famous experiments, its own heroes.

**Host B:** So over the coming episodes we are going to go deep into that tradition. Paper by paper. And we are going to judge both sides by a framework from one of the most clear eyed scholars of teaching in America. Mary Kennedy.

**Host A:** But first, the story itself. Because to answer Sold a Story you have to understand why it is so persuasive. And that means going back to the 1960s.

**Host B:** The Kennedy era.

**Host A:** The Kennedy era, in the other sense. And it begins with a beeping sound from space.

**Host B:** October 1957. The Soviet Union launches Sputnik, the first artificial satellite. And for Americans it is a shock that is hard to recover now. The Russians are ahead. In rockets, which means in science, which means, people conclude, in schools.

**Host A:** And the response, in mathematics, is the New Math. In 1958, with money from the new National Science Foundation, a group called the School Mathematics Study Group is set up, led by a mathematician named Edward Begle. And through the late fifties and into the Kennedy years, school mathematics in America is rewritten. Out go the old drills. In come sets, and bases other than ten, and the commutative property, and the idea that children should understand the structure of mathematics the way a mathematician does.

**Host B:** In 1962 Kennedy tells the country at Rice University that we choose to go to the moon, not because it is easy but because it is hard. And the new mathematics textbooks have the same spirit. Not easy. Rigorous. Structural.

**Host A:** And by the mid sixties, there is a backlash. In 1965 the physicist Richard Feynman, who had served on a textbook selection committee in California, wrote a scathing essay about the new books, full of what he saw as pointless formalism. And the same year, the comedian and mathematician Tom Lehrer recorded a song called New Math, which became famous, mocking a father unable to help his child subtract in base eight.

**Host A:** And in 1973 the mathematician Morris Kline published a book whose title said it all. Why Johnny Can't Add. The Failure of the New Math.

**Host B:** So the first great American reform of mathematics teaching in the modern era was built by mathematicians, for understanding, from the top down. And it collapsed. And parents remember that collapse to this day.

**Host A:** And meanwhile, in the same decade, a different crisis. Not about rockets. About poverty.

**Host B:** In 1964 Lyndon Johnson, who became president after Kennedy was assassinated, declares a War on Poverty. In 1965 comes Head Start, preschool for poor children. And in the same years, a young man from Chicago who had worked in advertising, studying how many times a child needs to see a commercial before remembering it, becomes obsessed with a question. Not what children are ready to learn. But how to teach them so that they learn.

**Host A:** Engelmann. He had no teaching qualification. He had a degree in philosophy. And his experiments with his own twins led him to a partnership with an educational psychologist at the University of Illinois named Carl Bereiter. In 1964 they start a preschool for disadvantaged children, and in 1966 they publish a book, Teaching Disadvantaged Children in the Preschool. And their method is the opposite of everything the progressive tradition of early childhood education believed.

**Host B:** The progressive tradition said, let children play. Let them explore. Learning grows out of interest.

**Host A:** And Bereiter and Engelmann said, poor children are behind, and every day they spend playing, they fall further behind. So teach them, directly, intensively, fast. Small groups. Rapid questions. The children answer together. Correct every error immediately. Praise every success. Move on only when everyone has it.

**Host B:** And it worked, by their measures. The children's test scores rose dramatically. And Engelmann's conclusion, which he would hold for the rest of his life, was this. If a child has not learned, the teacher has not taught. Or rather, the programme has not taught. The fault is never in the child.

**Host A:** Which, put that way, is a deeply egalitarian idea.

**Host B:** It is. And this is the thing that critics of Direct Instruction often forget. Engelmann was not a conservative scold. He was furious on behalf of poor black children in Illinois and later in Oregon, who were being told by kindly educators that they were not ready, that they needed more time, that their language was deficient. He thought that was a polite form of abandonment. And he wanted to prove it.

**Host A:** And he got his chance with Follow Through.

**Host B:** Follow Through. In 1967, Congress set up a programme meant to follow Head Start into the early grades of elementary school. The idea was to keep the gains that preschool produced from fading away. It was supposed to be a large service programme. Here is how the critics who later evaluated the evaluation described what happened next. The reader will give it to us.

**Reader:** Follow Through has been the largest and most expensive federal educational experiment in this country's history.

**Host A:** That is from the first line of a famous critique, which we will come to, by Ernest House, Gene Glass, Leslie McLean and Decker Walker, published in the Harvard Educational Review in 1978. And they go on to explain that the money that was expected never came. An expected appropriation of one hundred and twenty million dollars was cut to fifteen million for the first year.

**Host B:** So the government turned a service programme it could not afford into an experiment. Something called planned variation. Instead of one approach everywhere, different communities would adopt different models, designed by different sponsors, universities and laboratories. And then the government would measure which models worked.

**Host A:** And there were over twenty sponsored models at various points. From the most structured to the most open. And then, in the official evaluation, carried out by a company called Abt Associates and published in 1977, the models were grouped into three broad families.

**Host B:** Basic skills models, which included Engelmann's Direct Instruction model, based by then at the University of Oregon, and a behaviour analysis model from the University of Kansas.

**Host A:** Cognitive conceptual models, which emphasised what we would now call thinking skills and learning to learn. Including a model from the High Scope foundation, based on the ideas of Jean Piaget.

**Host B:** And affective cognitive models, which emphasised children's self esteem and emotional development alongside learning. Including the Bank Street model from New York, and the Open Education model. And, and I want listeners to notice this, one model from the Far West Laboratory in San Francisco that was literally called the Responsive Education model.

**Host A:** The word responsive, in Follow Through, in 1968.

**Host B:** Which is going to come back to haunt this series.

**Host A:** And the results. This is the heart of Sold a Story. Direct Instruction came out on top. Not just in basic skills, like reading words and arithmetic, where you would expect a drill based model to win. But also on the measures of cognitive skills, like reading comprehension and maths problem solving. And on the measures of self esteem, the affective measures, which the progressive models had emphasised.

**Host B:** And it is worth saying what the measures actually were, because the whole fight is about them. Basic skills were measured mostly with a standard achievement battery, the Metropolitan Achievement Test, covering things like word knowledge, spelling and maths computation. Cognitive skills were measured with the harder parts of that test, reading comprehension and maths problem solving, and with a nonverbal reasoning test called Raven's Coloured Progressive Matrices, those puzzles where you find the missing piece in a pattern. And the affective measures were questionnaires, the Coopersmith Self Esteem Inventory, and a scale measuring whether children believed their success and failure were caused by their own efforts or by luck.

**Host A:** So the affective measures were trying to capture something like confidence and a sense of agency. And Direct Instruction children did better on those too.

**Host B:** Which Engelmann loved to point out. The progressive models said, we will build self esteem first, and learning will follow. Engelmann said, no, success builds self esteem. Teach them to read and they will feel good about themselves. And on those measures, he looked right.

**Host A:** The models that focused on cognitive development and on children's feelings did not do better on cognitive development or on children's feelings. The model that focused on basic skills did better on everything.

**Host B:** That is the claim. And Engelmann spent the rest of his life telling the story of what happened next. Which was, essentially, nothing. The government did not tell schools to adopt Direct Instruction. Funding continued for all the models, including the ones that had done worst. And the education world moved on.

**Host A:** Hanford quotes it in the season, in the reporting around it. Powerful people, she says, disputed the findings and sought to discredit them.

**Host B:** And one of those powerful people, if you want to put it that way, was Gene Glass.

**Host A:** Who is not a villain in any ordinary sense. Gene Glass invented the meta analysis. He is one of the most important methodologists of the twentieth century. And in 1978, with Ernest House and two colleagues, he published that critique in the Harvard Educational Review, with a title that is itself an argument. No Simple Answer.

**Host B:** And Educational Leadership published a shorter version for teachers the same year. Let me read the heart of it.

**Reader:** The effectiveness of a teaching approach varies greatly from one school district to the next.

**Host A:** That is the central finding of the critique. When House and Glass looked at the data, they found that the variation in results between different sites using the same model was larger than the variation between models. Direct Instruction worked brilliantly in some towns and much less well in others. So did everything else. They put it bluntly.

**Reader:** Models that worked well in one town worked poorly in another.

**Host B:** Their second complaint was about the measures. The main achievement test was a standard test of basic skills, and they argued that the evaluation measured very few of the goals the sponsors of the other models had actually set out to achieve. If your model aims to produce curious, confident, self directed learners, and you test children on spelling and computation, you should not be surprised if the spelling model wins.

**Host A:** And their third complaint was about the categories themselves. They called the classification scheme, in their words, vague and ill defined. Models labelled cognitive taught reading and maths about as much as models labelled basic skills.

**Host B:** And their conclusion?

**Reader:** No approach was demonstrated to be better than the others.

**Host A:** No simple answer. And that, for Engelmann and his supporters, was the moment the establishment closed ranks. The experiment had a result, and the critics argued it away.

**Host B:** So who is right?

**Host A:** I think this is where it gets genuinely interesting, and where a story that wants heroes and villains starts to creak. Because both sides had a point. The defenders of Direct Instruction were right that, on the measures used, their model did best, and that the cognitive and affective models did not deliver their own promises. And there were later reanalyses. In the early 1980s, Carl Bereiter, who had by then moved on from Direct Instruction himself, and a colleague named Midian Kurland, reanalysed the data and argued that the structured models really did come out ahead.

**Host B:** And there were follow up studies. Wesley Becker, one of Engelmann's closest collaborators, and Russell Gersten published a study in 1982 following Direct Instruction students into the fifth and sixth grades. And they reported that, compared with similar children, the Direct Instruction students kept an advantage in several areas, though the gap with national averages tended to reopen once the programme stopped.

**Host A:** And then in 2018, the journal Review of Educational Research published a large meta analysis. Jean Stockard, Timothy Wood, Cristy Coughlin and Caitlin Rasplica Khoury, from Oregon, gathered half a century of research on Direct Instruction curricula, from 1966 to 2016. Three hundred and twenty eight studies, almost four thousand effects. And they found that all the estimated effects were positive. Reading, maths, language, spelling. The effects did not fade much over time. They were larger the more students were exposed to the programmes. And the authors argued the effects were large enough to be educationally significant.

**Host B:** Now, the authors were associated with the Direct Instruction world, and critics have pointed that out, and some of the studies were small or old. But it is a serious piece of work in a serious journal. You cannot wave it away.

**Host A:** There is one more study that critics of Direct Instruction always mention, and Education Week mentioned it in its explainer before the season. In 1986, researchers from the High Scope foundation, Lawrence Schweinhart, David Weikart and Mary Larner, published a follow up of a small preschool experiment in Michigan that had compared three approaches, including a Direct Instruction preschool and High Scope's own Piaget inspired curriculum. By age fifteen, they reported, the young people who had been in the Direct Instruction preschool reported more delinquent behaviour than those in the other groups. And later follow ups claimed the differences persisted into adulthood.

**Host B:** That is a serious claim.

**Host A:** It is, and it is also a small study, run by a team with its own model in the race. So the Direct Instruction world dismissed it as rivals grading their competitor's homework. And the High Scope world held it up as evidence that drilling three year olds has costs that achievement tests do not see. I do not think it settles anything. But it shows you that each side has its favourite study, and each side has a reason to distrust the other's.

**Host B:** And then there is the equity argument.

**Host A:** Which cuts both ways. Direct Instruction has mostly been used in schools serving poor children and children of colour. Its defenders say that is exactly the point. Those children deserve the most effective teaching, and they should not be the ones experimented on with fashionable methods. Its critics say that is exactly the problem. Rich children get rich, open, creative classrooms, and poor children get scripts. And there is a real argument there about who gets to think in school. I do not think either side can wish the other's point away.

**Host A:** So the defenders of the story have real evidence. And the critics of Follow Through had real objections. And here is the thing I want to put on the table now, because it will run through every episode of this series. House and Glass's main finding, that results varied more between sites than between models, is not just a statistical complaint. It is a finding about teaching.

**Host B:** Say more.

**Host A:** It says that what happened in a classroom depended less on the label of the method, and more on what actually went on in that town, that school, with those teachers and those children. And that is exactly what the responsive teaching tradition is about. And it is exactly what Mary Kennedy spent her career trying to explain.

**Host B:** So let us introduce Kennedy, briefly, because she gets her own episode next time.

**Host A:** Mary Kennedy was for many years a professor at Michigan State University, one of the great centres of research on teacher education. And she is famous for a book from 2005 with a provocative title. Inside Teaching. How Classroom Life Undermines Reform.

**Host B:** She and her team observed nearly five hundred teaching episodes by forty five elementary teachers, all of them involved in some kind of reform. And then they talked with the teachers about what they had been trying to do in each moment.

**Host A:** And what she found was that reforms failed not because teachers were lazy, or ignorant, or against the reform. Teachers were juggling. They supported intellectual engagement in principle. But in practice they worried about keeping the lesson moving, what Kennedy called lesson momentum. They needed, in one teacher's words, to always remain calm and in control. Open ended questions were risky, because unexpected answers were hard to manage.

**Host B:** There is a lovely, painful example in the book of a hands on writing lesson. The procedure the teacher used was so elaborate that, in Kennedy's words, everyone's attention was diverted from the content to the procedure. The children were busy following the activity, and the thing the activity was supposed to teach got lost.

**Host A:** Which is, by the way, exactly the criticism that explicit instruction advocates make of a lot of inquiry teaching. That the activity eats the learning.

**Host B:** And Kennedy was not anti reform. That is what makes her interesting. She was documenting why good ideas die in classrooms. And she found that teachers were already working about as hard as anyone could reasonably expect. Reformers kept asking them to work harder. That was not the problem.

**Host B:** And she has a sentence in that book that I think every policymaker should have above their desk.

**Reader:** The kind of teaching that reformers seek may entail more time and energy than teachers actually have.

**Host A:** And then, in 2016, she published an article in the Journal of Teacher Education called Parsing the Practice of Teaching. And she argued that teaching is not a list of techniques. It is a practice organised around a set of persistent problems. Problems that every teacher, in every classroom, in every method, has to solve, all the time, at the same time. She named five.

**Host B:** The first is portraying the curriculum. Making the content visible and comprehensible to children.

**Host A:** The second is enlisting student participation. Getting children to actually take part, to want to, to try.

**Host B:** The third is exposing student thinking. Finding out what is actually going on in their heads.

**Host A:** The fourth is containing student behaviour. Keeping order, so that learning is possible.

**Host B:** And the fifth is accommodating the teacher's own personal needs. The teacher is a person too, with a limited amount of energy and patience and a need to feel competent.

**Host A:** And her argument is that any approach to teaching, any method, any programme, should be judged by how well it helps teachers solve all five. Not one of them. All five, together, because they compete.

**Host B:** So let us do that, now, with Direct Instruction. As a warm up for the series.

**Host A:** Portraying the curriculum. This is where Direct Instruction is extraordinary. Engelmann's great insight, which he and Douglas Carnine developed into a whole theory of instruction, was about the design of examples. How to sequence them so that a child cannot draw the wrong conclusion. If you want to teach the concept of red, you show things that are red and things that are almost red but not, so that the boundary is clear. The programme does the portraying for the teacher, and does it with enormous care.

**Host B:** Enlisting participation. Again strong, in its way. Every child answers, many times a minute, in unison on a signal. Nobody can hide. And Engelmann was proud that children in Direct Instruction classrooms were busy and successful, and he argued that success itself is motivating.

**Host A:** Containing behaviour. Very strong. The pace and the structure leave little room for chaos.

**Host B:** Accommodating the teacher's needs. Here it gets interesting. For a new teacher, or a struggling one, or one in a chaotic school, a script can be a lifeline. You know what to say. You know what to do next. Some teachers love it for exactly that. And others feel it treats them as a reading machine and leaves no room for their own judgement. Kennedy, in a 2016 interview about her article, said that reforms all try to prescribe what teachers should be doing, and that development should instead help teachers get better at figuring out what to do for themselves.

**Host A:** And the third problem. Exposing student thinking.

**Host B:** That is the one where the two traditions part company.

**Host A:** Direct Instruction exposes student thinking in one sense. It hears every answer, constantly, and catches every error, instantly. If a child says the wrong thing, the teacher knows at once and corrects it. But it is designed to expose whether the child has the right answer, not how the child is thinking. And in mathematics, that is the whole question.

**Host B:** Because two children can give the same wrong answer for completely different reasons.

**Host A:** Or the same right answer. One child says that seven and eight is fifteen because she has memorised it. Three completely different mathematical minds. And what each needs next is completely different.

**Host B:** And that is the starting point of the responsive tradition in mathematics. The claim that the most important thing a mathematics teacher can know is not the right answer, which the teacher already knows, but the child's thinking, which she does not.

**Host A:** Let us be fair to Engelmann again, though, because he would object. He would say his programmes were designed so that the child's thinking would not go wrong in the first place. Faultless communication, he called it. If the examples are designed well enough, there is only one sensible interpretation, and the child will reach it. You do not need to diagnose a misconception you have prevented.

**Host B:** It is an astonishing ambition. To design teaching so precisely that misunderstanding becomes impossible.

**Host A:** And the responsive tradition would say, children are not that predictable. They will always construct something you did not expect. Better to learn to see it than to pretend you can prevent it.

**Host A:** Cognitively Guided Instruction. Elizabeth Fennema, Thomas Carpenter, Penelope Peterson and colleagues, at the University of Wisconsin. Forty first grade teachers, randomly assigned.
