# Responsive Math Teaching 2. Inside Teaching

**Host A:** In the late 1980s, a researcher from Michigan State University named David Cohen spent time in the second grade classroom of a teacher in California. He called her Mrs. Oublier. Which is French for to forget.

**Host B:** That is a pointed name.

**Host A:** It is. California had just adopted a new mathematics framework, in 1985, one of the most ambitious reforms in the country. It called for teaching for understanding. Children reasoning, explaining, making sense. And Mrs. Oublier embraced it. She told Cohen that her teaching had been transformed. She used the new materials, she had children working with blocks and counters, she talked about understanding.

**Host B:** And?

**Host A:** And when Cohen watched, what he saw was a lesson organised almost exactly like the lessons she had always taught. The blocks were there. But the children used them step by step, on instruction, to get the answer the teacher expected. The questions had one right answer. The talk was the teacher's. The new materials were poured into the old structure. Cohen published the study in 1990 under the title A Revolution in One Classroom. And the revolution was real to her. She believed it had happened.

**Host B:** She was not lying.

**Host A:** Not at all. That is what makes it devastating. She had changed the surface of her practice and not its structure, and she did not know. And that story became a kind of parable in American education research. Reforms do not fail because teachers resist them. They fail because teachers absorb them into what they already do.

**Host B:** And that is where Mary Kennedy comes in.

**Host A:** That is the exact question Kennedy spent her career on. Not, which method is best. But, what is actually going on when a teacher teaches, and why do good ideas from outside the classroom so often turn into something else inside it.

**Host B:** Last time we said this series would use Kennedy as a lens to judge both Direct Instruction, the hero of Sold a Story's second season, and responsive mathematics teaching, the tradition we are defending, or at least explaining. So today we go deep into Kennedy herself.

**Host A:** Four pieces of work. A problem she named in 1999. A study published in 2004 and turned into a book in 2005. An article from 2016 that reframed what teaching is. And a review of professional development from the same year.

**Host B:** Start with the problem.

**Host A:** The problem of enactment. Kennedy introduced the phrase in a chapter in 1999, about the role of teacher education. And the idea is simple and has haunted teacher education ever since. Teachers can learn something, believe it, be able to explain it, and still not be able to do it in a classroom. Knowing about a practice and enacting it are different things.

**Host B:** Mrs. Oublier.

**Host A:** Mrs. Oublier is the problem of enactment in a single person. And Kennedy connected it to an older idea, from the sociologist Dan Lortie, who in 1975 wrote a classic book called Schoolteacher. Lortie pointed out that by the time anyone becomes a teacher, they have spent something like thirteen thousand hours watching teachers, as students. He called it the apprenticeship of observation.

**Host B:** Thirteen thousand hours.

**Host A:** Thirteen thousand hours of seeing teaching from the wrong side of the desk. Seeing the teacher's actions, but not her reasons. And so new teachers arrive with a deep, unexamined picture of what teaching looks like, absorbed over twelve years. And a few months of teacher education, however brilliant, struggles against it. When the pressure is on, in the classroom, the old picture comes back.

**Host B:** Which matters enormously for responsive teaching, because almost nobody was taught that way as a child.

**Host A:** Exactly. Most adults in America learned mathematics by watching a teacher demonstrate a procedure and then practising it. So that is the picture in their heads. A teacher who wants to teach responsively is asking herself to do something she has almost never seen. That is a very different situation from a teacher learning Direct Instruction, which, whatever else you think of it, comes with every word written down.

**Host B:** So enactment is easier with a script.

**Host A:** Enactment of the script is easier with a script. Whether the script enacts what the child needs is another question. Hold that.

**Host B:** Now the big study.

**Host A:** Kennedy wanted to understand why reforms of the 1990s, which were broadly about teaching for understanding, kept not changing practice. And she noticed that there were three common explanations. Teachers lack the knowledge to do it. Teachers lack the will. Or teachers have other agendas that compete with reform. And most research had looked at the first two. She decided to look at the third. What are teachers actually trying to do, moment to moment, and how does that fit with what reformers want?

**Host B:** How did she study it?

**Host A:** Carefully. She and her team recruited forty five upper elementary teachers, in clusters across different reform settings. Some in Vermont, where there was a portfolio assessment reform. Some in a California science project. Some in Michigan professional development schools. Some going through National Board certification. All of them, in other words, involved in reform. Each teacher chose a lesson, and it was videotaped. Then the teacher and the researcher each chose episodes from the video to talk about. And in interviews that lasted up to two hours, they went through them, one by one. What was going on here? Why did you respond that way? Have you always done this? What made you change?

**Host B:** How many episodes?

**Host A:** Four hundred and ninety nine episodes. And from them, nine hundred and thirty seven separate intentions. Reasons teachers gave for what they did. It was published in 2004 in the journal Education Policy Analysis Archives, under the title Reform Ideals and Teachers' Practical Intentions, and then expanded into the 2005 book.

**Host B:** And what were teachers trying to do?

**Host A:** Kennedy sorted the intentions into six big areas of concern. And I am going to give you the rough shares, out of those nine hundred and thirty seven. The biggest group, about a quarter, was fostering student learning. Monitoring progress, modelling thinking, adapting the content. That is the one reformers would hope for.

**Host B:** Good.

**Host A:** Close behind, about a fifth, was maintaining lesson momentum. Keeping the lesson moving. Avoiding distractions. Keeping everyone on the same page.

**Host B:** Momentum.

**Host A:** Then, nearly a fifth, students' willingness to participate. Keeping them focused, affirming them, protecting their self esteem. Then, about one in seven, content coverage. Getting through what the next teacher expects, what the state test will ask. Then about one in eight, the classroom as a community. The teacher's persona, fairness, norms for how children treat each other. And about one in nine, personal needs. The teacher's own needs. Reducing her own strain. Her need for order.

**Host B:** So only a quarter of what teachers were trying to do, moment to moment, was directly about learning.

**Host A:** Directly. Which is not a criticism of teachers. It is a description of the job. A classroom is a place where twenty five children have to be kept together, kept willing, kept moving, kept safe, and taught, all at once, by one adult with finite patience.

**Host B:** And how did that match the reform ideals?

**Host A:** That is the punchline. Kennedy identified three ideals that ran through the reforms of the time. Important content. Intellectual engagement. And universal access to knowledge. And she found that the teachers' intentions touched all three, but in a transformed way. Here is what she wrote about the first.

**Reader:** No teacher indicated a specific intention to ensure that the ideas they taught were inherently important.

**Host A:** Content mattered to teachers, but because it fitted a larger system. Because it was in the curriculum guide, because the next teacher expected it, because it would be tested. Not because the idea itself was powerful. And on the second ideal she was even more blunt.

**Reader:** No teacher specifically mentioned intellectual engagement as an important intention.

**Host B:** None?

**Host A:** None. What teachers did talk about was participation. Willingness. Getting children to take part. Which is not the same as intellectual engagement. A child can participate cheerfully in a fun activity without thinking hard about anything. And universal access, the third ideal, became universal participation. Make sure everyone joins in. Rather than make sure everyone gets access to powerful ideas.

**Host B:** So each reform ideal was translated into something that made sense inside the logic of the classroom.

**Host A:** Exactly. And she has a line that sums up the gap.

**Reader:** Teachers' intentions cover a much wider swath than reform ideals cover.

**Host B:** That is the whole story, really. Reformers ask for three things. Teachers are juggling six.

**Host A:** And she shows the trading in individual cases. In one, a teacher she calls Buford faced a choice between pressing a line of genuinely demanding thinking and keeping every child in the class with her. And she chose to keep everyone with her. Intellectual engagement was traded for universal participation. And Kennedy does not sneer at that. You can see why a teacher would make it. A brilliant discussion that loses half the room is not obviously better than a modest one that keeps everyone.

**Host B:** That is the kind of trade that never shows up in a policy document.

**Host A:** Never. Policy documents speak as if the ideals all point the same way. Inside the classroom, they pull against each other, every few minutes. And she also noticed how teachers' reasons for content were almost always external. She wrote that teachers seemed very aware that their instruction fitted into a larger system. Next year's teacher. The test. The curriculum guide. So when a reform said, teach fewer topics in more depth, teachers heard, fall behind the system. And when a reform said, teach this big idea because it is powerful, the teacher's reasoning was, but is it on the test, and will the fourth grade teacher expect it?

**Host B:** Which means that a lot of what looks like resistance to reform is actually loyalty to colleagues.

**Host A:** Loyalty to the next teacher. Yes. That is a very humane reading, and I think a true one.

**Host A:** And there are two findings in that paper that I think are dynamite for our debate. The first. She found that the single most common specific intention was that teachers wanted to respond positively to students. And then this.

**Reader:** Almost to a person, teachers abhorred the idea of telling a child that he or she was wrong.

**Host B:** Wow.

**Host A:** Think about that alongside Direct Instruction, which is built on immediate correction. My turn, your turn. And alongside responsive teaching, which is built on asking children to explain their thinking, including their wrong thinking. And then look at what most teachers in Kennedy's study actually wanted to do. Avoid saying wrong. Affirm. Keep self esteem intact.

**Host B:** Which explains Mrs. Oublier's blocks, maybe. If you can not bear to tell a child they are wrong, you design lessons where they can not be.

**Host A:** That is a brilliant connection, and I think Kennedy would agree. Questions with one right answer, carefully led, are a way of making sure nobody is ever wrong in public. And that is very far from both Engelmann and from responsive teaching. Engelmann would correct the error at once. The responsive teacher would invite the error into the open and make it the subject of the lesson. And many real teachers want neither. They want to avoid the error altogether.

**Host B:** And the second piece of dynamite?

**Host A:** Disruption. Kennedy wrote this.

**Reader:** Almost all teachers seemed to believe that the potential for a major disruption was always present.

**Host B:** Always present.

**Host A:** The classroom, from the teacher's side, is always one moment from chaos. And so many choices that look, from outside, like failures of nerve or failures of knowledge are actually acts of prevention. You do not ask the open question, because the open question might open the door to the child who will derail everything. You keep the pace up, because a slow moment is when trouble starts.

**Host B:** Lesson momentum again.

**Host A:** And she gives a wonderful example in the book of a reform activity that went wrong. A hands on writing lesson, so procedurally complicated that, in her words, everyone's attention was diverted from the content to the procedure. The children were so busy doing the activity that the thing it was meant to teach got lost.

**Host B:** And that is the famous criticism of a lot of activity based maths too. Children spend forty minutes cutting out paper shapes and learn nothing about area.

**Host A:** The activity eats the learning. Which is precisely the critique the explicit instruction world makes. And Kennedy documented it from inside reform classrooms.

**Host B:** So is Kennedy on Engelmann's side?

**Host A:** That is the fascinating thing. She is on nobody's side. Her conclusion in the book is not that reform was wrong, or that teachers were wrong. It is that reformers had not understood the practice they were trying to change. And there is her sentence that I quoted last time.

**Reader:** The kind of teaching that reformers seek may entail more time and energy than teachers actually have.

**Host B:** And reformers' answer was usually, work harder.

**Host A:** And Kennedy found they were already working about as hard as anyone reasonably could. That was not the variable.

**Host B:** So what is the variable?

**Host A:** That is where the 2016 article comes in. Parsing the Practice of Teaching, in the Journal of Teacher Education. And to understand it you have to know what was happening in teacher education at the time.

**Host B:** Core practices.

**Host A:** Core practices. Around 2009, a group of teacher educators, including Deborah Ball and Francesca Forzani at the University of Michigan, and Pam Grossman and colleagues, argued that teacher education had been too focused on knowledge and beliefs, and not enough on practice. Novice teachers needed to learn to do specific things. Lead a discussion. Elicit and interpret student thinking. Explain a concept. And so there was a movement to identify a set of high leverage practices, or core practices, and to teach them directly to novices, with rehearsals, the way you might train a surgeon.

**Host B:** Which sounds sensible.

**Host A:** It sounds very sensible, and it was a serious intellectual movement, so let me describe it fairly. Pam Grossman and her colleagues had studied how other professions train practitioners, clergy, clinical psychologists, and teachers, and in 2009 they published a framework with three ideas that became famous in teacher education. Representations of practice, meaning ways of showing novices what skilled practice looks like, like video or written cases. Decompositions of practice, breaking a complex practice into parts that can be named and learned. And approximations of practice, opportunities to try parts of the work in simplified settings, like rehearsing a discussion with colleagues playing the students, before doing it with real children.

**Host B:** Like a flight simulator.

**Host A:** Like a flight simulator, or a medical student practising on a mannequin. And at the University of Michigan, Deborah Ball and Francesca Forzani argued in the same year that teacher education should be centred on the work of teaching itself, on what they called high leverage practices. Things like leading a group discussion, eliciting and interpreting individual students' thinking, explaining and modelling content. A group called TeachingWorks later published a list of nineteen of them. And in 2013 Morva McDonald, Elham Kazemi and Sarah Kavanagh described a cycle for teaching core practices to novices. Introduce the practice, prepare and rehearse it, enact it with real students, analyse what happened, and go round again.

**Host B:** That is very close to how you would train a doctor or a musician.

**Host A:** And it was a huge advance on teacher education that consisted mostly of reading theory and then being thrown into a classroom. And some of the best research on responsive mathematics teaching came out of that movement, as we will see in the discussion episode.

**Host B:** So what is Kennedy's worry?

**Host A:** Kennedy, in the 2016 article, looks back at attempts like that, to break teaching down into lists of discrete procedures, and raises a deep problem. Lists of practices tell novices what to do. They do not tell them why. And the why is the whole thing. The abstract puts it this way. Teacher educators should focus less on the observable behaviours of teaching and more on the purposes those behaviours serve.

**Host B:** Purposes over behaviours.

**Host A:** Because the same behaviour can serve different purposes, and the same purpose can be served by different behaviours. A teacher asks a child to come to the board. Why? To expose her thinking to the class? To keep her from being disruptive at her desk? To give her a moment of status? To slow the lesson down? You can not judge the behaviour without knowing the purpose. And a list of behaviours, taught as techniques, may leave novices doing the right things for no reason, which means doing them at the wrong moments.

**Host B:** So she proposes the five persistent problems.

**Host A:** She proposes that we think of teaching as a practice defined by persistent problems, problems that are always present and always compete. She names five. And notice how closely they line up with the six concerns from her own 2004 study. Portraying the curriculum. Enlisting student participation. Exposing student thinking. Containing student behaviour. And accommodating personal needs, meaning the teacher's own.

**Host B:** Lesson momentum and community and coverage have been folded in.

**Host A:** Roughly, yes. And the key idea is that these problems are never solved. They are persistent. You solve them today, and they come back tomorrow, with a new class, a new topic, a new child. And they compete. Almost anything you do to help with one makes another harder. Slow down to expose a child's thinking, and you risk the momentum that keeps behaviour contained. Push hard through the curriculum, and you risk losing participation. And the teacher has to do all of this while also keeping herself sane.

**Host B:** And her proposal for teacher education?

**Host A:** Not to solve the problems for novices. To help them analyse them. The abstract says teacher education should help novices analyse these problems and consider alternative courses of action by how well each addresses them. In the 2016 interview I mentioned last time, she put it very simply. Don't fall in love with a specific teaching technique.

**Host B:** That is quite a challenge to both sides of our debate.

**Host A:** It is a challenge to both. To Direct Instruction, because a script is the purest form of falling in love with a technique. It solves the problems for the teacher, and leaves no room for her to analyse anything. And to responsive teaching, if it turns into its own set of techniques. Turn and talk. Number talks. Five practices. Whatever. If novices learn those as moves, without the purposes, they become Mrs. Oublier, with new materials poured into old structures.

**Host B:** So Kennedy's lens does not tell you which method to choose. It tells you what any method has to do.

**Host A:** Exactly. And that is why it is the right lens for this series. Let me lay out how we will use it. For each paper we read, from Wisconsin in the 1980s to the most recent work on noticing and discussion, we will ask five questions. How does this approach help the teacher portray the content? How does it enlist participation? How does it expose student thinking? How does it contain behaviour? And what does it ask of the teacher as a person?

**Host B:** And for Direct Instruction we did that briefly last time.

**Host A:** And it scores very high on portrayal, participation in its own sense, and containment. Very high for some teachers' needs, low for others'. And on exposing thinking, it exposes answers rather than reasoning.

**Host B:** And the bet of responsive mathematics teaching is that exposing student thinking is the problem that, solved well, helps solve the others.

**Host A:** That is the bet, and I want to state it carefully, because it is a big claim. The claim is that if a teacher understands how a child is thinking about a piece of mathematics, then she portrays the content better, because she knows which representation will connect. She enlists participation better, because children participate more when their own ideas are taken seriously. And even behaviour can improve, because children who are thinking about their own ideas are less likely to be bored. That is the bet.

**Host B:** And the risk?

**Host A:** The risk is Kennedy's other finding. That exposing thinking slows everything down, threatens momentum, opens the door to disruption, and asks more of the teacher than she has. So responsive teaching has to show not just that it is right about learning, but that it can be lived by real teachers, in real classrooms, with all five problems at once.

**Host B:** Can we make the five problems concrete? Take one short moment of mathematics teaching and look at it through all five.

**Host A:** Let us. Here is a second grade class doing what many American schools now call a number talk. A short daily routine where the teacher writes a calculation on the board and children work it out in their heads and then share their strategies. This is written for the episode, not quoted.

**Teacher:** Here is today's problem. Twenty nine plus thirty six. Work it out in your head. Thumbs up when you have an answer. Do not call out. Mia, what did you get?

**Child:** Sixty five.

**Teacher:** How did you think about it?

**Child:** I took one from the thirty six and gave it to the twenty nine, so it was thirty plus thirty five. That is sixty five.

**Teacher:** Did anyone think about it a different way? Jayden?

**Child:** I did twenty plus thirty is fifty, and nine plus six is fifteen, so sixty five.

**Teacher:** Two different ways, same answer. Who can explain what Mia did with the one?

**Host A:** Now look at it through Kennedy. Portraying the curriculum. The content here is not just the answer. It is the idea that numbers can be taken apart and recombined, which is the foundation of place value and of algebra. The teacher portrays it through the children's own strategies, rather than by explaining it herself.

**Host B:** Enlisting participation. The thumbs up is clever. Every child can signal without the fast ones shouting out. And asking who can explain Mia's way brings more children in.

**Host A:** Exposing thinking. The whole routine exists for that. How did you think about it.

**Host B:** Containing behaviour. Do not call out. Thumbs up. A tight structure, five or ten minutes, everyone on the carpet.

**Host A:** And the teacher's needs. Routines like this are predictable. The teacher knows how the ten minutes will go, even if she does not know what the children will say. Which is a big part of why number talks spread so fast. They give the teacher a safe container for unpredictable thinking.

**Host B:** Now do the same moment in Direct Instruction.

**Host A:** The problem would probably not be posed that way at all. The programme would teach one efficient strategy, say adding the tens and then the ones, step by step, with examples carefully chosen so that every child arrives at the same method. Portrayal, very clear. Participation, every child answering on signal. Behaviour, tightly contained. Teacher's needs, the words are written. Thinking exposed, every answer checked. But Mia's idea, giving one to make thirty, would never be heard. And whether that matters is the whole debate.

**Host B:** What would Engelmann say?

**Host A:** He would say Mia's idea is lovely, and that the children who did not have it, who were sitting there confused while Mia and Jayden explained, needed to be taught a reliable method, not watch clever classmates. That the number talk serves the strong and leaves the weak behind.

**Host B:** And the responsive tradition would say?

**Host A:** That if the teacher is skilled, the children who are confused are the ones she most wants to hear from, and that a strategy a child understands is more reliable than one she has been drilled in. And that Mia's idea, compensation, making a friendly number, is exactly the kind of flexibility that drill alone does not produce.

**Host B:** Both of which are empirical claims.

**Host A:** Both empirical, and both testable, and the evidence is what the next five episodes are about. But Kennedy's lens lets us see that the argument is not, explicit versus discovery. It is about which of the five problems each approach chooses to solve for the teacher, and which it leaves to her.

**Host B:** Let us do the fourth piece of Kennedy's work, because it is the one that speaks most directly to Sold a Story. The professional development review.

**Host A:** Published in 2016 in the Review of Educational Research, under the title How Does Professional Development Improve Teaching? And what makes it unusual is that Kennedy was very strict about which studies to include. She wanted studies that measured the effect of teacher professional development on student achievement, with proper comparison groups. And that left her with twenty eight.

**Host B:** Out of how many programmes in the world?

**Host A:** Out of thousands of programmes, twenty eight good studies. That alone is a sobering fact about the field.

**Host B:** And she did not just ask whether they worked.

**Host A:** She asked how they tried to work. She asked what each programme was trying to change. Many were about portraying content, giving teachers better knowledge of mathematics or science or reading. Some were about containing behaviour. Some about enlisting participation. Some about exposing student thinking. And then she asked how each programme tried to get teachers to enact what they learned. And she found four broad approaches.

**Host B:** Go through them.

**Host A:** Prescription. The programme shows teachers the best way to handle a problem, with little room for judgement. Eight of the studies were like that. Strategies. The programme defines goals and offers illustrative practices for reaching them, but leaves the teacher to adapt. Ten studies. Insight. The programme asks provocative questions, shows teachers familiar events in new ways, so that they see their own classrooms differently. Seven studies. And body of knowledge. The programme presents an organised body of concepts and leaves it almost entirely to teachers whether and how to use it. Three studies.

**Host B:** And which worked best?

**Host A:** The pattern was what she described as an inverted U. Strategies and insight did best. The two extremes, prescription at one end and pure body of knowledge at the other, did less well. And of the extremes, prescription did worst.

**Host B:** Prescription did worst.

**Host A:** For the programmes about portraying content, which was the largest group, strategy had the highest effects, then insight, then body of knowledge, then prescription. And several other findings matter. Programmes that ran for more than a year, with follow up, did better than one year programmes without. And programmes where teachers were assigned, rather than volunteering, had lower effects. For the content programmes overall, the average effect on student achievement was about a tenth of a standard deviation, rising to about a sixth when the mandatory programmes were taken out.

**Host B:** Those are small numbers.

**Host A:** They are small, and Kennedy says so. Professional development does not work miracles. And coaching, which is enormously popular, was mixed. Prescriptive coaching, where the coach tells you what to do, had lower effects than coaching that worked through strategies and insight.

**Host B:** So how do we read that against Direct Instruction?

**Host A:** Very carefully, because they are different questions. The Direct Instruction meta analysis, by Stockard and colleagues, which we discussed last time, asks whether children taught with Direct Instruction programmes learn more than children taught otherwise. It says yes, with moderate to large effects. Kennedy's review asks how to help teachers get better at teaching. And it says prescription is the weakest way.

**Host B:** So a programme can be a powerful teaching tool and a weak teacher development tool.

**Host A:** Exactly. A script can teach children and leave teachers unchanged. Which might be fine, if the script is always there. And might be a problem if the teacher ever has to teach something the script does not cover, or a child the script did not anticipate.

**Host B:** Which is every day.

**Host A:** Which, Kennedy would say, is every day. Because the persistent problems do not go away when you buy a programme. They show up in the moments between the lines of the script.

**Host B:** Can we use Kennedy to answer the central question of Sold a Story's second season? Why did schools not adopt Direct Instruction after Follow Through?

**Host A:** Hanford's answer, and Engelmann's, is ideology. Powerful people did not like the result. And there is a famous comment from the late Robert Slavin, one of the great researchers on what works in schools, quoted by Education Week before the season, that schools often reject Direct Instruction because it does not fit their philosophy, regardless of the research.

**Host B:** And Kennedy's answer would be?

**Host A:** I think Kennedy gives us a second answer that does not cancel the first but deepens it. Look at the 2004 findings again. The most common specific intention teachers had was to respond positively to students. Almost to a person, they abhorred telling a child she was wrong. They cared intensely about their own persona, about the classroom as a community, about fairness and warmth. Now look at a Direct Instruction lesson. Immediate correction. Choral responses. A script that speaks in someone else's voice. Pace set by the programme, not the teacher.

**Host B:** So Direct Instruction collides with what teachers are trying to be.

**Host A:** With what many teachers are trying to be, as people, in front of children. Not just with a philosophy learned in college, but with the daily intentions Kennedy found in nearly five hundred episodes. That is not ideology in the conspiratorial sense. It is the texture of the job as teachers experience it. And it means that even if every policymaker in 1977 had said, adopt Direct Instruction, many teachers would have adopted it the way Mrs. Oublier adopted the California framework. They would have softened the corrections, slowed the pace, added warmth, and told themselves they were doing it.

**Host B:** So the problem of enactment cuts both ways.

**Host A:** Both ways. Responsive teaching dies in classrooms because it asks teachers to expose thinking in ways that threaten momentum and control. Direct Instruction dies in classrooms because it asks teachers to correct, to script, to give up their persona. Different collisions with the same persistent problems.

**Host B:** That is a much more interesting answer than either side usually gives.

**Host A:** It is Kennedy's gift. She makes you look at the teacher, not the method.

**Host B:** And it suggests that whatever wins the argument, the next battle is enactment.

**Host A:** Always enactment. The science of math laws passing in states now will meet the same teachers with the same intentions. If the laws prescribe, Kennedy's review suggests the effects on teaching will be modest. If they come with strategies and insight, sustained over years, they have a better chance. Whichever side they favour.

**Host B:** A word on Kennedy herself, the person, because we have been treating her as a lens.

**Host A:** She spent most of her career at Michigan State, and in the 1990s she led a national research centre there on teacher learning. She was never a classroom romantic. Her early work was in evaluation and research methods, and you can feel that in everything she writes. She counts things. She is suspicious of slogans. And she is unusual among education researchers in taking teachers' own reasons as data, not as excuses.

**Host B:** That is a rare combination.

**Host A:** Rare, and exactly what a debate like this needs. Because both Sold a Story and its critics tend to talk about teachers as either victims or obstacles. Kennedy talks about them as people solving hard problems, with reasons.

**Host B:** Let me push on this, because a Direct Instruction advocate would say, you are being sentimental about teacher judgement. Teacher judgement gave us three cueing in reading. Teacher judgement gave us Mrs. Oublier. Why trust it?

**Host A:** That is a fair challenge, and I think Kennedy would say two things. First, she is not saying trust teacher judgement as it is. She is saying, develop it. Insight and strategies are not, do what you like. They are disciplined ways of seeing and acting, with clear purposes. And second, she would say that judgement is not optional. Even the most scripted teacher has to decide what to do when the child does not respond as the script expects, when the fire alarm goes, when a child cries. The question is not whether teachers use judgement. It is whether their judgement is educated.

**Host B:** Educated judgement.

**Host A:** And that phrase, I think, is the bridge to the responsive tradition. Because the next episode is about a group of researchers in Wisconsin who decided that the best way to educate teachers' judgement in mathematics was not to give them a programme, and not to give them a philosophy, but to give them very specific, research based knowledge about how children think. And then to trust them.

**Host B:** Insight and strategies, in Kennedy's terms.

**Host A:** Insight and strategies. And it was tested in a randomised experiment, in 1986 and 1987, in first grade classrooms. And it is, in many ways, the responsive tradition's answer to Follow Through.

**Host B:** Before we go there, let me try to state Kennedy's whole contribution in a few sentences. Because I think it changes how you hear Sold a Story.

**Host A:** Go.

**Host B:** Teaching is not a method. It is a practice organised around persistent problems that compete for the teacher's attention. Any method, Direct Instruction or responsive teaching or anything else, solves some of those problems for the teacher and leaves others for her to handle. Reforms fail when they ignore the problems they leave behind. And the best way to help teachers is not to prescribe, but to give them strategies and insight that help them see and act on all five problems at once.

**Host A:** That is very good. And I would add one thing. Kennedy's 2004 finding that almost no teacher mentioned intellectual engagement as a goal is, for me, the most important sentence for our debate. Because it means that the real enemy of good mathematics teaching is not Direct Instruction, and it is not responsive teaching. It is the classroom's natural tendency to replace thinking with participation. To keep children busy, cheerful and moving, and to call it learning.

**Host B:** And both traditions, at their best, are fighting that.
