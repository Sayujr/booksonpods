# Responsive Math Teaching 3. Children's Mathematics

**Host A:** Here is a problem. Connie has five marbles. How many more marbles does she need to have thirteen marbles altogether?

**Host B:** Eight.

**Host A:** Eight. You did that instantly, because you know that thirteen minus five is eight, or that five and eight make thirteen. Now give that problem to a five year old who has never been taught subtraction.

**Host B:** She can not do it.

**Host A:** That is what almost everyone assumed in the 1970s. Subtraction has not been taught. So the child can not subtract. But a group of researchers at the University of Wisconsin in Madison actually tried it. Hundreds of times, with hundreds of children. And what they found was that many young children, before any formal teaching, can solve that problem. And the way they solve it is beautiful.

**Host B:** Go on.

**Host A:** The child takes five counters. Then she adds more counters, one at a time, counting as she goes. Six, seven, eight, nine, ten, eleven, twelve, thirteen. And then she looks at the counters she added and counts them. Eight. She has modelled the story. Connie has five, she gets some more, she ends up with thirteen. How many did she get? The child acts it out, exactly as the story describes it.

**Host B:** She never subtracted.

**Host A:** She never subtracted. She joined. Because the problem is a joining story with the change unknown. And that tiny observation, repeated with thousands of children and dozens of problem types, became the foundation of one of the most important research programmes in the history of mathematics education. Cognitively Guided Instruction. And it is the responsive tradition's answer to Follow Through.

**Host B:** Today we go deep into it. Three papers and a book. The children's research that came first. The 1989 experiment in the American Educational Research Journal. The 1996 four year longitudinal study in the Journal for Research in Mathematics Education. And the 2001 follow up of the teachers years later. And we will look at them through Mary Kennedy's lens from last time.

**Host A:** Start with the children. Because Cognitively Guided Instruction was built from the bottom up, from children's thinking, before anyone thought about teachers.

**Host B:** In the late 1970s and early 1980s, Thomas Carpenter and James Moser at Wisconsin ran a longitudinal study. They followed a cohort of young children from first through third grade, interviewing them several times a year, individually, giving them word problems and watching exactly how they solved them. They published the core findings in 1984 in the Journal for Research in Mathematics Education. And together with work by other researchers around the world in the same years, they built a map.

**Host A:** A map with two dimensions. The first is the problem. And the key insight is that addition and subtraction are not two operations. They are many different kinds of situations, which children experience very differently.

**Host B:** Let us do the map, because it is the heart of everything.

**Host A:** There are joining problems. Something is added to something. And there are three versions, depending on which number you do not know. Result unknown. Connie had five marbles, Juan gave her eight more, how many does she have now. Change unknown, the one we started with. Connie has five, how many more does she need to have thirteen. And start unknown. Connie had some marbles, Juan gave her five more, now she has thirteen, how many did she have to start with.

**Host B:** And those three are wildly different in difficulty for a child.

**Host A:** Result unknown is the easiest. A young child can act it out directly. Five counters, eight counters, count them all. Change unknown is harder, but a child can still act it out, as we heard. Start unknown is very hard, because how do you act out a story when you do not know how many you started with?

**Host B:** Then separating.

**Host A:** Separating problems, where something is taken away, and again three versions. Result unknown, change unknown, start unknown. Then part part whole problems, where nothing happens, there is just a set made of two parts. Five boys and eight girls, how many children. And compare problems, where two sets are compared. Connie has thirteen marbles, Juan has five, how many more does Connie have than Juan.

**Host B:** And compare problems are famously hard.

**Host A:** Because there is no action to model. Nothing is joined or taken away. The child has to line the two sets up and see the difference. And that is a different kind of thinking.

**Host B:** So the first dimension is the problem type. What is the second?

**Host A:** The child's strategy. And the Wisconsin researchers found that children's strategies develop in a remarkably consistent sequence. First, direct modelling. The child uses objects or fingers to act out the story exactly. For result unknown, join the two sets and count all. For change unknown, add on until you reach the total and count what you added. For separating, take away and count what is left.

**Host A:** Then counting strategies. The child realises she does not need to make the first set. She can just start counting from it. Five, six, seven, up to thirteen, keeping track on her fingers of how many she counted. Counting on. And later, counting on from the larger number. Eight, nine, ten, eleven, twelve, thirteen, instead of starting from five. Which is a quiet but profound discovery, that order does not matter in addition.

**Host B:** Commutativity, discovered by a six year old.

**Host A:** Then derived facts. The child uses facts she knows to work out ones she does not. Five and five is ten, so five and six is eleven. Seven and seven is fourteen, so seven and eight is fifteen. And finally, recalled facts. She just knows.

**Host B:** And the claim is that this sequence is natural.

**Host A:** The claim is that it is common and robust. Children do not need to be taught direct modelling. They invent it. And they move through counting to derived facts, at different speeds, often using different strategies for different problem types at the same time. A child might count on for a result unknown problem and still need to model directly for a start unknown one.

**Host B:** So the map tells a teacher two things about any child. Which kinds of problems she can solve, and how.

**Host A:** And it tells the teacher what comes next. If a child is directly modelling, you do not teach her the fact. You give her problems that make counting on more attractive. Bigger numbers, for example. If you give her fifty three plus four, counting out fifty three counters becomes tedious, and she may discover that she can start at fifty three and count on four.

**Host B:** That is a very specific kind of teaching knowledge.

**Host A:** And that is what made Cognitively Guided Instruction different. In the 1980s, educational psychology mostly gave teachers general principles. Children construct knowledge. Use concrete materials. Allow for individual differences. CGI said, here is exactly how children think about this specific mathematical domain. Use it.

**Host B:** Who were the researchers?

**Host A:** The central figures were Thomas Carpenter and Elizabeth Fennema, at Wisconsin. Fennema was already famous for her work on gender and mathematics, on why girls were pushed away from maths. And Penelope Peterson, an educational psychologist who studied teachers' thinking, then also at Wisconsin, later at Michigan State and Northwestern. Along with doctoral students who became major researchers themselves. Megan Franke, Linda Levi, Victoria Jacobs, Susan Empson. Names we will meet again in this series.

**Host B:** And they asked a question.

**Host A:** The question was this. Teachers did not know this map. Most had never heard of it. What would happen if they did? Not if they were given a new curriculum. Not if they were given a script. Just, if they understood how their students were thinking about addition and subtraction. Would it change what they did? And would their students learn more?

**Host B:** And they tested it.

**Host A:** They tested it the way the Direct Instruction world would want. A randomised experiment. Published in 1989 in the American Educational Research Journal, by Carpenter, Fennema, Peterson, Chi Pang Chiang, and Megan Loef. Under the title Using Knowledge of Children's Mathematics Thinking in Classroom Teaching. An Experimental Study.

**Host B:** The design.

**Host A:** Forty first grade teachers in Wisconsin, volunteers, randomly assigned. Twenty to the experimental group, twenty to control. The experimental teachers went through a month long workshop in the summer. And in that workshop they studied the research on children's thinking. The problem types. The strategies. They watched videos of children solving problems. They interviewed children. And then they discussed what it might mean for their teaching.

**Host B:** But they were not told what to do.

**Host A:** And this is the crucial design decision. The abstract says that instruction was not prescribed. Teachers in both groups chose their own practices. The experimental teachers were given knowledge, and then they planned their own teaching for the following school year. The control group had a shorter workshop on non routine problem solving, so they also got professional development, just not about children's thinking.

**Host B:** In Kennedy's terms, that is insight, or strategies, not prescription.

**Host A:** It is almost the purest example of insight in the history of the field. Show teachers how children think, and trust them to work out what that means.

**Host B:** And what happened?

**Host A:** The researchers observed the classrooms through the year. And the experimental teachers taught differently. They spent significantly more time on problem solving, and significantly less time on number facts. They encouraged children to use a variety of strategies. And they listened to children's processes significantly more. They knew more about individual students. When the researchers interviewed the teachers and asked them to predict how specific children would solve specific problems, the experimental teachers were better at it.

**Host B:** So the workshop changed the teachers.

**Host A:** It changed what they did and what they knew. But the question Sold a Story would ask is, did the children learn more?

**Host B:** And?

**Host A:** Here is the result that I think should be in every debate about mathematics teaching. The students in the experimental classes did better at solving problems. That you might expect, since their teachers spent more time on it. But they also did better at number facts. The abstract says it directly.

**Reader:** Students in experimental classes exceeded students in control classes in number fact knowledge.

**Host B:** Even though their teachers spent less time on facts.

**Host A:** Even though their teachers spent less time on facts. That is the finding that cuts against the intuition of the whole fluency debate. The children who spent more time solving problems in their own ways, and less time drilling facts, knew more facts by the end of the year.

**Host B:** How can that be?

**Host A:** The CGI explanation is the derived fact strategy. Children who reason about numbers, who know five and five is ten and use it to get five and six, end up building a web of connected facts that they can recall or quickly rebuild. Facts learned that way are not isolated items to be memorised. They are connected to each other. And connected knowledge is easier to remember.

**Host B:** And the cognitive science people would say?

**Host A:** Many would actually agree that connected knowledge is easier to remember. Where they would push back is on the idea that you do not need explicit practice. And I want to be careful. The CGI teachers did not abandon practice. They spent less time on number facts than control teachers, not none. So the result says, less drill and more reasoning did not hurt facts and seemed to help. It does not say drill is bad.

**Host B:** What else?

**Host A:** The students in the CGI classrooms reported greater understanding and more confidence in their ability to solve problems. And there is a detail in the observations I love. The researchers coded how much time teachers spent listening to students explain how they solved problems. And it was much higher in the CGI classrooms. In Kennedy's terms, they were working on exposing student thinking. And it seems to have paid off across the other problems.

**Host B:** Let me be the sceptic. Forty volunteer teachers in Wisconsin. First grade addition and subtraction. One year. Is that a Follow Through?

**Host A:** It is not a Follow Through. Follow Through had tens of thousands of children across the country. This is forty classrooms. And the volunteers were probably teachers who were open to new ideas. And first grade addition and subtraction is a domain where the research on children's thinking was unusually rich. All fair.

**Host B:** So why is it important?

**Host A:** Because it is a randomised experiment, with a clear result, that tested the core claim of the responsive tradition. That teacher knowledge of children's thinking, without a prescribed curriculum, improves children's learning, including on the basic skills. And because it was followed, unlike most such studies, by years of further research with the same teachers.

**Host B:** The longitudinal study.

**Host A:** The 1996 study, published in the Journal for Research in Mathematics Education by Elizabeth Fennema, Thomas Carpenter, Megan Franke, Linda Levi, Victoria Jacobs and Susan Empson. They worked with twenty one primary teachers over four years. Not a summer workshop, but sustained professional development, meeting regularly, studying children's thinking together, bringing examples from their own classrooms.

**Host B:** And they tracked how the teachers changed.

**Host A:** They developed a set of levels to describe teachers' beliefs and practice. At the lowest level, a teacher demonstrates procedures and has children practise them. Children's thinking is barely visible. At higher levels, the teacher increasingly lets children solve problems in their own ways, listens to their strategies, and finally uses what she learns about each child's thinking to make decisions about what to do next. The top levels were split in two. Level four A and four B. And the highest describes a teacher who uses her knowledge of individual children's thinking to make instructional decisions, and who sees each child's thinking as something to build on.

**Host B:** And how many changed?

**Host A:** Fundamental changes in eighteen of the twenty one teachers. And the abstract puts the shift in one line.

**Reader:** The teachers' role evolved from demonstrating procedures to helping children build on their mathematical thinking.

**Host B:** And the children?

**Host A:** Two findings. The first is striking.

**Reader:** For every teacher, class achievement in concepts and problem solving was higher at the end of the study than at the beginning.

**Host B:** Every teacher.

**Host A:** Every one of the twenty one. And changes in individual teachers' instruction were directly related to changes in their students' achievement. Teachers who moved further up the levels saw bigger gains.

**Host B:** And the second finding?

**Host A:** The honest one.

**Reader:** There was no overall change in computational performance.

**Host B:** So computation did not improve.

**Host A:** Did not improve, and did not decline. The teachers shifted their emphasis from skills to concepts and problem solving, and the children's computation held steady. Which you can read two ways. A supporter says, look, we gained understanding at no cost in skills. A critic says, look, four years of intensive professional development, and computation did not budge.

**Host B:** Which reading do you take?

**Host A:** I take both, honestly. It tells me that CGI is a powerful way to grow children's understanding and problem solving, and that it does not by itself guarantee gains in procedural fluency beyond what children were already getting. If you care most about computation, the 1996 study is not a triumph. If you care about understanding, it is.

**Host B:** And then the follow up.

**Host A:** The 2001 study, in the American Educational Research Journal, by Megan Franke, Thomas Carpenter, Linda Levi and Elizabeth Fennema. And this is one of the rarest things in education research. They went back to teachers about four years after the professional development had ended, to see what had lasted.

**Host B:** Most reforms evaporate.

**Host A:** Most reforms evaporate, and Kennedy's work explains why. So the question was, after the researchers left, did these teachers keep using children's thinking, or drift back to old habits? They followed up twenty two teachers.

**Host B:** And?

**Host A:** All twenty two were still using some knowledge of children's thinking in their teaching. That alone is remarkable. But the interesting finding is about ten of them. Ten teachers had kept learning. Their practice had continued to develop after the programme ended. The authors called this generative growth.

**Host B:** Generative growth.

**Host A:** Meaning the knowledge had become generative. The teachers were not just applying what they had been taught. They were learning from their own students, building on the framework, extending it to new topics. And the ten generative teachers shared five characteristics. They treated children's thinking as central to their teaching. They had detailed knowledge of that thinking. They used frameworks for describing how children's mathematical thinking develops. They saw themselves as building and extending their own knowledge. And they sought out colleagues with similar knowledge for support.

**Host B:** That last one is interesting.

**Host A:** It is enormously important. The teachers who kept growing were the ones who had someone to talk to about their students' thinking. Collaboration sustained the change. And that fits Kennedy's review exactly. Programmes that lasted longer, with follow up and collective participation, had bigger effects.

**Host B:** So, Kennedy's lens. Let us run CGI through the five problems.

**Host A:** Portraying the curriculum. CGI does something unusual. It portrays the content through problems, not explanations. The teacher's skill is in choosing and sequencing problems. The map of problem types gives her a language for that. Which problem will stretch this child next? A start unknown? A compare? Bigger numbers to push her from modelling to counting on? So portrayal becomes a matter of choosing the right problem for the right child.

**Host B:** Enlisting participation.

**Host A:** Very strong in its own way. Children participate because their own strategies are valued. A child who models with counters is not doing it wrong. She is at a stage, and the teacher respects it. And there is evidence from the 1989 study that children in CGI classrooms reported more confidence.

**Host B:** Exposing student thinking.

**Host A:** That is what CGI is. The entire approach is built on the teacher asking, how did you solve it, and understanding the answer. And the map gives the teacher a way to interpret what she hears. Not just, that is interesting, but, she counted on from the larger number, she is ready for derived facts.

**Host B:** Containing behaviour.

**Host A:** Here CGI says less. It assumes the teacher manages her class, and it works in many styles of classroom. Some CGI teachers had quite traditional classroom management. The approach does not solve this problem for the teacher.

**Host B:** And the teacher's personal needs.

**Host A:** That is the hardest one. CGI asks a great deal of the teacher. She has to learn a complex framework. She has to listen to every child. She has to make decisions in the moment based on what she hears. For a confident, experienced teacher who loves mathematics, that is energising. For a struggling teacher in a chaotic school, it can be overwhelming. The 1996 study needed four years to transform eighteen teachers.

**Host B:** So compared with Direct Instruction, CGI is the mirror image.

**Host A:** Almost exactly. Direct Instruction solves portrayal, participation, behaviour and many teachers' needs for the teacher, and is weakest on exposing thinking. CGI is strongest on exposing thinking, uses it to help with portrayal and participation, and leaves behaviour and the teacher's needs largely to the teacher. And it asks much more of her.

**Host B:** Let me raise the hardest objection. Engelmann would say, your four year study transformed eighteen teachers. My programme could be used by thousands of teachers next September. Which helps more children?

**Host A:** That is the hardest objection, and I do not think CGI has a complete answer. Scale is its great weakness. It depends on teachers learning a body of knowledge deeply, over time, with colleagues. You can not buy it in a box. There have been large scale efforts. In 2007, Victoria Jacobs, Megan Franke, Thomas Carpenter, Linda Levi and Dan Battey published a study in the Journal for Research in Mathematics Education of professional development on children's algebraic reasoning, based on CGI ideas, in nearly twenty urban schools in Los Angeles, with about a hundred and eighty teachers. And students in the schools with the professional development did better on understanding the equals sign and relational thinking.

**Host B:** The equals sign.

**Host A:** The equals sign is a famous one. Many children think it means, write the answer here. So if you give them eight plus four equals blank plus five, they write twelve. Because eight plus four is twelve. They do not see that the equals sign means the same as, a relationship between two sides. CGI style teaching of what Carpenter, Franke and Levi called relational thinking tackled that. And that study was at scale, in urban schools serving many low income children.

**Host B:** So it can scale, somewhat.

**Host A:** Somewhat. And there was also research in the early 1990s, by Josefina Villaseñor and Henry Kepner, implementing CGI in urban first grade classrooms in Milwaukee, with promising results on problem solving. But nobody would claim CGI has the scale of evidence that Direct Instruction's half century meta analysis has. It has fewer studies. Mostly smaller. Mostly in early elementary arithmetic.

**Host B:** And the equity question cuts which way?

**Host A:** That is a fascinating question. Direct Instruction's defenders say, poor children need the most structured teaching. CGI's defenders say, poor children have mathematical ideas too, and they have been denied the respect of having them taken seriously. And the CGI researchers were deliberate about working in urban schools for exactly that reason. In Children's Mathematics, the book that came out of the programme, they write repeatedly that all children come to school with intuitive mathematical knowledge. Not just middle class children.

**Host B:** Tell us about the book.

**Host A:** Children's Mathematics. Cognitively Guided Instruction. By Thomas Carpenter, Elizabeth Fennema, Megan Franke, Linda Levi and Susan Empson. First published in 1999, with a second edition in 2015. It is written for teachers, not researchers. It lays out the problem types and strategies, extends them to multiplication and division, and to multi digit numbers, and it is full of transcripts of children solving problems. And it comes with video of real children. It is one of the most widely used books in elementary mathematics teacher education in America.

**Host B:** Multiplication and division too.

**Host A:** Multiplication and division have their own problem types. Multiplication, where you know the number of groups and the size of each, and want the total. Measurement division, where you know the total and the size of each group, and want the number of groups. Nineteen cookies, three in each bag, how many bags. And partitive division, where you know the total and the number of groups, and want the size of each. Nineteen cookies shared among three friends, how many does each get. And children model those two kinds of division completely differently. For measurement division, they make groups of three until they run out. For partitive, they deal out the cookies one at a time, like cards.

**Host B:** And a teacher who knows that will hear two children solving division problems and understand that they are doing different things.

**Host A:** And will not be confused when a child who can do one kind can not do the other. That is the power of the map.

**Host B:** Let us hear one, the way the book presents them. This is written for the episode, but it is the kind of exchange the book is full of.

**Teacher:** Nineteen cookies. Three in each bag. How many bags can you fill? Show me how you would solve it.

**Child:** Three, six, nine, twelve, fifteen, eighteen. That is six bags. And one cookie left over.

**Teacher:** How did you know to stop at eighteen?

**Child:** Because twenty one is too many. There are only nineteen.

**Teacher:** What would you do with the one left over?

**Child:** Eat it.

**Host A:** That is skip counting. A child who knows her threes is using them to solve a measurement division problem, without being taught division. And the teacher's question, what would you do with the left over one, opens up remainders, which is the next piece of mathematics, without the teacher having to introduce it.

**Host B:** And the joke at the end is real. Children say that.

**Host A:** All the time. And a CGI teacher would take it seriously. If the problem had been about sharing among three friends, eating the leftover would raise a question of fairness. Should we cut it into thirds? And that leads to fractions.

**Host B:** Which is the second edition's extension.

**Host A:** Susan Empson and Linda Levi wrote a separate book on fractions in the same tradition, Extending Children's Mathematics, in 2011, built on equal sharing problems. Four children share three pizzas equally. How much does each child get? And the strategies children invent for that are the seeds of fraction understanding.

**Host B:** Let us spend a few minutes inside a CGI classroom, because the papers describe it, but it helps to hear the shape of a lesson. This is a composite, written for the episode from the kinds of lessons the research describes.

**Host A:** A first grade class. Twenty two children. The teacher reads one story problem aloud, with numbers chosen carefully. Sometimes she offers a choice of number sets, easier and harder, so every child can start. Today it is a separating problem, result unknown, and the numbers are fourteen and six. The children work on it at their tables, with counters, paper, fingers, whatever they want. The teacher walks around. She is not explaining. She is watching, and asking.

**Teacher:** Marcus, how are you solving it?

**Child:** I got fourteen cubes. Now I take away six. One, two, three, four, five, six. Now I count what is left. Eight.

**Teacher:** Thank you. Keep going, try the next one.

**Host A:** She has learned that Marcus is still directly modelling. She moves on.

**Teacher:** Priya, I see you did not use cubes. How did you know?

**Child:** I went backwards. Fourteen, thirteen, twelve, eleven, ten, nine, eight. I put up six fingers.

**Host A:** Priya is counting down. A counting strategy. One step further along.

**Teacher:** Sam, you wrote eight very fast.

**Child:** Because six and eight is fourteen. I know six and six is twelve, and two more is fourteen. So it is eight.

**Host A:** Sam is using derived facts. And in about four minutes, the teacher has a picture of where every child is. Then comes sharing time. And here is where the skill is. She does not ask children at random. She chooses the order. Marcus first, because his strategy is the most concrete and everyone can follow it. Then Priya. Then Sam. And she asks the class questions that connect them.

**Teacher:** Marcus took away six cubes. Priya counted back six. How are those the same?

**Child:** They both took away six. Priya did it with numbers.

**Teacher:** And Sam did not take away at all. He thought about what goes with six to make fourteen. Is that still the same problem?

**Host A:** And that question, is that still the same problem, is where the deep mathematics lives. The relationship between addition and subtraction. That subtraction can be thought of as a missing addend. And the teacher does not tell them. She gets them to see it, from their own strategies.

**Host B:** That sequencing of who shares is something we will hear about again, in the discussion episode, from Mary Kay Stein and Margaret Smith.

**Host A:** It is. And notice something about this lesson that connects to Engelmann, surprisingly.

**Host B:** Go on.

**Host A:** Engelmann's genius was in the sequencing of examples. His theory of instruction, written with Douglas Carnine, is obsessed with which example comes next, so that the child can only draw the right conclusion. And a CGI teacher is also obsessed with sequencing. Which problem next, which numbers, which child shares first. The difference is who does the sequencing, and from what. In Direct Instruction, the programme sequences the examples in advance, from an analysis of the content. In CGI, the teacher sequences problems and sharing in the moment, from an analysis of the children in front of her.

**Host B:** So they agree that sequence is everything.

**Host A:** They agree that sequence is everything. They disagree about whether it can be fixed in advance. And the honest answer may be that some of it can, and some of it can not.

**Host B:** Let me bring Elizabeth Fennema back for a moment, because her story matters to this.

**Host A:** Before CGI, Fennema was best known for research, much of it with Julia Sherman in the 1970s, on gender differences in mathematics, showing that girls' lower participation in advanced maths had a lot to do with confidence, expectations and how they were treated, rather than ability. And you can feel that background in CGI. The insistence that every child's thinking is worth taking seriously. The concern with confidence as well as competence. The 1989 study measured children's confidence, which a pure achievement study might not have bothered to do.

**Host B:** And later CGI research looked at gender too.

**Host A:** It did. There was work in the 1990s by Fennema, Carpenter and colleagues looking at whether boys and girls in CGI classrooms used different strategies. They reported that girls tended to use more concrete strategies, like modelling and counting, while boys more often used invented, more abstract strategies. Which raised a hard question for responsive teaching. If you let children choose their own strategies, do you risk letting existing differences grow?

**Host B:** That is a serious challenge.

**Host A:** It is, and it is one of the places where the explicit instruction world has a point. If every child is taught the same efficient strategy, nobody is left behind with a slower one. The CGI answer is that a teacher who knows the map will notice which children are stuck at modelling, and will deliberately give them problems and conversations that move them on. But that depends on the teacher noticing. Which is exactly the subject of episode five.

**Host B:** Let me also ask about the teachers who did not change. Three of the twenty one in the four year study.

**Host A:** The paper reports that fundamental change happened in eighteen of twenty one. And it would be lovely to know more about the three. Were they struggling with classroom management? Did they disagree with the approach? Did they lack mathematical confidence? Kennedy would want to know what problems they were trying to solve that CGI did not help with. I suspect the answer is in her five problems somewhere.

**Host B:** The research tends to tell the success stories.

**Host A:** It does, and the follow up is honest about the range. Ten of twenty two showed generative growth. That means twelve did not keep growing, even if they kept some of what they had learned. A fair summary is that CGI changed almost everyone it touched, and kept changing about half.

**Host B:** Which, by the standards of professional development, is extraordinary.

**Host A:** By Kennedy's standards, where most effects are small and fade, it is extraordinary. By the standards of a story that wants a method you can install everywhere next year, it is a warning.

**Host B:** Now let me ask the question this series keeps coming back to. Is CGI discovery learning?

**Host A:** No, and this is a confusion that the Sold a Story framing makes very easy. Discovery learning, in its pure form, means leaving children to find things out with minimal guidance. CGI is not minimal guidance. The teacher chooses every problem deliberately. She asks specific questions. She decides when to push a child to a more efficient strategy, when to ask another child to share a different approach, when to introduce notation. She is guiding constantly. What she is not doing is demonstrating a procedure first and asking children to copy it.

**Host B:** So guided, but from the child's thinking outwards.

**Host A:** That is a good way to put it. Explicit instruction starts from the teacher's model and moves towards the child. CGI starts from the child's strategy and moves towards the mathematics. Both are guided. They differ in the starting point.

**Host B:** And the cognitive load theorists would say, starting from the child's strategy is inefficient, because novices waste working memory searching for a method.

**Host A:** And CGI's answer is that young children are not searching blindly. They already have strategies, direct modelling, counting, and those strategies are well within their working memory. The research map shows that children can solve these problems with what they already have. You are not asking them to invent from nothing. You are asking them to use what they have and to refine it. That is a crucial point, and it is often missed in the debate.

**Host B:** But that may be true for first grade arithmetic and less true for, say, algebra in ninth grade.

**Host A:** Very fair. CGI is strongest exactly where children have rich informal knowledge to draw on. Counting, sharing, combining. The further you go from everyday experience, the less a child has to start from, and the stronger the cognitive load argument becomes. We will come back to that in the final episode.

**Host B:** Let me add one more thing about the 1989 paper that I think is underappreciated. The beliefs.

**Host A:** Go on.

**Host B:** The experimental teachers came to believe that instruction should build on students' existing knowledge more than the control teachers did. And the same group, Penelope Peterson, Elizabeth Fennema, Thomas Carpenter and Megan Loef, published a companion study in 1989 on teachers' beliefs, which found that teachers' beliefs about how children learn mathematics were related to how their students performed.

**Host A:** Which brings us back to Kennedy and Mrs. Oublier. Because CGI was not trying to change teachers' behaviour directly. It was trying to change what they knew and believed about children. And the behaviour followed. Mrs. Oublier had new materials and old beliefs. CGI tried to give teachers new knowledge, which changed their beliefs, which changed their practice.

**Host B:** Knowledge, then beliefs, then practice.

**Host A:** Or sometimes the other way round. In the 1996 study, some teachers' beliefs changed first, and some teachers' practice changed first and pulled their beliefs along. Change is messy. But the direction was clear.

**Host B:** Let us state the CGI case, as strongly as its defenders would.

**Host A:** The case is this. Children come to school with rich, intuitive mathematical knowledge. That knowledge develops in predictable ways. Teachers who understand those patterns can teach far more effectively, by choosing problems that stretch each child, and by building on each child's strategies.
