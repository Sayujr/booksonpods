That teaching was its own kind of intellectual work, with its own problems, and that you could study it seriously.

**Host B:** And Lampert and Ball took that idea literally.

**Host A:** They took it all the way. Both of them taught mathematics at an elementary school near the campus, a school that served many families from the university's graduate student housing. And Lampert taught a fifth grade class, and Ball taught a third grade class, every day, while doing their university jobs.

**Host B:** Which is an enormous amount of work.

**Host A:** It is an absurd amount of work. And Mary Kennedy, if she were here, would point that out immediately. Because her fifth problem, accommodating personal needs, is exactly the problem that this arrangement makes visible. Lampert and Ball had support that ordinary teachers do not. They had research assistants. They had video cameras. They had colleagues watching. They had a university salary. But they also had something ordinary teachers have, which is a room full of children who need to learn mathematics by June.

**Host B:** And they wrote about it.

**Host A:** They wrote about it with a kind of honesty that is rare in education research. Not here is the method that works. But here is what happened, here is what I was trying to do, here is what I was worried about, here is what I chose, and here is what it cost.

**Host B:** Lampert 1985. Harvard Educational Review. How Do Teachers Manage to Teach? Perspectives on Problems in Practice.

**Host A:** This is the paper that set up everything that followed. And it starts from a very simple observation about the way education research usually describes teaching. Most research, she argued, treats teaching as a series of technical problems. You have a goal. You find the method that achieves the goal. You apply the method. And if the method works, the problem is solved.

**Host B:** Which is exactly the Follow Through picture. Find the model that works. Scale it up.

**Host A:** Exactly the Follow Through picture. And Lampert says that is not what it is like to be a teacher. What it is like to be a teacher is to face, constantly, choices between things that you value, where you can not have both. One writer summarising the paper years later put it well. Teachers, in Lampert's account, constantly face all sorts of moral, intellectual, and practical dilemmas. If you choose to praise one student, another will feel neglected. If you slow down for the child who is confused, you lose the child who is bored. If you let a wrong idea stand long enough for the class to examine it, some children will go home believing it.

**Host B:** And those are not problems you solve.

**Host A:** That is the key move. A problem, in the technical sense, has a solution. Once you find it, the problem goes away. A dilemma does not go away. You choose. And the thing you did not choose is still there, still valuable, still pulling at you. And tomorrow you will face the same dilemma again, in a slightly different form, and you might choose differently.

**Host B:** So what does a teacher do with a dilemma, if she can not solve it?

**Host A:** Lampert's phrase is that the teacher becomes a dilemma manager. And she illustrates it from her own classroom. In the paper she describes a situation that will be familiar to anyone who has ever taught. A group of boys in her class who were loud, quick, competitive, and inclined to dominate. And a group of girls who were quieter, and who were more easily drawn away from the mathematics when the boys took over. And she describes her choices. Where to stand. Whom to call on. Where to seat whom. Every choice that helps one group costs something for the other.

**Host B:** And she does not pretend there was a right answer.

**Host A:** She does not. What she describes is a kind of improvised compromise. She makes arrangements that keep both concerns alive, without fully satisfying either. And she argues that this is not a failure of teaching. It is what skilled teaching actually is. The skilled teacher does not eliminate the conflicts. She holds them, and keeps working within them, and keeps both of the things she values in play.

**Host B:** That sounds like a description that could apply to almost any profession.

**Host A:** She would agree with that, and she would say that is the point. Teaching is like medicine or law or parenting in that respect. It is a practice full of competing goods. But the research on teaching, in 1985, mostly did not describe it that way. It described it like engineering.

**Host B:** How does this connect to Kennedy?

**Host A:** Very directly. Kennedy's five persistent problems are, in a sense, an expanded and systematised version of Lampert's dilemmas. Kennedy says every lesson has to portray the curriculum, enlist participation, expose thinking, contain behaviour, and accommodate the teacher's own needs. And those five pull against each other all the time. Exposing student thinking, letting children talk about their ideas, makes containing behaviour harder. Portraying the curriculum efficiently, getting through the content, makes exposing student thinking harder. So Kennedy's problems are Lampert's dilemmas, named and classified.

**Host B:** And Kennedy calls them problems, not dilemmas.

**Host A:** She calls them persistent problems, which is a nice compromise. Persistent means they never go away. Every lesson, every day, every teacher. And that is Lampert's insight. You do not solve them once. You manage them forever.

**Host B:** And Direct Instruction?

**Host A:** Direct Instruction, in a sense, is a very powerful answer to the dilemma problem. Because it makes many of those choices in advance. The script decides the pace. The script decides what the teacher says. The signals decide who answers and when. The children answer together, in unison, so there is no choice about whom to call on. Many of the dilemmas Lampert describes are simply taken off the table.

**Host B:** Is that a good thing?

**Host A:** That is the question of this whole series. A defender of Direct Instruction would say yes, absolutely. A new teacher facing thirty children is overwhelmed by dilemmas. A well designed script carries her through. It frees her attention to watch the children, to hear the errors, to correct them immediately. And Engelmann's whole theory, which he called faultless communication, was built on the idea that if you design the sequence of examples carefully enough, the child can only learn the right thing.

**Host B:** And Lampert would say?

**Host A:** Lampert would say that taking the dilemmas off the table also takes something else off the table. Because the dilemmas are where the children's ideas live. When a child says something unexpected, the teacher faces a dilemma. Follow the idea, or stay on the plan. If the script has already decided, the child's idea has nowhere to go. And her 1990 paper is about what happens when you let it go somewhere.

**Host B:** Lampert 1990. When the Problem Is Not the Question and the Solution Is Not the Answer: Mathematical Knowing and Teaching. American Educational Research Journal, volume 27.

**Host A:** This paper is long, dense, and genuinely beautiful. And it has two halves. One half is about mathematics itself, what it means to know mathematics. And the other half is about her classroom, what it would look like to teach children to know mathematics in that way.

**Host B:** Start with the mathematics half.

**Host A:** She starts with a contrast. How mathematics is usually known in school, and how mathematicians actually know it. And her description of the school version is cutting. Knowing mathematics in school, she writes, comes to mean having a set of unexamined beliefs. You know the rules. You know the procedures. You know that the answer is in the back of the book. And you know that the teacher knows. What you do not do is ask why. What you do not do is argue.

**Host B:** And mathematicians do argue.

**Host A:** Mathematicians argue all the time. And here she brings in two thinkers. George Pólya, the Hungarian mathematician who wrote How to Solve It, and who argued that mathematics in the making looks completely different from mathematics in the textbook. In the textbook, it looks like a chain of deductions. In the making, it looks like guessing, testing, and revising.

**Host B:** And the second?

**Host A:** Imre Lakatos. A philosopher of mathematics, Hungarian born like Pólya, who wrote a book called Proofs and Refutations. And Proofs and Refutations is one of the strangest and most wonderful books ever written about mathematics. It is written as a dialogue in an imaginary classroom. A teacher and his students, who have names like Alpha and Beta and Gamma, are trying to prove a theorem about polyhedra. And every time they think they have proved it, someone finds a counterexample. A shape that breaks the rule. And then they have to decide what to do. Throw out the theorem? Change the definition of a polyhedron? Restrict the theorem to some cases? And through that process, the theorem and the proof and the definitions all evolve together.

**Host B:** So mathematics as argument.

**Host A:** Mathematics as an argument among people. And Lampert quotes a line from Lakatos that I find quite moving. I respect conscious guessing, because it comes from the best human qualities: courage and modesty.

**Host B:** Courage and modesty.

**Host A:** Courage, because you have to say something before you know it is right. Modesty, because you have to be ready to give it up. And Lampert's question is, what if school mathematics taught that? Not just the results, but the courage and the modesty.

**Host B:** And her answer is the fifth grade classroom.

**Host A:** Her answer is the fifth grade classroom. And here is the most important thing about the title. When the problem is not the question. In most classrooms, the problem is the question the teacher asks. What is seven times eight. And the solution is the answer. Fifty six. Lampert wanted something different. She wanted the question she asked to be just an entry point into a real mathematical problem, one that the class would work on together, and she wanted the solution to be not just an answer but an argument. A reason. Something you could defend.

**Host B:** She has a line about that.

**Host A:** She does. Students' strategies yield answers to teachers' questions, but the solution is more than the answer.

**Host B:** So walk us through the lesson.

**Host A:** The squares and the last digits. She starts with the list of squares we described in the opening, and the class notices patterns. Then she poses the harder questions. What is the last digit of five to the fourth? Six to the fourth? Seven to the fourth?

**Host B:** Let us do them, for listeners who want to play along.

**Host A:** Five to the fourth. Five times five is twenty five, ends in five. Twenty five times five ends in five again. Any power of five ends in five. So five to the fourth ends in five.

**Host B:** That one is friendly.

**Host A:** Six to the fourth. Same logic. Six times six is thirty six, ends in six. Six times anything ending in six ends in six. So every power of six ends in six.

**Host B:** Also friendly.

**Host A:** Seven to the fourth. This is where it gets interesting. Seven squared is forty nine, ends in nine. Seven cubed, multiply by seven, nine times seven is sixty three, ends in three. Seven to the fourth, three times seven is twenty one, ends in one. So seven to the fourth ends in one. And then it cycles. Seven to the fifth ends in seven again. Seven, nine, three, one, seven, nine, three, one.

**Host B:** So the last digits of powers of seven go round in a cycle of four.

**Host A:** They go round in a cycle of four. And there is another way to see seven to the fourth. It is seven squared times seven squared. Forty nine times forty nine. And you only need the last digits. Nine times nine is eighty one. Ends in one.

**Host B:** And the children are discovering this?

**Host A:** The children are proposing ideas, and many of the first ideas are wrong. That is the point. Someone might guess that because five to the fourth ends in five and six to the fourth ends in six, seven to the fourth must end in seven. That is a perfectly reasonable conjecture. It is wrong. And the question is what the teacher does with it.

**Host B:** And what does Lampert do?

**Host A:** This is the heart of the paper. She does not say, no, that is wrong. She does not say, yes, that is right, to the children who get it right, either. She writes, and this is a sentence I want listeners to remember, I purposely did not ratify any of the students' assertions.

**Host B:** She did not ratify.

**Host A:** She did not ratify. Because if she says that is right, the argument stops. The children learn that the way to know if something is true is to look at the teacher's face. And she wants them to learn that the way to know if something is true is to give a reason, and to test it, and to listen to the reasons of others.

**Host B:** But somebody has to decide eventually.

**Host A:** Somebody does. The class does. Through argument. Someone says, but seven times seven is forty nine, so it can not end in seven. Someone else says, but you have to keep going, you have to multiply again. And the reasons accumulate, and the wrong conjectures fall away because someone has found a reason they can not survive. Not because the teacher said so.

**Host B:** That is where Arthur comes in.

**Host A:** That is where Arthur comes in. Arthur has been defending one idea. And in the course of the argument, he hears a reason that shows his idea does not work. And he says, I want to revise my thinking. And Lampert points out how remarkable that is. He is not saying I was wrong, I am bad at maths. He is not saying I give up. He is saying that his thinking is something he can change, deliberately, for a reason, while he himself stays the same person.

**Host B:** Courage and modesty.

**Host A:** Courage and modesty, in a ten year old. And Lampert says this did not happen by accident. She had worked on the language of the classroom for months. And one of the things she worked on was a tiny change in how children phrase their claims. Instead of saying it is fifty six, they learned to say, I think it is fifty six. And she explains why that tiny change matters. Saying I think rather than it is protects the student from associating his or her sense of self with being right or wrong.

**Host B:** That is a very modern sounding idea.

**Host A:** It is a very modern sounding idea. You can hear it in everything that came later about growth mindset and classroom culture. But Lampert's version is not really about self esteem. It is about mathematics. She wants children to separate their ideas from themselves so that they can examine the ideas. If my idea is me, then attacking my idea is attacking me, and I will defend it no matter what. If my idea is something I put on the table, then we can all look at it together.

**Host B:** And this is all in 1990.

**Host A:** All in 1990. And here I want to pause, because this is exactly the kind of classroom that Sold a Story's second season would be suspicious of, and we should take that suspicion seriously.

**Host B:** Go ahead. Make the suspicious case.

**Host A:** The suspicious case goes like this. Lampert was a professor of education at a major research university. She had thought about mathematics and philosophy for years. She had read Lakatos. She had a research team. She was, by any measure, an extraordinary teacher. And what she describes is beautiful. But how many of the three and a half million teachers in America can do what she did? And if they try, and they fail, what happens to the children?

**Host B:** And what does happen?

**Host A:** What happens, the critics would say, is something that looks like Lampert's classroom on the surface, with children talking and the teacher refusing to give answers, but without the deep mathematical knowledge that lets Lampert steer the argument toward the truth. And in that classroom, wrong ideas are not refuted. They just float around. And the children who already have support at home figure it out, and the children who do not, fall further behind.

**Host B:** That is a serious charge.

**Host A:** It is a serious charge. And it is essentially the charge that Kirschner, Sweller and Clark would make in 2006, which we will get to in our final episode. Minimal guidance does not work. And Lampert's defenders would say, first, her classroom is not minimal guidance. Look at how much she is doing. She chose the problem with enormous care. She has built the norms over months. She knows exactly where the mathematics is going. She is guiding constantly. She is just not guiding by telling.

**Host B:** And second?

**Host A:** And second, they would say that this is precisely the problem. The question is not whether Lampert's teaching is good. The question is what it takes to make it possible for ordinary teachers. And that, in a sense, is what the rest of this episode, and the rest of this series, is about.

**Host B:** Before we leave Lampert, one more thing about the 1990 paper.

**Host A:** The relationship between the mathematics half and the classroom half. Because she is making a claim that is easy to miss. She is not saying, here is a nice way to motivate children. She is saying that what it means to know mathematics is to be able to take part in this kind of argument. And that if you only learn the rules, you do not know mathematics. You know something about mathematics. And that is a claim about the curriculum, about what the content is. It is Kennedy's first problem, portraying the curriculum, but it changes what the curriculum is.

**Host B:** So the debate with Direct Instruction is not only about method.

**Host A:** It is also about what mathematics is. And that is something Sold a Story, in my view, does not quite face. If you think mathematics is a body of facts and procedures, then the question of how to teach it is a question of efficiency, and Direct Instruction has a strong case. If you think mathematics is also a practice of reasoning, conjecturing, and arguing, then you have to ask whether a method that never lets children conjecture can teach that part. And that is an argument about values as much as evidence.

**Host B:** Lampert went on, in 2001, to write a whole book about a year of this teaching.

**Host A:** Teaching Problems and the Problems of Teaching, published by Yale University Press in 2001. And it is a remarkable book because she uses records of an entire year of fifth grade mathematics, with video and notes and children's notebooks, to show how a teacher manages the problems of teaching across different scales of time. A single exchange with a child. A lesson. A week. A unit. A year. And she shows how decisions at each scale constrain the others.

**Host B:** The title is a pun.

**Host A:** The title is a pun. Teaching problems, meaning teaching by using problems, mathematical problems. And the problems of teaching, meaning the dilemmas, the difficulties, the things that make teaching hard. And her argument is that you can not separate the two. If you teach with problems, you take on a particular set of problems of teaching.

**Host B:** And one reviewer put the challenge of the book as how to square a perennial circle.

**Host A:** How to teach the whole class while attending to each child. Which is, in a sense, the circle every teacher is trying to square.

**Host B:** Now to Deborah Ball.

**Host A:** Deborah Loewenberg Ball. She had been an elementary school teacher for years before she became a researcher. And she kept teaching. At the same school as Lampert, she taught third grade mathematics, every day, for several years, and had the lessons videotaped, and kept notes, and kept the children's notebooks. And that archive became one of the most studied records of teaching in the world. Researchers are still writing papers from it decades later.

**Host B:** And her 1993 paper.

**Host A:** With an Eye on the Mathematical Horizon: Dilemmas of Teaching Elementary School Mathematics. The Elementary School Journal, 1993. And as the title tells you, it is built directly on Lampert's idea of dilemmas.

**Host B:** Before we get to the dilemmas, where does the phrase intellectually honest come from? Because it is all over this paper.

**Host A:** It comes from the Kennedy era, which is why I love it. In 1959, two years after Sputnik, a group of scientists, mathematicians and psychologists met at Woods Hole on Cape Cod to talk about how to reform science and mathematics education in America. And the psychologist Jerome Bruner wrote up the conclusions in a short book in 1960, The Process of Education. And in that book is one of the most famous sentences in all of education. Any subject can be taught effectively in some intellectually honest form to any child at any stage of development.

**Host B:** In some intellectually honest form.

**Host A:** That phrase is the whole challenge. Bruner was saying you do not have to wait until children are older to teach them real mathematics. You can teach a seven year old something true and important about, say, functions or proof, if you find the right form. But it has to be honest. It can not be a simplification that is actually false, that the child will have to unlearn later. And Ball takes that phrase and asks what it means in a real third grade classroom on a Tuesday morning.

**Host B:** So the New Math era handed her the question.

**Host A:** The New Math era handed her the question, and her answer is much more grounded than anything the New Math reformers produced. Because she was the one standing in front of the children.

**Host B:** Her three dilemmas.

**Host A:** Three dilemmas. The first is about representing the content. The second is about respecting children as mathematical thinkers. And the third is about creating and using community.

**Host B:** Start with representation.

**Host A:** The first dilemma is about how to represent a mathematical idea so that children can get hold of it, without distorting it. And her example is negative numbers, with third graders.

**Host B:** Negative numbers in third grade.

**Host A:** Negative numbers in third grade, which already tells you something about Bruner's influence. And the problem with negative numbers is that every representation of them is imperfect. She tried different models. One was a building with floors above and below the ground, so that going down from the first floor takes you to the basement levels, which you can call negative floors. And another model was money, where negative numbers represent owing.

**Host B:** And they both have problems.

**Host A:** Every model has problems. The building model makes it easy to see that negative numbers are below zero, and to see that minus five is lower than minus two. But it makes some operations awkward. What does it mean to add a negative floor? And the money model makes some operations natural, like having a debt and paying it off, but children of eight have a hard time with the idea that you can have less than no money. And neither model makes it obvious why, say, five plus negative three equals two.

**Host B:** So what does she conclude?

**Host A:** She concludes that there is no perfect representation, and that the teacher has to choose, knowing what each representation makes visible and what it hides. And that this choice requires a particular kind of knowledge. You have to understand negative numbers well enough to see the mathematical structure each model captures and each model misses. And you have to understand eight year olds well enough to know which models they can actually think with.

**Host B:** That is going to come back later in the episode.

**Host A:** That is the seed of mathematical knowledge for teaching. Hold on to it.

**Host B:** Second dilemma. Respecting children as mathematical thinkers.

**Host A:** This is the famous one. Sean numbers.

**Host B:** Tell the story.

**Host A:** Ball's third graders had been working on even and odd numbers. And one morning, during a discussion, a boy named Sean says that six can be both odd and even.

**Host B:** That sounds simply wrong.

**Host A:** It sounds simply wrong. Six is even. Every textbook says so. And here is how Sean explains it, in words that Ball and her colleague Hyman Bass later quoted when they returned to the episode. Both! Three things to make it, and there could be two things to make it.

**Host B:** Three things to make it.

**Host A:** What Sean means is this. Six can be made of three twos. Two plus two plus two. Three groups. Three is odd. And six can also be made of two threes. Three plus three. Two groups. Two is even. So in Sean's way of thinking, six is odd, because it is three of something, and also even, because it is two of something.

**Host B:** That is actually quite clever.

**Host A:** It is very clever. And here is Ball's dilemma. Every instinct of a teacher, and every instinct of the curriculum, says correct him. Six is even. The definition of even is that a number can be split into two equal groups, or into pairs with none left over. Six fits. Done. Move on.

**Host B:** And what does she do?

**Host A:** She does not correct him. Ball and Bass put it simply. The teacher does not immediately challenge or correct Sean. Instead, she asks the class to think about his idea. And then other children start to engage with it. And one girl, Mei, says something devastating. She points out that if Sean's reasoning is right, then lots of numbers would be both odd and even, and in her words, if all numbers were odd and even, we wouldn't even be having this discussion!

**Host B:** That is a brilliant objection.

**Host A:** It is a mathematician's objection. She is saying, your definition is useless, because it does not distinguish anything. A definition of even that makes everything even is not a definition. And that is a profound insight into what definitions are for. They exist to separate things.

**Host B:** And Sean?

**Host A:** Sean does not simply give up. And this is where the episode becomes wonderful. Because Sean has noticed something real. Not every number is like six. Take four. Four is two twos. Two groups of two. Even number of groups. Four can not be made of an odd number of twos. Take ten. Ten is five twos. An odd number of twos. So ten is like six. Take eight. Eight is four twos, an even number of twos. So eight is not like six.

**Host B:** So there is a pattern.

**Host A:** There is a pattern. Six, ten, fourteen, eighteen. Numbers that are two times an odd number. They have a special property, and Sean has found it. And rather than tell Sean he is wrong, Ball lets the class explore the idea, and the class gives these numbers a name. Sean numbers.

**Host B:** They named a class of numbers after a third grader.

**Host A:** They named a class of numbers after a third grader. And Ball and Bass, looking back, describe Sean's idea with a lovely phrase. An odd way of being even. Sean numbers are even numbers that are made of an odd number of twos.

**Host B:** And is this real mathematics?

**Host A:** It is real mathematics. In number theory, these are sometimes called singly even numbers, numbers that are divisible by two but not by four. They have real properties. And Sean found them on his own, at the age of eight.

**Host B:** But Sean was also, by the standard definition, wrong about six.

**Host A:** And that is exactly the dilemma, and Ball does not pretend it away. On one side, she wants to respect Sean as a mathematical thinker, to take his idea seriously, to let the class see that a child's idea can be important. On the other side, she has a responsibility to the mathematics. Six is even. The definition of even is not up for a vote. And there are other children in the room who are not sure what even means, and who might go home more confused.

**Host B:** Ball and Bass have a phrase for this.

**Host A:** They put it as a balance. How to balance mathematical rigour with generosity toward emergent student thinking.

**Host B:** Rigour and generosity.

**Host A:** Rigour and generosity. And notice that this is precisely Lampert's dilemma structure. Two things you value, pulling in opposite directions. You can not have full rigour and full generosity at the same moment. You manage it.

**Host B:** How did she manage it?
