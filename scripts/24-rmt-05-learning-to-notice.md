**Host B:** Start with Goodwin.

**Host A:** Charles Goodwin was an anthropologist at UCLA who studied how people see. Not in the sense of eyesight. In the sense of how members of a profession learn to see the world in the particular way their profession requires. And in 1994 he published a paper in American Anthropologist called Professional Vision.

**Host B:** And his definition.

**Host A:** Professional vision, he wrote, consists of ways of seeing and understanding events that are answerable to the distinctive interests of a particular social group.

**Host B:** Answerable to the interests of a group.

**Host A:** Meaning that how a professional sees is not neutral. It is shaped by what the profession cares about. And he gives two examples that could not be more different.

**Host B:** The first.

**Host A:** The first is archaeologists. He watched archaeologists in the field learning to look at dirt. To a non archaeologist, dirt is dirt. To an archaeologist, the colour of the soil, the texture, the layers, are evidence. And he showed how they use tools, like a standard chart of soil colours, and practices, like drawing the layers of an excavation on a map, to turn dirt into data. A student archaeologist has to learn to see the dirt that way. It does not come naturally.

**Host B:** And the second.

**Host A:** The second is much darker. It is the trial in 1992 of the Los Angeles police officers who were filmed beating Rodney King. The video seemed, to most people who watched it, to show a man on the ground being beaten. But in the first trial, the defence called a police expert who went through the video frame by frame, and taught the jury to see it differently. To see each movement of King's body as a possible sign of aggression, and each blow as a measured response within police procedure. And the jury acquitted the officers.

**Host B:** So professional vision can be used to see things that are not there.

**Host A:** Or to see things in a way that serves a profession's interests. Goodwin's point was that seeing is never just seeing. Professions teach their members how to look. They highlight some features and hide others. And that power can be used well, as with the archaeologists, or used to justify something terrible, as in the King trial.

**Host B:** What does this have to do with teaching?

**Host A:** Everything. Because a teacher watching a child solve a problem is also seeing through a professional lens. And the question is, what lens? A teacher who has been trained to look for correct answers sees right and wrong. A teacher who has been trained to look for behaviour sees on task and off task. And a teacher who has been trained in children's mathematical thinking sees something else. She sees that this child is counting all, and that one is counting on, and that one over there is using a derived fact. Same classroom. Same children. Different vision.

**Host B:** And Direct Instruction has a professional vision too.

**Host A:** It absolutely does. And it is a sharp one. A Direct Instruction teacher is trained to listen to the unison response of the group and detect, instantly, which child said the wrong thing, or said it late. Engelmann's teacher training was intense about this. You hear the error. You correct it immediately. You go back. You test again. That is a form of noticing. A very specific form.

**Host B:** Noticing errors.

**Host A:** Noticing deviations from the correct response. Which is extremely useful. What it is not designed to notice is the strategy behind the error, or a correct answer reached by an interesting route, or a wrong answer that contains a good idea. Like Sean.

**Host B:** So the two traditions differ in what they train teachers to see.

**Host A:** That is a precise way to put it, and I think it is one of the most useful ways to frame this whole debate. Not do teachers notice, but what are they trained to notice, and what does that make visible and invisible.

**Host B:** Before we get to the teachers, there is another root of the word noticing, and it comes from England.

**Host A:** John Mason. A mathematics educator at the Open University in Britain, who spent decades writing about how mathematicians and teachers pay attention. And in 2002 he published a book called Researching Your Own Practice: The Discipline of Noticing.

**Host B:** The discipline of noticing.

**Host A:** The word discipline matters. Mason's point is that everyone notices things. You notice that a child looks bored. You notice that the lesson went badly. But that ordinary noticing is passive and patchy. It happens to you. A discipline of noticing is something you practise deliberately, so that in the moment, you are more likely to notice the thing that matters, and to have a different response available.

**Host B:** How do you practise it?

**Host A:** One of his central tools is a distinction between two kinds of description. What he calls an account of a moment, and an account for it. An account of is a brief but vivid description of what happened, as free as possible of judgement and explanation. The child drew nineteen lines and rubbed out seven. An account for is an interpretation, an explanation, a judgement. She does not yet trust counting back. She is a weak student.

**Host B:** And most people jump straight to the account for.

**Host A:** Almost everyone jumps straight to the account for. We see a child and immediately judge. And Mason argues that learning to stay with the account of, for a little while, is what opens up the possibility of seeing something new. If you have already decided the child is weak, you will not see the clever thing she is doing.

**Host B:** That is very close to Goodwin.

**Host A:** It is close to Goodwin, from a different direction. Goodwin shows how professions shape what their members see. Mason offers a practice for individual teachers to become aware of their own seeing, and to change it. And the American researchers we are about to discuss drew on both.

**Host B:** And there is a reason this matters for the debate.

**Host A:** There is. Because Mason's idea suggests that noticing is not only about knowing more. A teacher can know the whole CGI map and still not see a particular child, because she has already filed that child under a judgement. Noticing is partly knowledge, and partly a habit of attention. And habits of attention can be trained, but slowly, and mostly by practising on real moments, your own or other people's.

**Host B:** Which leads straight to video.

**Host A:** Which leads straight to video.

**Host B:** Sherin and van Es.

**Host A:** Miriam Sherin, at Northwestern, and Elizabeth van Es, at the University of California, Irvine. In the early 2000s, they took Goodwin's idea and applied it to teachers. And their method was simple and turned out to be very influential. Video clubs.

**Host B:** What is a video club?

**Host A:** A group of teachers who meet regularly to watch short clips of video from each other's classrooms, with a facilitator, and talk about what they see. In their 2009 paper in the Journal of Teacher Education, Effects of Video Club Participation on Teachers' Professional Vision, they report on two clubs. One at a suburban middle school, four teachers, meeting seven times across a year. And one at an urban elementary school, seven teachers of fourth and fifth grade, meeting ten times, about an hour each.

**Host B:** And they defined what they were looking for.

**Host A:** They defined professional vision for teachers as the ability to notice and interpret significant features of classroom interactions. And they split it into two parts. Selective attention, meaning what you look at. And knowledge based reasoning, meaning how you make sense of what you see.

**Host B:** And what happened?

**Host A:** In the elementary club, they coded every comment teachers made in the meetings. In the first meeting, about a quarter of the teachers' comments were about students' mathematical thinking. The rest were about the teacher, or about classroom management, or about general things. By the last meeting, ninety two percent of the comments were about students' mathematical thinking.

**Host B:** Twenty five to ninety two.

**Host A:** Twenty five to ninety two. And the way teachers talked changed too. Early on, they mostly described and evaluated. That was a good question. That student was confused. Later, they interpreted. They asked what a student's idea meant, they connected one student's idea to another. In interviews before and after, the share of interpretive comments rose from about a quarter to about a half.

**Host B:** And did it change their teaching?

**Host A:** They also observed the teachers' classrooms early and late in the year, and found signs that it did. They wrote that student ideas that, initially, were typically dismissed by the teachers, later on became the objects of focused analysis. Teachers started treating children's ideas as things to investigate, in class, not just in the video club.

**Host B:** Now the caution.

**Host A:** The caution is that these are small studies. Eleven teachers. No comparison group. And of course a facilitator who cares about student thinking, leading a discussion, will tend to steer the conversation toward student thinking. So some of the change in the meetings is the facilitator's influence. But the classroom observations suggest it went beyond the meetings. And the basic finding, that what teachers notice can change with structured practice, has been replicated many times since.

**Host B:** Now the central paper. Jacobs, Lamb and Philipp.

**Host A:** Victoria Jacobs, Lisa Lamb and Randolph Philipp, at San Diego State University. Professional Noticing of Children's Mathematical Thinking. Journal for Research in Mathematics Education, 2010. And it is one of the most cited papers in mathematics education of the last twenty years.

**Host B:** What is the question?

**Host A:** The question is, what does it actually mean to notice children's mathematical thinking, and how does that ability develop? And they made a crucial decision at the start. They defined professional noticing not as one skill but as three.

**Host B:** Which are?

**Host A:** First, attending to children's strategies. Do you see the mathematically important details of what the child actually did? Second, interpreting children's understandings. Can you say, on the basis of the evidence, what the child understands? And third, deciding how to respond on the basis of children's understandings. Can you choose a next step, in their study a next problem to pose, that makes sense given what this child understands?

**Host B:** Attend, interpret, decide.

**Host A:** Attend, interpret, decide. And they insist that in real teaching these three happen almost all at once. Their phrase is that they happen in the background, almost simultaneously, as if constituting a single, integrated teaching move. But for research, you can pull them apart.

**Host B:** Who did they study?

**Host A:** A hundred and thirty one people, in four groups, chosen to represent different amounts of experience with children's mathematical thinking. Group one, thirty six prospective teachers, college students in their first mathematics course for teachers, no classroom experience. Group two, thirty one experienced elementary teachers, kindergarten to third grade, who were just about to begin professional development in children's mathematical thinking. They had years of teaching experience but no special training. Group three, thirty one experienced teachers who had done two years of that professional development. And group four, thirty three experienced teachers who had done four or more years of it, and had begun to lead professional development for others. They called them emerging teacher leaders.

**Host B:** And the professional development was?

**Host A:** Cognitively Guided Instruction. The programme from episode three. Which is important. This is not a test of noticing in the abstract. It is a test of noticing through the CGI lens.

**Host B:** And the tasks?

**Host A:** Two tasks. This is where I want to go into detail, because the tasks are wonderful. The first was a nine minute video clip from a first and second grade classroom. The class is doing a lunch count. And the problem is this. We have nineteen children, and seven are having hot lunch. How many are having cold lunch?

**Host B:** Nineteen minus seven. Twelve.

**Host A:** Twelve. And in the video, you see several children solve it in different ways. Two children, Katie and Sam, count back seven from nineteen on their fingers. Eighteen, seventeen, sixteen, fifteen, fourteen, thirteen, twelve. Two others, Annette and Maureen, draw nineteen tally marks and then cross out, or erase, seven of them, and count what is left. And a child named Sunny uses a counting frame, a frame with beads, in a more efficient way, working with the numbers rather than counting every single one.

**Host B:** So three levels of strategy.

**Host A:** Exactly. In CGI terms, Annette and Maureen are direct modelling. They represent every object. Katie and Sam are counting. They do not need to represent every object, they keep track of a count. And Sunny is doing something closer to using number relationships. And the teacher watching the video is asked to describe what the children did, and what they understood.

**Host B:** And the second task?

**Host A:** The second was written work. Second graders solving this problem. Todd has six bags of M and M's. Each bag has forty three M and M's. How many M and M's does Todd have?

**Host B:** Six times forty three. Two hundred fifty eight.

**Host A:** Two hundred fifty eight. Which is a hard problem for second graders, who have not been taught multiplication. And the participants saw three pieces of written work. Alexis drew six groups of forty three tally marks, that is two hundred and fifty eight little lines, and counted them all.

**Host B:** That is heroic.

**Host A:** It is heroic. Cassandra broke the numbers apart by place value. Six forties and six threes. Two hundred forty plus eighteen. But she made a small slip along the way and got two hundred fifty two instead of two hundred fifty eight. And Josie skip counted by forties, forty, eighty, a hundred twenty, all the way to two hundred forty, and then counted on the threes to get to two hundred fifty eight.

**Host B:** So which child understands the most?

**Host A:** That is exactly the question. And here is the trap. Cassandra got the wrong answer. Alexis and Josie got the right answer. A teacher who looks only at answers ranks Cassandra last. But Cassandra's strategy, splitting by place value, is arguably the most sophisticated of the three. Her error was a small slip, not a misunderstanding. And Alexis, who got the right answer, used the least efficient strategy, counting every single one.

**Host B:** So the task is designed to catch answer focused noticing.

**Host A:** It is beautifully designed for that. And then they asked the participants three questions about each task, one for each skill. Describe in detail what you think each child did. Describe what you learned about these children's understandings. And pretend you are the teacher of these children, what problem or problems might you pose next?

**Host B:** And the results.

**Host A:** Let me give the numbers, because they are striking. For attending, did the participant describe the mathematically important details of the strategies? Forty two percent of the prospective teachers did. Sixty five percent of the experienced teachers without training. Ninety percent of the teachers with two years of professional development. And ninety seven percent of the emerging leaders.

**Host B:** So experience alone gets you from forty to sixty five.

**Host A:** Experience alone gets you a fair way on attending. And two years of focused professional development gets you almost all the way. Now interpreting. Here they looked for robust evidence, meaning the participant interpreted children's understanding in a detailed way that was consistent with the research on children's thinking. Prospective teachers, zero percent. Experienced teachers without training, sixteen percent. Two years of professional development, twenty six percent. Four years plus, seventy six percent.

**Host B:** Zero percent.

**Host A:** Zero percent of the prospective teachers showed robust interpreting. And only sixteen percent of experienced teachers did. And now deciding how to respond. Robust evidence of choosing a next problem that built on the children's understanding. Prospective teachers, zero percent. Experienced teachers, three percent. Two years of professional development, nineteen percent. Four years plus, sixty four percent.

**Host B:** Three percent of experienced teachers.

**Host A:** Three percent. These are teachers who had been teaching young children for years. And almost none of them, in this task, could choose a next problem based on what the children understood. Most chose problems that were generically harder or easier, or that practised the same thing, without building on the specific strategies they had seen.

**Host B:** That is a very sobering number.

**Host A:** It is a sobering number. And the authors draw some careful conclusions. First, they say that noticing effectively is both complex and challenging. Expertise in attending to children's strategies, they write, is neither something adults routinely know how to do, nor, as their data show, something that simply arrives with years in the classroom.

**Host B:** Experience is not enough.

**Host A:** Experience alone helped with attending, and a bit with interpreting, but hardly at all with deciding. The big gains came with sustained professional development. And here is the second conclusion, the hopeful one. Given time, this expertise can be learned. But the time is long. Attending improved within two years. Interpreting and deciding kept improving through four years and beyond.

**Host B:** Four years.

**Host A:** Four or more years of sustained professional development, to get to the point where two thirds of teachers can reliably choose a next problem based on a child's thinking. Which tells you something important about what responsive teaching asks of a school system.

**Host B:** Kennedy would have something to say about that.

**Host A:** Kennedy would say, look how long it takes. Look how much support it needs. And remember her review of professional development, where the programmes that worked tended to be the ones that helped teachers develop insight, but where even the best effects were modest. Four years of professional development is a very large investment. And most teachers in America never get anything like it.

**Host B:** And the critics would say?

**Host A:** The critics would say several things, and they are fair. First, this is a cross sectional study. Different people in each group, not the same people followed over time. The teachers who stayed in professional development for four years and became leaders may have been different from the start. More interested. More able. So some of the difference between the groups might be selection, not learning.

**Host B:** Second.

**Host A:** Second, it measures noticing through written responses to a video and to student work. Not noticing in a live classroom, with twenty five children and a fire drill. Writing a thoughtful paragraph about Cassandra at your kitchen table is not the same as seeing Cassandra's strategy in four seconds while two other children need you.

**Host B:** Third.

**Host A:** Third, and the authors are clear about this themselves, it measures noticing through one particular lens, the lens of CGI. The participants with CGI training were, in a sense, being tested on the framework they had been taught. A Direct Instruction teacher might notice different things, important things, that this study would not score.

**Host B:** And fourth?

**Host A:** Fourth, and this is the big one for Sold a Story. The study does not show that better noticing leads to better learning for children. It shows that teachers can learn to notice. Whether children of teachers who notice better learn more is a separate question, and the evidence on that is much thinner.

**Host B:** And yet.

**Host A:** And yet. The finding that three percent of experienced teachers chose next problems that built on children's understanding is, I think, important for both sides. For the responsive side, it says do not assume teachers can do this. They mostly can not, without a lot of help. For the Direct Instruction side, it says, well, it is a skill that can be learned, and if it matters, the question is whether we invest in it.

**Host B:** One more thing from this paper.

**Host A:** A small phrase, but I love it. In discussing how perception works, they cite the idea that we construct what we see. We do not simply receive what is in front of us. We construct it, using what we know. A teacher who knows the CGI map constructs a different picture of Cassandra's paper than a teacher who does not. And that is Goodwin again. Professional vision.

**Host B:** Let us hear what this sounds like. The lunch count, in two classrooms.

**Host A:** Two short scenes, invented but faithful to the research. First, a teacher whose vision is trained on answers.

**Teacher:** Nineteen children. Seven hot lunch. How many cold lunch? Annette?

**Child:** Twelve.

**Teacher:** Twelve. Good. Katie?

**Child:** Twelve.

**Teacher:** Good. Everybody got twelve? Wonderful. Let us move on to the calendar.

**Host B:** Nothing wrong with that.

**Host A:** Nothing wrong at all. The children got the right answer. The lesson moves on. But the teacher has learned nothing about the difference between Annette, who needed to draw nineteen marks, and Katie, who could count back on her fingers. And tomorrow, she will give them both the same problem again.

**Host B:** And the second classroom.

**Teacher:** Nineteen children. Seven hot lunch. How many cold lunch? Annette, how did you figure it out?

**Child:** I drew nineteen lines. Then I rubbed out seven. Then I counted the ones that were left. Twelve.

**Teacher:** You made all nineteen children, and took away the hot lunch ones. Katie, you got twelve too. Did you do it the same way?

**Child:** No. I just started at nineteen and went down. Eighteen, seventeen, sixteen, fifteen, fourteen, thirteen, twelve.

**Teacher:** How did you know when to stop?

**Child:** I put up a finger every time. When I had seven fingers, I stopped.

**Teacher:** So your fingers were keeping track of the seven, and your voice was keeping track of the number. Sunny?

**Child:** I moved the beads. Ten and nine is nineteen. I took seven from the nine. Two left. Ten and two is twelve.

**Teacher:** Interesting. You did not take the seven from the ten. You took it from the nine. Annette, what do you think of Sunny's way?

**Host B:** She heard three different things.

**Host A:** She heard three levels of strategy. Annette is direct modelling. Katie is counting back, with her fingers keeping track of the seven, which is a real cognitive achievement, holding two counts at once. And Sunny is using place value, ten and nine, taking seven from the nine. And now the deciding part. What does she pose next? For Annette, maybe a problem with smaller numbers where counting back is tempting. For Katie, maybe a problem where counting back is slow, like nineteen minus fifteen, so she might discover counting up. For Sunny, maybe a problem that crosses ten, like twenty three minus seven, so that she can not just take it from the ones.

**Host B:** And that is the three percent.

**Host A:** That is the three percent. Choosing a different next problem for each child, based on what you saw. And you can hear why it is hard. The teacher needs the CGI map in her head. She needs to hear the details in real time. And she needs to remember, after the lesson, what each child did.

**Host B:** And that second classroom took longer.

**Host A:** It took longer. Kennedy again. Every minute spent exposing student thinking is a minute not spent on something else.

**Host B:** So a teacher has noticed. Then what?

**Host A:** That is where Jacobs and Empson come in, and Annie Keith and Ethan. Their 2016 paper in the journal ZDM, Responding to Children's Mathematical Thinking in the Moment: An Emerging Framework of Teaching Moves.

**Host B:** What did they study?

**Host A:** One teacher, studied intensely. Annie Keith, twenty nine years of experience, teaching a mixed class of fourth and fifth graders, twenty five children. They recorded two class lessons, each well over an hour, and a series of one on one interviews in which Ms. Keith worked with five children on fraction story problems. Seven problems in all. And they analysed every move she made.

**Host B:** Why one teacher?

**Host A:** Because they were trying to describe what expert responsiveness looks like, in detail, before trying to measure it in others. It is the kind of study you do at the start of building a framework. And they were open that it is an emerging framework.

**Host B:** And the framework?

**Host A:** Four categories of teaching moves. First, ensuring that the child is making sense of the story problem. Before anything else, does the child understand the situation? Who is sharing what with whom? Second, exploring details of the child's existing strategy. This is the biggest category, and it has five parts. Posing starter questions. Pressing the child for a detailed explanation. Probing the child's representation to connect it to the story. Questioning the child about quantities and how they relate. And inviting the child to think ahead.

**Host B:** Which is Ethan.

**Host A:** Which is Ethan. Inviting the child to think ahead, before carrying out a step. Third category, encouraging the child to consider other strategies. And fourth, inviting the child to generate symbolic notation, to write an equation for what they did.

**Host B:** What strikes me is that almost none of these is telling.

**Host A:** Almost none of them is telling. And that is deliberate. But it is not the same as not teaching. Every one of these moves is a deliberate push. A press for detail. A probe. An invitation. The child is doing the mathematics, but the teacher is shaping where it goes.

**Host B:** And there is a principle they emphasise.

**Host A:** There is a principle that runs through the whole paper. The child keeps control. They write that it is critical that children, like Ethan, have the option to reject the teacher's suggestions. And then, otherwise, children lose control of the problem solving process.

**Host B:** Why does that matter so much?

**Host A:** Because once the teacher takes over, the child is no longer reasoning. The child is following. And the teacher's thinking replaces the child's. And this is exactly the failure that Jacobs and others had seen over and over in less experienced teachers. A child is struggling, and the teacher, out of kindness, steps in and shows the next step. And the child does it. And the teacher thinks the child has learned it. But it was the teacher's step.

**Host B:** That is the opposite of what Direct Instruction would say.

**Host A:** It is a direct clash. In Direct Instruction, the teacher models. I do, we do, you do. The teacher's thinking is supposed to come first, clearly, so the child can copy it, and then the support is gradually removed. And Rosenshine's principles, which we discussed in episode one, say the same. Model. Guide practice. Check for understanding.

**Host B:** So who is right?

**Host A:** I think it depends on what you are trying to teach. If the goal is a procedure, a fixed method, then modelling is efficient. The child needs to see the method and practise it. If the goal is reasoning, the ability to figure out a method for yourself, then modelling can short circuit the very thing you are trying to develop. Ethan does not need to see someone else solve six bars among eight children. He needs to solve it himself and then see his solution more clearly.

**Host B:** And Jacobs and Empson say something about perfection.

**Host A:** They say something humble. We do not believe that there are any perfect comments or questions. There is no script for responsive teaching. The same move can be brilliant with one child and wrong with another. What makes a move good is how it fits what the child is doing at that moment.

**Host B:** Which makes it very hard to scale.

**Host A:** Which makes it very hard to scale. And very hard to evaluate. You can not check whether a teacher is doing it right by checking whether she said the right words. And they also noticed something about Ms. Keith that is easy to skip over. They wrote that a caring and respectful stance was evident throughout her interviews and class lessons. The moves are not just techniques. They come from a stance toward the child. A belief that the child's thinking is worth exploring.

**Host B:** And the interviews and the classroom were consistent.

**Host A:** That was an important finding. They wrote that their data indicated a high degree of authenticity between teaching moves in interviews and class lessons. Meaning she was not just a skilled one on one tutor. She did the same kinds of things with the whole class. Which matters, because a common objection is that this kind of responsiveness only works one on one.

**Host B:** But it is one teacher with twenty nine years of experience.

**Host A:** It is one extraordinary teacher. And so the honest question, again, is what it takes to get ordinary teachers closer to her. And the Jacobs, Lamb and Philipp numbers say it takes years.

**Host B:** Now to science. Hammer.

**Host A:** David Hammer, a physicist turned education researcher, now at Tufts, and a group of colleagues across science and mathematics. They developed a version of responsive teaching that is in some ways more radical than anything we have discussed so far.

**Host B:** More radical how?

**Host A:** In CGI, the teacher knows where she is going. The curriculum goal is clear. Children should learn to add and subtract, to understand place value. The responsiveness is in how you get there. Hammer and his colleagues push further. They argue that teachers should sometimes adapt the goals themselves, in response to what children think.

**Host B:** Change the goals.
