# Responsive Math Teaching 6. Orchestrating the Discussion

**Host A:** Here is a problem from a middle school classroom. A fourth grade class needs five leaves each day to feed its two caterpillars. How many leaves would they need each day for twelve caterpillars?

**Host B:** Five leaves for two caterpillars. Twelve caterpillars is six times as many. So thirty leaves.

**Host A:** Thirty leaves. And you did it with a scale factor. Two caterpillars times six is twelve, so five leaves times six is thirty. Now imagine a room of eleven and twelve year olds working on it in small groups. What do you get?

**Host B:** Lots of different ways.

**Host A:** Lots of different ways. Some children draw twelve little caterpillars, put them in pairs, and draw five leaves next to each pair, and count the leaves. Some make a table. Two caterpillars, five leaves. Four caterpillars, ten leaves. Six, fifteen. All the way to twelve, thirty. Some work out how many leaves one caterpillar needs. Two and a half. And multiply by twelve. And some children do something else.

**Host B:** Something wrong.

**Host A:** Something wrong, and very common. They say, twelve caterpillars is ten more than two caterpillars. So we need ten more leaves than five. Fifteen leaves.

**Host B:** Additive thinking.

**Host A:** Additive thinking. Treating a multiplication situation as an addition situation. It is one of the biggest obstacles in the whole middle school curriculum, because ratio and proportion depend on getting past it.

**Host B:** So the teacher has all these solutions. What does she do?

**Host A:** That is the question. And in a famous paper, the researchers describe a teacher who does what many well meaning teachers did, and still do. He walks around while the groups work. And then he invites groups up to the front, one after another, to share how they solved it. The picture group. The table group. The unit rate group. And at some point a group presents the additive solution, fifteen leaves. And he thanks each group. And says, what a lot of different ways to solve this problem. And the lesson ends.

**Host B:** That sounds like a nice lesson.

**Host A:** It sounds like a nice lesson. Children were working on a real problem. They talked. They shared. The teacher did not lecture. Every box on the reform checklist is ticked. And the researchers say that it was, in an important sense, a failure.

**Host B:** Why?

**Host A:** Because nobody connected anything. Nobody compared the picture with the table, or the table with the unit rate, to see that they were all doing the same multiplication in different forms. And, worst of all, nobody confronted the additive answer. It just sat there, alongside thirty, as one of the many different ways. So some children went home thinking that fifteen and thirty were both fine answers. Different strategies. And the mathematical point of the lesson, the difference between adding and multiplying, was never made.

**Host B:** The researchers called it show and tell.

**Host A:** They called it show and tell. Like in kindergarten, where children bring something from home and show it to the class, one after another. Every child gets a turn. Nothing connects. And they argued that a lot of reform mathematics teaching in America had turned into exactly that.

**Host B:** Welcome back to Books on Pods, and to part six of our series on responsive mathematics teaching, as a response to the second season of Sold a Story. Today, orchestrating the discussion.

**Host A:** And that paper is the centrepiece. Stein, Engle, Smith and Hughes. Orchestrating Productive Mathematical Discussions: Five Practices for Helping Teachers Move Beyond Show and Tell. Mathematical Thinking and Learning, 2008.

**Host B:** Recap first. Episode one, Sold a Story's argument, Direct Instruction and Follow Through, and its critics. Episode two, Mary Kennedy and the five persistent problems. Episode three, Cognitively Guided Instruction. Episode four, Lampert and Ball and the knowledge teaching requires. Episode five, professional noticing, and the finding that very few experienced teachers could choose a next problem based on a child's thinking, without years of support.

**Host A:** And last time we ended with the problem of scale. Annie Keith can follow Ethan's thinking beautifully, one on one. But how do you do that with a whole class? If every child's thinking matters, and every child shares, you get show and tell.

**Host B:** And show and tell is exactly what the critics of reform mathematics have always said happens.

**Host A:** Exactly. And this is important for our series, because I think the show and tell problem is one of the strongest pieces of evidence for the Sold a Story critique. Not that responsive teaching can not work, but that it very often degrades, in ordinary classrooms, into something that looks responsive and teaches very little. And the responsive tradition's own researchers saw this, named it, and tried to fix it.

**Host B:** So today. The roots, in the 1990s, of the idea that a classroom discussion has norms. Then Kazemi and Stipek in 2001, comparing four classrooms and what they called press for conceptual thinking. Then Mary Kay Stein's earlier work on mathematical tasks, and what happens to good tasks once they enter a real classroom. Then the five practices paper in detail. Then Franke and Webb's study of teacher questioning. Then Railside, Jo Boaler's study of a high school that taught through group work, and the controversy that followed. And finally, Lampert again, and the idea of rehearsal. How do you teach a new teacher to orchestrate?

**Host A:** And Kennedy's lens throughout. Because orchestrating a discussion is where all five of her problems collide at once.

**Host B:** Start with norms.

**Host A:** In the late 1980s and 1990s, Paul Cobb, Erna Yackel and their colleagues worked with second grade classrooms in Indiana, and later elsewhere, trying to understand how classrooms where children discuss mathematics actually work. And they came up with a distinction that turned out to be very useful.

**Host B:** Social norms and sociomathematical norms.

**Host A:** Social norms are the general rules of discussion in a classroom. You explain your thinking. You listen to others. You can disagree, respectfully. And those norms could apply to any subject. Any classroom with good discussion has them.

**Host B:** And sociomathematical norms?

**Host A:** Sociomathematical norms are the norms that are specific to mathematics. And in a 1996 paper in the Journal for Research in Mathematics Education, Yackel and Cobb described some of them. What counts as a mathematically different solution? Not just different words for the same method, but a genuinely different approach. What counts as a sophisticated solution? An efficient solution? And what counts as an acceptable mathematical explanation?

**Host B:** Why does that matter?

**Host A:** Because those are exactly the things that separate a productive mathematics discussion from show and tell. In show and tell, every solution is welcome, and there is no sense that some are more sophisticated than others, or that an explanation has to actually justify something. In a classroom with strong sociomathematical norms, children learn that a different solution has to be really different, that an explanation has to say why, not just what, and that efficiency matters.

**Host B:** And those norms are created by the teacher.

**Host A:** They are created over time, by the teacher and the children together, through hundreds of small exchanges. When a child says, I did it a different way, and the teacher asks, how is that different from what Maria did, the teacher is building a norm. When a child explains by just listing steps, and the teacher asks, but why did you do that, she is building a norm. And this is very close to what Lampert described in her fifth grade class. I think, instead of it is. I want to revise my thinking. Those are norms.

**Host B:** And the norms are invisible.

**Host A:** The norms are invisible to a visitor, which is part of what makes this kind of teaching so hard to copy. A visitor sees children explaining and debating. The visitor does not see the months of work that made it possible.

**Host B:** Kazemi and Stipek.

**Host A:** Elham Kazemi and Deborah Stipek. Promoting Conceptual Thinking in Four Upper Elementary Mathematics Classrooms. The Elementary School Journal, 2001.

**Host B:** Four classrooms.

**Host A:** Four classrooms, fourth and fifth grade, all of them teachers who were trying to teach in a reform way. All of them used group work. All of them asked children to explain. All of them had the surface features. And Kazemi and Stipek wanted to know what distinguished the classrooms where children were really thinking conceptually from the ones where they were not.

**Host B:** And what was it?

**Host A:** They called it press for conceptual thinking. In some classrooms, the press was high. In others, it was low. And the surface features were the same. In both kinds of classroom, children shared their solutions and the teacher asked them to explain. The difference was in what the teacher did with the explanation.

**Host B:** Give the contrast.

**Host A:** In a low press classroom, a child describes the steps she took, and the teacher says, great, thank you, who did it differently? The explanation is a description. The teacher praises effort and participation. Errors are corrected or passed over quickly, and kindly.

**Host B:** And in a high press classroom?

**Host A:** In a high press classroom, a child describes the steps, and the teacher asks, why did you do that? How do you know that works? Can someone explain how her way is related to his way? And errors are treated as interesting. The class looks at them together.

**Host B:** And they described the norms of the high press classrooms.

**Host A:** They identified four. I will paraphrase them closely. First, an explanation has to be a mathematical argument, not just a description of a procedure. Second, mathematical thinking involves understanding how multiple strategies relate to each other, not just listing them. Third, errors are opportunities. A chance to rethink a problem, explore a contradiction, try another strategy. And fourth, when children work together, each child is accountable, and the group has to reach agreement through mathematical argument, not by voting or by deferring to the fastest child.

**Host B:** The second one is the cure for show and tell.

**Host A:** The second one is exactly the cure for show and tell. Not just sharing strategies, but relating them. And Kazemi and Stipek were careful to say that both kinds of classroom were warm. Both teachers cared about children. The low press teachers were not bad teachers. They had taken on the outward forms of reform teaching without the mathematical press that gives it its point.

**Host B:** Which is Mrs. Oublier.

**Host A:** Which is Mrs. Oublier, from episode two. David Cohen's teacher who believed she had transformed her teaching, and had in fact layered new activities on top of an old conception of mathematics. Kazemi and Stipek's low press classrooms are a gentler version of the same thing. The activities changed. The mathematical demand did not.

**Host B:** And Kennedy would say?

**Host A:** Kennedy would say that the low press teachers were solving her problems in a particular way. They were enlisting participation, very successfully. Children were engaged, sharing, happy. And they were containing behaviour. But they were not portraying the curriculum with much mathematical depth, and they were exposing student thinking without doing anything with it. And she would point out that pressing for conceptual thinking is risky. When you ask a child why, and the child can not answer, there is a moment of discomfort. When you put an error in front of the class, a child might feel embarrassed. Remember from her study that almost every teacher abhorred the idea of telling a child he was wrong. High press is, in a sense, the willingness to tolerate that discomfort for the sake of the mathematics.

**Host B:** Now Mary Kay Stein's earlier work. Tasks.

**Host A:** Before the five practices, Stein was known for something else. In the early 1990s she was part of a project called QUASAR, based at the University of Pittsburgh, which worked with middle schools in economically disadvantaged communities, trying to bring ambitious mathematics teaching to children who rarely got it. And one of the things the QUASAR researchers studied closely was mathematical tasks. The problems teachers give children.

**Host B:** What did they find?

**Host A:** They developed a way of classifying tasks by their cognitive demand. And Margaret Smith and Stein later laid it out for teachers in a widely read 1998 article. There are four levels. At the bottom, memorisation. Recall a fact. At the next level, procedures without connections. Carry out an algorithm, with no attention to why it works. Then procedures with connections. Use a procedure in a way that builds understanding of the underlying concept. And at the top, what they called doing mathematics. Complex, non algorithmic thinking, where there is no obvious path and children have to explore.

**Host B:** And the finding?

**Host A:** The finding, from studies by Stein, Grover and Henningsen in 1996, and Henningsen and Stein in 1997, was that high level tasks often declined during the lesson. A teacher would start with a doing mathematics task. Children would struggle. And the teacher, or the children, would reduce the demand. The teacher would give a hint that turned the task into a procedure. Or tell the children which method to use. Or accept answers without explanations. Or the task would take too long and the teacher would move on. And by the end of the lesson, a doing mathematics task had become a procedures without connections task.

**Host B:** And when did tasks maintain their demand?

**Host A:** When the teacher scaffolded without taking over. When there was enough time. When the teacher pressed for explanations and meaning. When the teacher or a capable student modelled high level performance. And when the task built on what children already knew. And interestingly, that list is quite close to Kazemi and Stipek's high press.

**Host B:** And this connects to Direct Instruction how?

**Host A:** This is important. Direct Instruction tasks are, by design, mostly at the lower levels of this framework. Recall, and procedures. Carefully sequenced, practised to mastery. And a Direct Instruction designer would say, that is deliberate. You build fluency first. Higher level thinking depends on fluent lower level skills.

**Host B:** And the QUASAR researchers?

**Host A:** The QUASAR researchers argued that children in poor communities, in particular, were too often given only low level tasks, and that this was one reason they did not develop as mathematical thinkers. They argued that these children could do high level tasks, if they were supported. And they found that in classrooms where high level tasks were maintained, students made the greatest gains on a test of reasoning and problem solving.

**Host B:** So the equity argument cuts both ways.

**Host A:** It cuts both ways, and we will spend time on it in the final episode. One side says, disadvantaged children need explicit, structured instruction most of all. The other side says, disadvantaged children have been given only drill for decades, and deserve the same intellectual demand as anyone else.

**Host B:** Now. The five practices.

**Host A:** Mary Kay Stein, Randi Engle, Margaret Smith and Elizabeth Hughes, 2008. And it starts from a real puzzle. The QUASAR work had shown that high level tasks matter, and that teachers need to maintain their demand. And many teachers had learned to choose good tasks, to let children work on them, and to have children share. But then what? The end of the lesson, the discussion, was where things fell apart. Show and tell.

**Host B:** Why is the discussion so hard?

**Host A:** Because, as they point out, the teacher is trying to do two things at once that pull in opposite directions. On one hand, she wants to give students authority over their own ideas. She wants them to reason, to share, to own their thinking. On the other hand, she is responsible for making sure the class gets to the mathematical point. She has a goal. And if she gives students too much authority, the discussion wanders. If she takes too much control, she is just lecturing through the children.

**Host B:** Lampert's dilemma.

**Host A:** Lampert's dilemma, exactly. Student authority versus teacher accountability to the mathematics. And Stein and her colleagues wanted to give ordinary teachers a way to manage that dilemma that did not depend on being Magdalene Lampert. A set of practices that could be learned.

**Host B:** And the key move was?

**Host A:** The key move was to shift a lot of the work to before the lesson. Improvisation in the moment is very hard. But much of what looks like improvisation in expert teachers is actually preparation. So they proposed five practices, and the first one happens before the children even see the task.

**Host B:** Practice one.

**Host A:** Anticipating. Before the lesson, the teacher works the task herself, in as many ways as she can, and tries to anticipate how her students will approach it. What strategies will they use? What errors will they make? Which strategies are most important for the mathematical goal? For the leaves and caterpillars, she would anticipate the picture, the table, the unit rate, the scale factor, and, crucially, the additive error.

**Host B:** So the fifteen leaves does not surprise her.

**Host A:** The fifteen leaves does not surprise her. She has planned for it. She has maybe even decided in advance that she wants it to come up, because it is the key to the whole lesson.

**Host B:** Practice two.

**Host A:** Monitoring. While the students are working, the teacher walks around and pays close attention to what each group is doing. Not just are they on task, but which of the anticipated strategies are they using, and are there any she did not anticipate. And many teachers who use the framework carry a chart, a list of anticipated strategies, and note down which group is doing which.

**Host B:** Which is noticing, from last episode.

**Host A:** Which is professional noticing, in a whole class setting. Attend, interpret. And the chart helps, because it gives the teacher's attention a structure.

**Host B:** Practice three.

**Host A:** Selecting. Based on what she saw, the teacher selects which students will present. Not volunteers. Not everybody. Specific students, chosen because their work will help the class reach the mathematical goal.

**Host B:** That is a big shift from show and tell.

**Host A:** It is the biggest shift. In show and tell, the teacher asks, who wants to share? And whoever volunteers gets up. Often it is the same confident children every time. And the solutions come up in a random order. Selecting means the teacher decides which ideas the class needs to see.

**Host B:** Practice four.

**Host A:** Sequencing. The teacher decides in what order the selected students will present. And the order matters. One common approach is to start with the most concrete, accessible strategy, like the picture, so that everyone can follow, and move toward the more abstract and efficient ones, like the scale factor. Another is to start with a common error, like the additive answer, so the class can confront it early. Or to put two contrasting strategies next to each other so the class can compare them.

**Host B:** And practice five.

**Host A:** Connecting. The teacher helps the class see the relationships between the different strategies, and between the strategies and the key mathematical ideas. How is the picture related to the table? Where in the table can you see the unit rate? Why does the additive strategy give the wrong answer, and what would happen if we checked it with four caterpillars? Connecting is where the mathematics happens.

**Host B:** Anticipating, monitoring, selecting, sequencing, connecting.

**Host A:** And in later versions, Smith and Stein added what they called practice zero. Setting goals and choosing the task. Because you can not anticipate, select, or connect without knowing what mathematical idea you are aiming for.

**Host B:** That is a very structured approach.

**Host A:** It is a very structured approach. And that is, I think, the most important thing about it for our series. The five practices are, in a sense, the responsive tradition admitting that pure improvisation does not work for most teachers. That responsive teaching needs structure. Planning. Routines. A script of sorts, though not for what the teacher says, but for how the teacher prepares and decides.

**Host B:** Would Engelmann approve?

**Host A:** I suspect he would say, you have discovered that teachers need structure, and that is a start. Now go further. Write down the anticipated strategies and the right sequence for every task, test them with real children, and give the teachers a program. And in fact, curriculum materials designed after this paper often do exactly that. They include anticipated student strategies, and suggested sequences, and connecting questions, in the teacher's guide.

**Host B:** So the two traditions converge.

**Host A:** They converge partly, on the idea that structure and planning are essential. They still differ on what the structure is for. Engelmann's structure is designed to prevent errors. The five practices are designed to use errors. Engelmann's structure fixes the teacher's words. The five practices fix the teacher's preparation and leave the words open.

**Host B:** And the authors are clear this is not a script.

**Host A:** Very clear. The practices are meant to support teacher judgement, not replace it. Monitoring might reveal a strategy the teacher did not anticipate, and she might decide on the spot that it is the most important thing in the room. The practices are a frame for improvisation, not a substitute for it.

**Host B:** And the evidence?

**Host A:** Here we need to be honest. The 2008 paper is a conceptual paper. It describes the practices, illustrates them with cases, and argues for them. It is not an experiment. And the five practices became hugely popular, a bestselling book for teachers, used in professional development all over the country, mostly on the strength of the argument and the cases, and on teachers' experience that they help. Direct evidence that teachers who use the five practices produce greater student learning is limited.

**Host B:** That is a pattern in this series.

**Host A:** It is a pattern. The responsive tradition has rich descriptions, strong theory, and a lot of professional enthusiasm, and much less evidence of the Follow Through kind. Large, controlled, comparisons of student outcomes. And Sold a Story's second season, as we discussed in episode one, leans heavily on exactly that difference.

**Host B:** Let us hear it. The leaves and caterpillars, orchestrated.

**Host A:** Here is an invented scene, built to show the five practices at work. The teacher has anticipated, monitored, selected and sequenced. Now she is connecting.

**Teacher:** I asked three groups to share today, and I want you to watch for how their ways are the same and how they are different. Jordan's group first.

**Child:** We drew twelve caterpillars. We circled them in twos. Each two gets five leaves. So we counted. Five, ten, fifteen, twenty, twenty five, thirty. Thirty leaves.

**Teacher:** How many circles did you make?

**Child:** Six.

**Teacher:** Keep that six in your mind, everyone. Now Priya's group.

**Child:** We made a table. Two caterpillars, five leaves. Four caterpillars, ten leaves. Six, fifteen. Eight, twenty. Ten, twenty five. Twelve, thirty.

**Teacher:** How many rows did your table have?

**Child:** Six.

**Teacher:** Six again. Where is Jordan's six in Priya's table?

**Children:** The rows. Each row is one more circle.

**Teacher:** So the circles and the rows are doing the same job. Now, I want to show you one more answer, because I think a lot of people might have thought this, and it is worth looking at. Marcus's group got fifteen. Marcus, can you tell us how?

**Child:** Twelve caterpillars is ten more than two. So we need ten more leaves. Five plus ten is fifteen.

**Teacher:** That makes sense as an idea. Let us test it. If Marcus's way works, how many leaves would four caterpillars need?

**Child:** Four is two more than two. So two more leaves. Seven.

**Teacher:** Priya, what does your table say for four caterpillars?

**Child:** Ten.

**Teacher:** So we have a disagreement. Seven or ten. Who can say which one makes sense, with the caterpillars?

**Child:** Ten. Because two caterpillars eat five. Two more caterpillars eat five more. Not two more.

**Teacher:** Marcus, what do you think now?

**Child:** I added the caterpillars to the leaves. But the leaves go up by five for every two caterpillars. Not one for one. I want to change mine.

**Host B:** That is the confrontation the show and tell lesson missed.

**Host A:** That is it. And notice what made it possible. The teacher anticipated the additive error. She looked for it while monitoring. She selected Marcus deliberately. She sequenced it after the picture and the table, so that the class already had a tool, the table, to test it with. And she connected by asking about four caterpillars, a case where the two methods give visibly different answers. And Marcus, like Arthur in Lampert's class, revised his own thinking, for a reason, in public.

**Host B:** And she did not say he was wrong.

**Host A:** She did not say he was wrong. She said it makes sense as an idea, and let's test it. The class did the refuting. That is Lampert's principle, not ratifying, made practical for an ordinary teacher with a plan.

**Host B:** But it still took a whole lesson.

**Host A:** It still took a whole lesson. And a Direct Instruction designer might say, you could have prevented the additive error in five minutes by explicitly teaching ratio tables with carefully chosen examples. And that is a fair argument. The responsive answer is that the additive error is deep, and if you merely prevent it in this context, it comes back in the next. Children have to confront it and understand why it fails. Whether that is true is an empirical question, and honestly, not one that has been settled.

**Host B:** Franke and Webb. Questioning.

**Host A:** Megan Franke, Noreen Webb and colleagues at UCLA. Teacher Questioning to Elicit Students' Mathematical Thinking in Elementary School Classrooms. Journal of Teacher Education, 2009. And this is a small study with a very precise finding.

**Host B:** Who did they study?

**Host A:** Three elementary teachers, two second grade and one third grade, in a large urban district in Southern California. All three had more than a year of CGI professional development focused on algebraic reasoning. So these were teachers already committed to eliciting student thinking. And the researchers videotaped two sessions in each classroom and analysed sixty six segments of whole class discussion.

**Host B:** What were they looking at?

**Host A:** What happens after a child gives an initial explanation. Does the teacher follow up? How? And does the child then explain more fully and correctly?

**Host B:** And?

**Host A:** First, the teachers almost always asked children to explain. In ninety eight percent of segments. These classrooms had strong norms of explanation. And the teachers asked follow up questions in seventy six percent of segments. But here is the first finding. In their words, follow up questions, however, did not guarantee further student explanation.

**Host B:** So asking more is not enough.

**Host A:** Asking more is not enough. It matters what kind of question. They classified follow ups into types. General questions, like can you tell me more, or how did you get that. Specific questions, which target a particular part of what the child said, like where did the four come from. Probing sequences, which are more than two related specific questions in a row, digging into one part of the child's thinking. And leading questions, which steer the child toward a particular answer.

**Host B:** And which worked?

**Host A:** Probing sequences led to further explanation in all fourteen cases where they were used. Single specific questions in twelve of eighteen. General questions in eight of ten. And leading questions in only two of six.

**Host B:** And for getting to a correct explanation, when the first one was wrong or incomplete?

**Host A:** That is the crucial number. When a child's first explanation was incorrect or incomplete, probing sequences most often helped the child reach a correct and complete one. Six of nine cases. Single specific questions did it in one of nine. General questions in one of seven.

**Host B:** So it is persistence.

**Host A:** It is persistence on a specific point. They write that uncovering details of students' strategies often required multiple specific questions. And that single questions, whether specific or general, were not always sufficient. You have to stay with the child, ask about this number, then that step, then how those connect.

**Host B:** And leading questions?

**Host A:** Their verdict on leading questions is sharp. Leading questions did not provide opportunities for students to build on their own understanding. Which is exactly what Jacobs and Empson said. When the teacher leads, the child follows, and the thinking is the teacher's.

**Host B:** This is a small study.

**Host A:** It is very small. Three teachers. Sixty six segments. Correlational. But it gives a precise picture of one of the micro skills of responsive teaching. And it fits with what Kazemi and Stipek found about press. High press, in practice, looks like probing sequences. Not one why. Several focused questions in a row.

**Host B:** And Kennedy?

**Host A:** Kennedy would notice the cost. Every probing sequence with one child is time when twenty other children are waiting. And her problem of containing behaviour means that the longer the teacher stays with one child, the greater the risk that the room drifts. The skilled teacher has to judge how long she can probe before she loses the class. Which is another Lampert dilemma.

**Host B:** Now Railside.

**Host A:** Jo Boaler and Megan Staples. Creating Mathematical Futures through an Equitable Teaching Approach: The Case of Railside School. Teachers College Record, 2008. And this is the study in this series that has been fought over most bitterly.

**Host B:** Set it up.

**Host A:** Boaler, then at Stanford, ran a five year study of about seven hundred students at three California high schools. The names are pseudonyms. Railside, an urban, diverse school with many students from low income families and many learning English. Hilltop, a rural school. And Greendale, a coastal school with an almost entirely white student body. At Hilltop and Greendale, mathematics was taught in a traditional way. Lecture, demonstration, individual practice. At Railside, the mathematics department had developed its own approach.

**Host B:** Describe Railside.

**Host A:** Railside's teachers taught through group work, using an approach called complex instruction, developed by Elizabeth Cohen and Rachel Lotan at Stanford. Every incoming student was placed in the same algebra class. No remedial track. Lessons were ninety minutes long, and students could take two mathematics courses a year, which meant a student who failed a course could catch up. The curriculum was designed by the teachers, organised around big ideas, with problems designed for groups. And complex instruction had specific features. Group roles that rotated, like facilitator or resource manager. Tasks designed so that many different abilities were needed, not just speed at calculation. And a practice called assigning competence, where the teacher publicly points out the intellectual contribution of a student who usually has low status in the group.

**Host B:** Assigning competence.

**Host A:** For example, a teacher notices that a quiet student made a drawing that helped the group, and says to the group, Jamal's diagram is really useful, it shows exactly why the pattern grows. That student's status rises. Others start to listen to him.

**Host B:** And the time.

**Host A:** They measured how time was spent. At Railside, about seventy two percent of class time was group work and about four percent was lecture. In the traditional classes, about forty eight percent was individual practice and about twenty one percent lecture.

**Host B:** And the results?

**Host A:** At the start of the first year, Railside students scored significantly lower than the students in the traditional classes on the researchers' own algebra test. Sixteen out of a possible score, compared to about twenty two. By the end of the first year, the gap had nearly closed. By the end of the second year, Railside students were significantly outperforming the traditional students, about twenty six to eighteen. In the third year, Railside was still ahead, but the difference was no longer statistically significant.

**Host B:** And beyond test scores?

**Host A:** Seventy one percent of Railside students in the second year said they enjoyed mathematics class, compared to forty six percent in traditional classes. By senior year, forty one percent of Railside students were taking precalculus or calculus, compared to about twenty seven percent at the other schools. Gaps between ethnic groups narrowed. And Boaler and Staples summarised the principle in a line that has been quoted thousands of times. Put simply, when there are many ways to be successful, many more students are successful.

**Host B:** That is a lovely line.

**Host A:** It is a lovely line. And the study became, for a decade, the flagship example of equitable reform mathematics.

**Host B:** And then.

**Host A:** And then the problems. First, the state tests. On California's standardised tests, Railside did not look as good. Its scores were lower. Boaler and Staples addressed this in the paper itself, arguing that the state tests depended heavily on language, and that many Railside students were learning English. They reported a very high correlation, across the state, between language and mathematics scores, and argued that the tests were testing language as much as mathematics.

**Host B:** That is a possible explanation.

**Host A:** It is a possible explanation, and also, critics said, a convenient one. When the measure you designed shows success, and the state's measure does not, you have to be careful about which to believe.

**Host B:** And then the critics went further.

**Host A:** In 2012, three critics, the mathematician James Milgram of Stanford, the mathematician Wayne Bishop of California State University, Los Angeles, and the statistician Paul Clopton, circulated a paper challenging the Railside study in detail. They identified the real schools, used public data, and argued that the Railside results were not what the study claimed, and that the researchers' own tests were problematic. Boaler responded publicly and angrily, describing what she said was years of harassment by these critics, and defending the study. And it became one of the bitterest episodes of the math wars.

**Host B:** Who was right?

**Host A:** I do not think we can settle that here, and I want to be careful. What can be said fairly is this. The Railside study was not an experiment. The schools were chosen, not randomly assigned. The students at the three schools differed. The tests were designed by the researchers. State test results were not favourable to Railside. And the researchers were close to the Railside teachers, which they acknowledged. On the other side, the classroom observations were extensive, about six hundred hours, the changes in students' attitudes and course taking were large, and the teaching practices described are coherent and have been used successfully elsewhere. A cautious reader would say Railside is an inspiring case study that raises important hypotheses, not proof.

**Host B:** And Boaler herself said Railside was not perfect.

**Host A:** In the paper, they write, Railside is not a perfect place. The teachers would like to achieve more. And I think that is the right note.

**Host B:** Why does Railside matter for Sold a Story?

**Host A:** Because Boaler later became the most prominent public voice of reform mathematics in America, through her books, her online courses, and the California Mathematics Framework, which we discussed in episode one. And critics of reform, including people in the Sold a Story orbit, have pointed to Railside as an example of how reform mathematics oversells weak evidence. And defenders point to it as evidence that a different way of teaching can change which children succeed.

**Host B:** And Kennedy?

**Host A:** Kennedy would find Railside interesting for a different reason. Railside is not one teacher. It is a department. The teachers designed the curriculum together, planned together, and developed the approach over years. And her fifth problem, accommodating personal needs, is partly addressed by that. A teacher doing complex instruction alone is exhausted. A department doing it together shares the load. Some of the most persuasive responsive teaching in the research is collective, not individual.

**Host B:** Last paper. Rehearsals.

**Host A:** Magdalene Lampert again, now with a large team. Lampert, Franke, Kazemi, Ghousseini, Turrou, Beasley, Cunard and Crowe. Keeping It Complex: Using Rehearsals to Support Novice Teacher Learning of Ambitious Teaching. Journal of Teacher Education, 2013.

**Host B:** The question.

**Host A:** The question is how to teach new teachers to do this. Everything we have discussed is hard. Anticipating, noticing, probing, orchestrating. The traditional answer was to teach the theory in a university course and hope it transfers to practice. And it mostly did not. Kennedy's problem of enactment.

**Host B:** And the answer?

**Host A:** Rehearsal. Borrowed from the performing arts and from other professions. Novice teachers practise teaching a specific instructional activity, in front of their peers, with a teacher educator coaching them. And the teacher educator does something clever. She plays a student. She gives the kinds of answers real children give, including wrong ones and surprising ones, so that the novice has to respond in the moment. And she stops the rehearsal to give feedback, or to try a move again.

**Host B:** Like a dress rehearsal for a play.

**Host A:** Like a rehearsal for a play, or a flight simulator for a pilot. And they define the goal as preparing novices for ambitious teaching. In their words, teaching that aims to accomplish an ambitious vision of what learners should know and be able to do.

**Host B:** What do novices rehearse?

**Host A:** Not whole lessons. Specific, bounded instructional activities, which work as containers for practice. Choral counting, where the class counts together and the teacher records the count on the board in a way that reveals patterns. Quick images, where the teacher flashes a picture of dots and asks how many and how you saw them. Strings, a sequence of related computation problems. Strategy sharing. Launching a word problem.

**Host B:** Those are routines.

**Host A:** They are routines. And that is the point. A routine reduces the complexity of the situation, so the novice can concentrate on the hardest part, which is eliciting and responding to student thinking. In a sense, they are applying Engelmann's insight, that structure helps, to responsive teaching.

**Host B:** And the study?

**Host A:** They studied ninety rehearsals, thirty at each of three universities. UCLA, Michigan and the University of Washington. And they coded more than twelve hundred exchanges between teacher educators and novices.

**Host B:** What did they find?

**Host A:** Rehearsals lasted on average twelve to fifteen minutes, with about fourteen exchanges per rehearsal. So the teacher educator was stepping in roughly once a minute. Most of the feedback was directive. About sixty one percent of exchanges involved the teacher educator suggesting or requesting a specific next move. Try asking him how he knew. Write that on the board. Evaluative feedback, praise or critique, came up in about twenty eight percent. And about twenty one percent involved the teacher educator stepping into a role, playing a student or modelling the teacher move.

**Host B:** And what were they working on?

**Host A:** By far the most common focus was eliciting and responding to student ideas. They wrote that eliciting and responding was by far the most frequently occurring substance code across all exchanges. It came up in about ninety six percent of rehearsals. Then representation, how to record ideas on the board. Then student engagement.

**Host B:** And the title. Keeping it complex.

**Host A:** The title is a warning. When you simplify teaching for novices, there is a temptation to strip it down to techniques. Ask open questions. Wait three seconds. And then you lose the thing that makes it ambitious, which is the mathematical substance and the real children. Rehearsal tries to simplify the situation without simplifying the teaching. The activity is bounded. But inside it, the novice has to deal with real mathematical ideas, in real time.

**Host B:** And they note something about practice.

**Host A:** They define deliberate practice, drawing on research on expertise, as not just repeated doing but cycles of repetition with feedback. Which is something Engelmann would agree with completely. He was obsessive about practice and feedback. For children. And rehearsals apply that same principle to teachers.

**Host B:** Does it work?

**Host A:** The 2013 paper describes rehearsals in detail but does not measure their effect on novices' later teaching or on children's learning. There has been later work, and some evidence that teachers prepared this way do more eliciting and responding in their classrooms. But, again, the evidence on children's outcomes is thin.

**Host B:** Pull it together. Kennedy's lens.

**Host A:** I think orchestrating the discussion is where Kennedy's framework is most powerful. Because the whole class discussion is the moment where all five problems are live at once. Portraying the curriculum: the teacher has to get to the mathematical point. Enlisting participation: she wants many children involved, not just the confident ones. Exposing student thinking: that is the purpose of the discussion. Containing behaviour: a discussion is where a class is most likely to drift. And accommodating her own needs: running a discussion like this, every day, is exhausting.

**Host B:** And the five practices help with which?

**Host A:** The five practices mostly help with the first and third. Portraying the curriculum and exposing student thinking. By planning, they make it much more likely that the discussion reaches the mathematical goal. Kazemi and Stipek's press, and Franke's probing sequences, help with exposing thinking too. Complex instruction, at Railside, adds a serious answer to enlisting participation, especially for low status students. Rehearsals are about building the capacity to do all of it.

**Host B:** And behaviour and personal needs?

**Host A:** Behaviour and personal needs are mostly left to the teacher, again. Railside's department structure is the one place in today's papers where the teacher's own needs are addressed directly, by sharing the work. And that is a pattern we have seen through the whole series. The responsive tradition has thought very hard about mathematics and children's thinking, and much less about the teacher's energy and the room's order. Direct Instruction, by contrast, has thought very hard about those, and much less about children's own ideas.

**Host B:** Make the case, as its defenders would.

**Host A:** The case for orchestrated discussion is this. Responsive teaching does not have to mean show and tell. Researchers in this tradition identified show and tell as a failure, and developed practical tools to fix it. Sociomathematical norms that give discussion mathematical standards. High press for conceptual thinking. Probing sequences of questions. Five practices that move much of the hard thinking to before the lesson, so ordinary teachers can do it. Group work structures that give low status students a real voice. And rehearsal methods that can train new teachers in these skills. Together, they offer a way to teach children to reason mathematically, to confront their own errors, and to see how different methods connect, in whole classes, not just one on one.

**Host B:** And the case against, as its critics would put it.

**Host A:** The case against is this. Almost all of these studies are small, descriptive, or correlational. The five practices, the most widely used framework, is a conceptual paper, not an experiment. The most famous outcome study, Railside, was not randomised, relied on researcher designed tests, and was contradicted by state tests, and its findings remain contested. A single orchestrated discussion takes a whole lesson to reach a point that explicit instruction could make in minutes. And the very fact that researchers had to invent five practices to rescue discussions from show and tell suggests how often, in ordinary hands, responsive teaching fails.

**Host B:** And Kennedy's verdict?

**Host A:** Kennedy, I think, would say that the five practices are one of the most useful things the responsive tradition ever produced, precisely because they take her problems seriously. They do not pretend the teacher can improvise everything. They give her a structure. But she would also say that a framework that requires anticipating every task, monitoring every group, and selecting, sequencing and connecting in real time, every day, is still very demanding. And that whether ordinary teachers can sustain it, year after year, depends on the conditions they work in. Time to plan. Colleagues. Good materials. Which is a question about schools, not just about teachers.
