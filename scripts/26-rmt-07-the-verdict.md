# Responsive Math Teaching 7. The Verdict

**Host A:** Order in the court.

**Host B:** We do not have a court.

**Host A:** We have a podcast. Which is worse, because in a court the jury has to stay until the end.

**Host B:** Welcome to Books on Pods, and to the seventh and final part of our series on responsive mathematics teaching, made as a response to the second season of Sold a Story. Today, the verdict.

**Host A:** Six episodes. Something like three dozen papers. Two research traditions that have been arguing, on and off, since the Kennedy administration. And today we put them on trial. Thesis. Antithesis. And, if we can find one, synthesis.

**Host B:** And the judge?

**Host A:** The judge is the person we have been leaning on all series. Mary Kennedy. Not her opinions on Direct Instruction, which she mostly did not write about. Her framework. The five persistent problems of practice. Portraying the curriculum. Enlisting student participation. Exposing student thinking. Containing student behaviour. Accommodating the teacher's personal needs. Every method of teaching, she says, is a bundle of solutions to those five problems. So we are going to ask, of each side, which problems do you solve, which do you ignore, and what does it cost?

**Host B:** And I should say, to be fair, we are not going to put real people in the dock. We are putting arguments in the dock.

**Host A:** Arguments have thicker skin.

**Host B:** And the format?

**Host A:** I will make the case for explicit instruction first. As strongly as I can. As if I believed it with my whole heart. Then you make the case for responsive teaching, the same way. Then we cross examine. And then we try to find out what is left standing.

**Host B:** You are taking the side of Sold a Story.

**Host A:** For the next twenty minutes, I am Zig Engelmann's barrister. I have the wig. I have the script. I will say it once, in unison, and you will repeat it back to me.

**Host B:** Begin.

**Host A:** Members of the jury. The case for explicit instruction rests on four pillars. History, cognitive science, meta analysis, and justice.

**Host B:** Pillar one.

**Host A:** History. In 1967, the United States government launched the largest experiment in the history of education. Project Follow Through. Tens of thousands of children. Dozens of communities. Over twenty models of how to teach poor children. Every philosophy of education that America had to offer was entered into the competition. Open classrooms. Developmental approaches. Self esteem programmes. Behavioural approaches. And when the Abt evaluation compared them, one model came out on top, in basic skills, in what they called cognitive skills, and, to the great surprise of its critics, in self esteem. Direct Instruction.

**Host B:** The critics will say.

**Host A:** The critics will say what House and Glass and their colleagues said in 1978. That the effects varied wildly from site to site. That no approach was demonstrated to be better than the others. I respect the critics. But I ask the jury to notice something. In the decades since, the critics have never produced an alternative model with a record as strong. And when Jean Stockard and her colleagues gathered half a century of research on Direct Instruction in 2018, three hundred and twenty eight studies, the effects were consistently positive, across subjects, ages and settings. Variation from site to site is a fact about all teaching. Consistent positive effects across hundreds of studies is a fact about this one.

**Host B:** Pillar two.

**Host A:** Cognitive science. In 2006, Paul Kirschner, John Sweller and Richard Clark published a paper in Educational Psychologist with a title that does not mince words. Why Minimal Guidance During Instruction Does Not Work: An Analysis of the Failure of Constructivist, Discovery, Problem Based, Experiential, and Inquiry Based Teaching.

**Host B:** That is a lot of failures in one title.

**Host A:** It is a comprehensive title. And their argument is built from the architecture of the human mind. Start with their definition of learning. Learning, in turn, is defined as a change in long term memory. If nothing has changed in long term memory, nothing has been learned.

**Host B:** And how does something get into long term memory?

**Host A:** Through working memory. And working memory is tiny. In their words, the capacity of working memory is limited to only a very small number of elements. A handful of new things at a time. Now give a novice a complex problem and ask her to discover the solution. Her working memory fills up with the search. Trying this. Trying that. Holding the problem in mind. And there is nothing left over for learning. She may even solve the problem, by trial and error, and learn almost nothing about why the solution works.

**Host B:** And the alternative?

**Host A:** The worked example. Show the learner a fully worked solution, step by step, and let her study it. And here they make one of the strongest claims in the paper. For novices, studying worked examples seems invariably superior to discovering or constructing a solution to a problem.

**Host B:** Invariably.

**Host A:** Invariably. The worked example effect is one of the most replicated findings in educational psychology. And they also explain why experts can solve problems that novices can not. It is not that experts have better general problem solving skills. They write that expert problem solvers derive their skill by drawing on the extensive experience stored in their long term memory. Experts have seen thousands of problems. They recognise patterns. Their skill is knowledge.

**Host B:** So you can not teach someone to think like a mathematician by having them act like a mathematician.

**Host A:** That is their sharpest point. And here I turn to Magdalene Lampert, whom I deeply admire. Lampert argued that knowing mathematics means taking part in the practice of mathematicians. Conjecture. Argument. Revision. And Kirschner, Sweller and Clark have a sentence for exactly that argument. The epistemology of a discipline should not be confused with a pedagogy.

**Host B:** Say more.

**Host A:** How mathematicians come to know new things, by conjecture and refutation, is not necessarily how a ten year old should learn old things. A research mathematician is an expert. She has a vast store of knowledge in long term memory. That is what makes her conjectures good. A ten year old does not. Asking a novice to learn the way an expert discovers is asking her to do the most expensive thing with the least equipment.

**Host B:** And their conclusion?

**Host A:** About minimally guided instruction, they wrote, it appears that there is no body of research supporting the technique. Strong words. But the third pillar backs them up.

**Host B:** Pillar three.

**Host A:** Meta analysis. In 2011, Louis Alfieri and his colleagues published a meta analysis in the Journal of Educational Psychology. Does Discovery Based Instruction Enhance Learning? They gathered a hundred and eight studies, five hundred and eighty comparisons, of unassisted discovery against explicit instruction. And the result was an effect size of minus nought point three eight. In favour of explicit instruction.

**Host B:** That is substantial.

**Host A:** That is substantial. In their words, the findings suggest that unassisted discovery does not benefit learners. And the National Mathematics Advisory Panel, convened by the federal government in 2008, reviewed the research on students who struggle with mathematics and found consistent support for explicit methods of instruction for students with learning difficulties. There have also been large studies using national data on young children, which have found that teacher directed practice is associated with better outcomes for first graders who struggle in mathematics, while some student centred activities are not.

**Host B:** Pillar four.

**Host A:** Justice. And this is the one I care most about. In 1988, the educator Lisa Delpit published an essay in the Harvard Educational Review called The Silenced Dialogue. She was writing as a Black teacher, about the progressive, process oriented methods that were fashionable among mostly white reformers. And she argued that those methods could fail Black children and poor children, because they withheld the explicit rules that middle class children absorbed at home. She wrote a sentence that I think should be engraved over the door of every school of education. If you are not already a participant in the culture of power, being told explicitly the rules of that culture makes acquiring power easier.

**Host B:** Delpit was writing about writing, not mathematics.

**Host A:** She was writing about literacy. But the principle travels. The child of two engineers will discover the scale factor in the caterpillar problem, or have it explained over dinner. The child whose parents work two jobs, or do not speak English, depends on the school to tell her. A pedagogy that refuses to tell, in the name of respecting her thinking, may leave her exactly where she started. And then we will say the method worked for the children who already had it, which is to say, it did not work.

**Host B:** And Follow Through was about poor children.

**Host A:** Follow Through was entirely about poor children. That was its whole purpose. And in that test, the method that told them explicitly, that drilled the basics, that left nothing to chance, did best. Engelmann used to say that if the children have not learned, the teacher has not taught. It is a hard saying. But it puts the responsibility where it belongs.

**Host B:** Anything else?

**Host A:** One more thing. Kennedy. I claim her for my side.

**Host B:** That is bold.

**Host A:** Look at her five problems. Direct Instruction solves four of them. Portraying the curriculum: the program does it, with carefully sequenced examples, tested in the field. Enlisting participation: every child responds, in unison, dozens of times a lesson. Nobody hides. Containing behaviour: the pace is brisk, the routines are tight, there is no dead time for mischief. And accommodating the teacher's needs: she does not have to invent every lesson from scratch at nine at night. The script carries her. Only one problem, exposing student thinking, is handled in a narrow way, through error detection. And I say that is a price worth paying for the other four. The prosecution rests.

**Host B:** The defence is impressed.

**Host A:** The defence should be.

**Host B:** Members of the jury. I am going to start by agreeing with my learned friend.

**Host A:** That is a dangerous way to begin.

**Host B:** It is an honest way to begin. Unassisted discovery does not work well. Leaving novices alone with a hard problem and no help, then walking away, is bad teaching. And if that is what responsive teaching meant, I would not defend it. But it is not. And the first thing I want the jury to notice is that almost nobody in this series advocated it.

**Host A:** Go on.

**Host B:** Look at who we have actually discussed. Carpenter and Fennema, in Cognitively Guided Instruction, gave teachers a detailed research map of children's thinking. Lampert chose every problem with enormous care and spent months building norms. Ball knew exactly where the mathematics was going, even while she let Sean talk. Annie Keith made a deliberate teaching move every few seconds. Stein and her colleagues wrote five practices precisely because unstructured sharing fails. None of that is minimal guidance. It is a different kind of guidance.

**Host A:** Kirschner, Sweller and Clark would say it is still too little.

**Host B:** And they were answered. In 2007, in the same journal, Cindy Hmelo-Silver, Ravit Golan Duncan and Clark Chinn published a response. Scaffolding and Achievement in Problem Based and Inquiry Learning. And their central point was that problem based and inquiry learning, as actually practised and studied, are not minimally guided at all. They are heavily scaffolded. Guidance is provided, sometimes in advance, sometimes just in time, when a student needs it. And they pointed to studies showing that well scaffolded inquiry produces learning, including in areas where explicit instruction has struggled.

**Host A:** And Kirschner and his colleagues replied that those studies were not well controlled.

**Host B:** They did. And that is fair. Many of the inquiry studies compare different curricula, in different schools, with different teachers. The evidence is messier. But now look at the meta analysis my friend cited. Alfieri and colleagues. He gave you the first number. Minus nought point three eight, unassisted discovery against explicit instruction. He did not give you the second number.

**Host A:** I was getting to it.

**Host B:** You were not. The same paper did a second meta analysis. Fifty six studies, three hundred and sixty comparisons. Enhanced discovery, meaning discovery with guidance, feedback, worked examples, or asking learners to explain, against other forms of instruction, including explicit instruction. And the effect was plus nought point three. In favour of enhanced discovery. Guided discovery alone came in at about plus nought point five. Asking learners to generate explanations, about plus nought point three six.

**Host A:** So the same paper says unassisted discovery loses to explicit instruction, and guided discovery beats it.

**Host B:** The same paper. Their words. Although direct teaching is better than unassisted discovery, the construction of explanations or participation in guided discovery is better for learners. So the real question was never discovery versus explicit instruction. It was guided versus unguided. And on that question, everyone in this series is on the same side.

**Host A:** That is a good point.

**Host B:** I have more. Pillar two of my case. Timing. In 1998, Daniel Schwartz and John Bransford published a paper in Cognition and Instruction called A Time for Telling. And I love the title, because it concedes my friend's point before he makes it. There is a time for telling. Lectures, explanations, explicit instruction, work. The question is when.

**Host A:** And when is it?

**Host B:** They ran a series of experiments with college students learning about memory. Some students first analysed contrasting cases, sets of data from classic memory experiments, looking for patterns and differences, before hearing a lecture. Others read and summarised a text on the same material before the same lecture. And then, a week later, all of them were asked to predict the results of a new experiment.

**Host A:** And?

**Host B:** The students who analysed the cases and then heard the lecture did best. Better than those who summarised and then heard the lecture. And better than those who analysed the cases twice, with no lecture at all. The lecture helped, but only fully when students had been prepared for it. The authors talk about a readiness for being told. Analysing the cases built what they called differentiated knowledge. The students noticed differences they could not yet explain, and the lecture then explained them. In their words, deep understanding requires both differentiated knowledge about phenomena and an explanation of what those differences mean.

**Host A:** So exploring and then telling.

**Host B:** Exploring, then telling. The discovery alone was not enough. The telling alone was not enough. Together, they were better than either. And Manu Kapur, in Singapore and later in Zurich, built a whole research programme on that sequence. He called it productive failure.

**Host A:** Failure is not usually a selling point.

**Host B:** It is a terrible brand name and a very interesting idea. Students first work on a problem that is designed to be just beyond them. A problem they can understand but not fully solve with what they know. They generate many attempts, mostly wrong. They fail. And then the teacher gives explicit instruction on the canonical solution, and, crucially, compares it with the students' failed attempts. Why did your method not work? What does the correct method do that yours did not?

**Host A:** That sounds like the five practices.

**Host B:** It sounds very like the five practices, ending in connecting. And in 2021, Tanmay Sinha and Kapur published a meta analysis in the Review of Educational Research. Fifty three studies, a hundred and sixty six comparisons, of problem solving followed by instruction against instruction followed by problem solving.

**Host A:** And?

**Host B:** Overall, a moderate advantage for problem solving first, roughly a third of a standard deviation, and larger when the design followed productive failure principles closely. Mostly on conceptual understanding and transfer, not on procedural skill. And with an important limit. It worked better for secondary and university students than for primary school children, who may not have enough prior knowledge to make meaningful attempts. Kapur himself says the approach requires a certain amount of prior knowledge.

**Host A:** Which is the expertise reversal effect, from my side.

**Host B:** We will get to that. Pillar three. What the outcome is. My friend measured success by tests of basic skills and procedures. And on those, explicit instruction does very well. But the Wisconsin experiment in episode three found that children in Cognitively Guided Instruction classrooms did better on problem solving, and, to everyone's surprise, as well or better on number facts, though there was no drill. The four year study found gains in concepts and problem solving, though not in computation. And Alfieri, Schwartz, Kapur, all find that the advantages of guided exploration show up mostly in understanding and transfer. Applying knowledge to new situations.

**Host A:** And the tests that decide children's futures are mostly about procedures.

**Host B:** Which is a real problem for my side, and I concede it. But it is also a question about what we want. Lampert's point in episode four was that mathematics is not only procedures. It is also reasoning. If you measure only procedures, you will find that the method optimised for procedures wins. That is not nothing. But it is not everything.

**Host A:** Pillar four?

**Host B:** Justice. My friend quoted Lisa Delpit, and he was right to. But equity has more than one face. The mathematics education researcher Rochelle Gutiérrez has argued that equity has at least four dimensions. Access, meaning who gets the resources and opportunities. Achievement, meaning who succeeds on the measures. And then two more that are often ignored. Identity. Do children see themselves as people who do mathematics? Can they bring who they are into the classroom? And power. Who gets to decide what counts as mathematics, and who gets a voice in the classroom?

**Host A:** And explicit instruction does well on achievement.

**Host B:** Explicit instruction can do very well on achievement, and that matters enormously. But a pedagogy in which children only ever repeat back what the teacher said may do less for identity and power. And Niral Shah, among others, has documented how racial stereotypes about who is good at mathematics shape what happens in classrooms, which children's ideas get heard, who gets called smart. Railside, whatever its contested numbers, tried to change that. Complex instruction, assigning competence, multiple ways to be smart. And whatever you think of the test results, the course taking numbers moved, and the students' sense of themselves as mathematical people moved.

**Host A:** And Delpit?

**Host B:** Delpit's argument is not that children should never think for themselves. Her argument is that teachers should not withhold what children need to know in the name of a process. I agree completely. Responsive teaching, done well, does not withhold. In the caterpillar lesson, the class did confront the additive error. They did get to thirty. The teacher made sure. The difference is that Marcus got there by revising his own thinking, rather than by being corrected.

**Host A:** And Kennedy?

**Host B:** I claim her too.

**Host A:** Of course you do.

**Host B:** Look at what Kennedy found in her study of forty five teachers. No teacher, she wrote, indicated a specific intention to ensure that the ideas they taught were inherently important. And no teacher mentioned intellectual engagement as an important intention. Teachers were overwhelmingly focused on getting through, keeping order, covering the material. A tradition that keeps asking, what is the important idea here, and what is this child thinking about it, is pushing against exactly the pull that Kennedy documented. It is adding something teachers were not doing.

**Host A:** Is that it?

**Host B:** One last thing. Follow Through itself. My friend dismissed the variation. But I think the variation is the most important finding of the whole experiment. House and his colleagues wrote that the effectiveness of a teaching approach varies greatly from one school district to the next. Models that worked well in one town worked poorly in another. If the same method produces great results in one place and poor results in another, then the method is not the whole story. The teacher, the school, the conditions, matter enormously. And a policy that focuses only on choosing the right method will keep being surprised. The defence rests.

**Host A:** The court will take a short recess.

**Host B:** We do not have a court.

**Host A:** The podcast will take a short recess.

**Host B:** Cross examination. You first.

**Host A:** Thank you. My first question for the defence. You said that responsive teaching, done well, is heavily guided. Annie Keith. Lampert. Ball. How many teachers in America can teach like Annie Keith?

**Host B:** Not many. Today.

**Host A:** Your own witnesses, Jacobs, Lamb and Philipp, found that three percent of experienced teachers could choose a next problem based on what a child understood. Three percent. After four years of professional development, about two thirds. How many American teachers get four years of focused professional development?

**Host B:** Very few.

**Host A:** So in practice, for most children, responsive teaching means teaching by teachers who have not been prepared to do it. Which means, as your own researchers documented, show and tell. Low press. Mrs. Oublier. Is that not right?

**Host B:** It is often right. And I think it is the strongest argument against my side. Not that responsive teaching does not work when done well, but that it is very hard to do well, and the system does not prepare teachers for it.

**Host A:** Second question. The 2016 federal trial. Teachers given intensive mathematical professional development. Their knowledge went up. Their students' learning?

**Host B:** Did not change measurably.

**Host A:** So even when we do invest in teachers, it does not reliably reach children.

**Host B:** That trial invested in knowledge. Not in practice. Not in noticing. Not in curriculum materials. Kennedy's review says programmes that just give teachers a body of knowledge tend to be weak. So the trial tells us that knowledge alone is not enough. It does not tell us that nothing works.

**Host A:** But you have no trial of the things you think work.

**Host B:** Fewer than I would like. Some. The 1989 CGI experiment was randomised, at the teacher level. It was small. But it was real.

**Host A:** Forty teachers. Against Follow Through's tens of thousands of children.

**Host B:** And Follow Through's results varied so much from site to site that its own independent reviewers said no approach was shown to be better. Big is not the same as clear.

**Host A:** Third question. Your favourite school, Railside. Did it beat the comparison schools on the state tests?

**Host B:** No. And the researchers argued that the state tests measured language as much as mathematics. And their critics argued that was an excuse. I do not think Railside settles anything. I think it is an inspiring case with contested evidence.

**Host A:** I have no further questions.

**Host B:** My turn.

**Host A:** I am ready.

**Host B:** First question. Kirschner, Sweller and Clark say that for novices, worked examples are invariably superior. What happens when the learner is not a novice?

**Host A:** The expertise reversal effect. As learners become more knowledgeable, the advantage of worked examples shrinks, and can reverse. For learners who already know a lot, solving problems can be better than studying worked examples, because the worked example is redundant and wastes their attention.

**Host B:** So explicit instruction is best for novices, and less so for learners with more knowledge.

**Host A:** That is what the research says, yes.

**Host B:** Then, by your own theory, the question is not whether to guide, but how much, for whom, and when. And as a child learns, the guidance should fade, and the child should do more of the thinking. Which is what responsive teaching tries to do.

**Host A:** Fading guidance is part of explicit instruction too. I do, we do, you do.

**Host B:** Yes. But you do is usually more of the same problem. Responsive teaching asks the child to do something different, to explain, to compare, to invent. Second question. You said the epistemology of a discipline should not be confused with a pedagogy. Is learning to reason mathematically part of the curriculum, or not?

**Host A:** It is. But it comes after knowledge.

**Host B:** When?

**Host A:** When the child has enough knowledge in long term memory to reason with.

**Host B:** Arthur was ten. He had enough to reason about the last digits of seven to the fourth. Sean was eight. He had enough to find singly even numbers. If reasoning waits until children are experts, most children will never reason at all, because most children never become experts in mathematics. Should they never conjecture?

**Host A:** I think a Direct Instruction designer would say that conjecture is a luxury. And I think Lampert's classroom would make them uncomfortable. But I concede that the children in it were doing something real.

**Host B:** Third question. Follow Through. Did Direct Instruction work equally well everywhere?

**Host A:** No. Its effects varied from site to site too.

**Host B:** So even for the method that did best, the place mattered. The teachers mattered. The implementation mattered.

**Host A:** Yes. Which is why Direct Instruction puts so much effort into training, supervision and fidelity. To reduce that variation.

**Host B:** And so both sides end up at the same place. The teacher matters. You try to make the teacher matter less, by writing more into the program. We try to make the teacher better, by developing their knowledge and judgement. And neither of us has fully succeeded.

**Host A:** That is a fair summary of the last sixty years.

**Host B:** Synthesis.

**Host A:** Synthesis. Let me start with the panel.

**Host B:** The National Mathematics Advisory Panel, 2008.

**Host A:** Which Sold a Story's camp often cites, and fairly, for its recommendations on explicit instruction for struggling students, and on fluency with number facts. But the panel also wrote a sentence that both sides tend to skip. All encompassing recommendations that instruction should be entirely student centred or teacher directed are not supported by research.

**Host B:** Entirely is the key word.

**Host A:** Entirely is the key word. The panel found that the research did not support either extreme. And it recommended that instruction develop conceptual understanding, computational fluency and problem solving together. Not one, then the others. Together.

**Host B:** Which sounds like a compromise.

**Host A:** It sounds like a compromise, and compromises have a bad reputation. But I think the research of the last twenty years has given us something more precise than a compromise. It has given us some principles about when each kind of instruction helps.

**Host B:** Principle one.

**Host A:** Guidance should match the learner's knowledge. That is the expertise reversal effect, and it is also what CGI says, from the other side. Novices in a domain need more explicit support. Children who already have relevant knowledge can do more of the thinking. Remember that in CGI, the problems children could solve on their own were the ones where they already had informal knowledge, like joining and separating. A good teacher, in either tradition, adjusts.

**Host B:** Principle two.

**Host A:** Sequence matters. Telling is powerful when students are ready for it. Schwartz and Bransford's time for telling. Kapur's productive failure. Explore first, then explain. But not explore and never explain. In both of those research programmes, explicit instruction is essential. It just comes second. And for younger learners, or learners with little prior knowledge, the evidence for exploring first is weaker, so explaining first may be better.

**Host B:** Principle three.

**Host A:** Comparing methods is one of the most powerful moves in all of mathematics teaching, and it belongs to both traditions. Bethany Rittle-Johnson and Jon Star ran experiments in which seventh graders learning to solve equations either studied worked examples one at a time, or studied two worked examples side by side, solved in different ways, and compared them. The comparison group learned the procedures at least as well, and became more flexible, better at choosing efficient methods.

**Host B:** Worked examples, which is Sweller's side.

**Host A:** Worked examples, which is Sweller's side. Compared, which is connecting, Stein's fifth practice. And in the caterpillar lesson, the comparison between Priya's table and Marcus's additive answer was exactly that. Two methods side by side, and a question about why one works.

**Host B:** Principle four.

**Host A:** Practice is not the enemy of understanding. Fluency with number facts and procedures frees working memory for reasoning. Kirschner and Sweller are right about that. And the CGI research shows that understanding can support fluency too, as with the children who learned their facts without drill. The two support each other. A classroom with no practice is as badly designed as a classroom with only practice.

**Host B:** Principle five.

**Host A:** Structure is not the enemy of responsiveness. This is the one I find most interesting, because it is where both traditions have quietly moved toward each other. The responsive tradition invented the five practices, rehearsals, instructional activities, anticipated strategies in teachers' guides. Routines and plans. And good explicit instruction has always included checking for understanding, listening for errors, and adjusting. The question is not structure or no structure. It is what the structure is for.

**Host B:** And principle six.

**Host A:** The teacher's conditions matter. Every successful example in this series, on both sides, had support. Follow Through's Direct Instruction sites had trainers and supervisors. CGI teachers had workshops and colleagues. Lampert and Ball had a university behind them. Railside had a department that planned together. Annie Keith had decades of professional community. No method works in a vacuum. And when it is put into schools without support, every method degrades.

**Host B:** Now Kennedy. The verdict.

**Host A:** Kennedy as judge. Let us go through her five problems one more time, and ask what each tradition contributes.

**Host B:** Portraying the curriculum.

**Host A:** Explicit instruction is very strong here. Carefully sequenced examples, tested in classrooms, can portray procedures and facts with remarkable clarity. The responsive tradition broadens what the curriculum is. Lampert and Ball argue that reasoning, conjecturing and defining are part of mathematics, and the five practices help a discussion actually reach a mathematical point. Verdict: both contribute. Explicit instruction for clarity and coherence, responsive teaching for depth and connections.

**Host B:** Enlisting student participation.

**Host A:** Explicit instruction gets every child to respond, many times a lesson, which is a real achievement. Responsive teaching gets children to participate in a different way, by contributing ideas, and complex instruction specifically tries to bring in children who are usually left out. Verdict: different kinds of participation. Unison response guarantees everyone is active. Discussion lets children author ideas. A good classroom probably needs both.

**Host B:** Exposing student thinking.

**Host A:** This is the responsive tradition's home ground. CGI, noticing, probing questions, all designed to see what children actually think, not just whether they are right. Explicit instruction exposes thinking narrowly, as error detection. Verdict: responsive teaching, clearly. Though, as we saw, the skill is rare and takes years to develop.

**Host B:** Containing behaviour.

**Host A:** Explicit instruction is very strong here. Brisk pace, clear routines, little dead time. Responsive teaching has thought much less about this, and open discussions are harder to manage. Verdict: explicit instruction, clearly. And the responsive tradition should learn from it.

**Host B:** Accommodating the teacher's needs.

**Host A:** Explicit instruction, with a good program, lightens the teacher's planning load enormously. Responsive teaching, done seriously, is exhausting, unless it is done collectively, with good materials and colleagues. Verdict: explicit instruction, for the individual teacher. Responsive teaching only becomes sustainable as a collective effort.

**Host B:** So the score?

**Host A:** The score, if you insist on one, is that each tradition is strong where the other is weak. Explicit instruction solves the problems of order, efficiency and clarity. Responsive teaching solves the problem of seeing and building on children's thinking. And Kennedy's deepest point is that every teacher, every day, has to solve all five, at once. A method that solves four and ignores one leaves the teacher to solve the fifth alone. And that is true of both.

**Host B:** So which is the fifth problem left over?

**Host A:** For Direct Instruction, the one left over is children's own thinking. Sean, Arthur, Cassandra with her good strategy and wrong answer. For responsive teaching, the ones left over are behaviour and the teacher's energy. And I think Kennedy would say that the most useful thing either tradition could do now is to work seriously on the problem it has neglected.

**Host B:** And Sold a Story.

**Host A:** Let me try to be fair. What does the second season get right? It is right that explicit instruction has a strong evidence base, stronger than many reformers admit. It is right that unguided discovery does not work. It is right that ideology has sometimes outrun evidence in schools of education, and that some reform claims, about timed tests, about learning styles, about Railside, have been oversold. It is right that children who struggle, and children without support at home, are most hurt when teaching is vague. And it is right that teachers are too often left without the knowledge or materials they need.

**Host B:** And what does it miss?

**Host A:** As we described in episode one, I think it tells the Follow Through story more cleanly than the evidence allows. The variation from site to site was enormous, and it is a central finding, not a footnote. I think it risks treating responsive teaching as if it were unguided discovery, when the serious responsive tradition is something else, heavily guided, deeply structured in its best forms. I think it does not engage much with the question of what mathematics is, which Lampert and Ball put at the centre. And I think it underplays the evidence that thoughtfully guided exploration, followed by explicit instruction, can outperform explicit instruction alone, especially for understanding and transfer.

**Host B:** And what would you say to its listeners?

**Host A:** I would say, you are right to be suspicious of teaching that leaves children to flounder. Keep that suspicion. But do not let it become a suspicion of children's thinking itself. The best evidence does not say, never let children think before you tell them. It says, think carefully about when, and how, and for whom. And the teacher who can listen to Sean and know what to do is not a romantic ideal. She is a professional with skills that can be named, studied and taught. It just takes longer than any of us would like.

**Host B:** And to the responsive side?

**Host A:** I would say, take Sold a Story seriously. Your evidence is thinner than your rhetoric. Show and tell is real. Teachers drowning in a discussion they can not steer is real. Children leaving a lesson still believing the additive answer is real. The five practices, rehearsals, good curriculum materials, explicit consolidation after exploration, those are the responsive tradition at its best, and they all involve more structure, not less. Lean into that. And run more trials. If your approach works, measure it in a way that convinces people who do not already agree.

**Host B:** So, the verdict.

**Host A:** The verdict of this court, which is not a court, is this. The charge that responsive teaching does not work is not proven. The charge that it is very hard to do well, and is often done badly, is proven. The charge that explicit instruction is mindless drill is not proven. The charge that it neglects children's own thinking is proven, at least in its purest forms. And the sentence, for both sides, is to work on what they have neglected, and to stop pretending the other side has nothing to teach them.

**Host B:** A suspended sentence.

**Host A:** A suspended sentence, with community service. Which is to say, teaching.
